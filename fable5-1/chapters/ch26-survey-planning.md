# Chapter 26 — Survey planning for calibration, error reduction, and error monitoring

> **Part VI — Planning and operating surveys.** The first chapter of the planning part: how to design an acquisition so that the errors described in Parts II–V are small, observable during the survey, and provable afterwards, using the calibration infrastructure of [Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md).

**In this chapter.** A survey plan is an error budget written as a schedule. You will be able to turn a stated use into a governing specification and a list of acceptance tests; design the geometry of a lidar block or a multibeam survey (line spacing, overlap, crosslines, flying height or vessel speed, and their consequences for density and feature detection); lay out control, checkpoints, base stations, tide gauges, and calibration events so that systematic errors are observable rather than assumed away; choose environmental windows (leaf-off, snow-free, low turbidity, calm sea, low PDOP, quiet ionosphere); run a daily quality-control loop with explicit re-run triggers; decide early whether to survey to the ellipsoid; navigate airspace, marine-consent, and privacy constraints; and plan risk and cost with mobilization and weather as the dominant terms. Two worked designs — a 600 km² lidar block and a 40 km² multibeam survey — and a planning checklist close the chapter.

## 26.1 From requirement to specification

A survey begins with a use, not a sensor. [Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md) covered how a use becomes a requirement — a minimum detectable change, a flood-model slope tolerance, a charted least depth with a stated confidence. Planning begins where that chapter ended: the requirement must be translated into a **governing specification**, a named, versioned document whose acceptance tests the deliverables will be judged against. The choice is rarely free. Charting surveys will be judged against IHO **S-44** (Edition 6.1.0, 2022) as implemented by the hydrographic office — in the United States, NOAA's *Hydrographic Surveys Specifications and Deliverables* (HSSD, revised annually; the 2024 edition restructured the document) with the *Field Procedures Manual* (FPM). Topographic lidar for a U.S. federal program will be judged against the USGS *Lidar Base Specification* (LBS, 2024 rev. A at the time of writing) and the ASPRS *Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data* (Edition 2, 2023; version 2 with Addenda I–IV, 2024). Aerodrome terrain and obstacle data must satisfy ICAO Annex 15 and PANS-AIM; Australian and New Zealand tenders cite the ICSM *LiDAR Acquisition Specifications and Tender Template*; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers work cites EM 1110-2-1003 or EM 1110-1-1000. [Chapter 70](ch70-specifications-guided-tour.md) tours these documents; here the point is procedural.

Write the deliverables list and the **acceptance tests** before designing a single line. For a lidar block the tests will typically be: aggregate nominal pulse density (ANPD) and spacing by swath; the spatial-distribution test; intra- and inter-swath relative accuracy from overlap differences; non-vegetated vertical accuracy (**NVA**, RMSE$_z$ and 95 %) and vegetated vertical accuracy (**VVA**, 95th percentile) from independent checkpoints; void and classification checks; metadata completeness. For a multibeam survey: total vertical and horizontal uncertainty (**TVU**, **THU**) against the order's $a$ and $b$ coefficients; feature-detection and coverage demonstrations; crossline statistics; junctions with adjacent surveys; sound-speed and tide documentation; the Descriptive Report. Each test implies something you must collect: checkpoints imply a control survey, crossline statistics imply crosslines, relative accuracy implies overlap. A plan that cannot name the dataset that will satisfy each acceptance test is not a plan.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Accuracy" in a tender is meaningless until the specification names the statistic, the confidence level, the land-cover class, and the reference. "10 cm" may be RMSE$_z$ (ASPRS NVA 10 cm → 19.6 cm at 95 %), a 95 % value, a 1σ value, or a TVU at 95 % with depth-dependent terms. Reading the number without its definition is the most common cause of a failed acceptance that nobody saw coming; [Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md) gives the conversions.

Two further decisions belong here because they shape everything downstream: the **product datum and epoch** — ellipsoidal or orthometric, which geoid, which tidal datum, which frame realization and epoch ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md), [Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)) — and the **surface definition**: DSM, DTM, or both; whether bridges are terrain; how water is treated ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md), [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). These decide what control you need and what you will later remove.

## 26.2 Geometry

Geometry — where the sensor goes and how it looks — determines density, coverage, feature detectability, and the observability of calibration errors. The sensor chapters ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md), [Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md), [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) derive footprint and density; this section applies them to a plan.

### 26.2.1 Swath, line spacing, overlap, and crosslines

For a cross-track scanner at height $h$ above ground with full field of view $\theta_{\text{FOV}}$, swath width is $W = 2h\tan(\theta_{\text{FOV}}/2)$. For a multibeam the equivalent is $W = 2d\tan\theta_{\text{half}}$ with $d$ the depth below the transducer and $\theta_{\text{half}}$ the *usable* half-angle (set by outer-beam uncertainty, not the nominal sector); the swath shrinks as the water shoals, so spacing must be planned against the shallowest depth each line will meet. **Line spacing** follows from the required **sidelap** (lidar) or **overlap** (sonar): $S = W(1 - f)$ for fraction $f$. Practice is 20–30 % sidelap for lidar, which also allows clipping the highest-incidence edge of each scan; full-coverage multibeam is usually met with 10–25 % overlap at the shallowest depth, with HSSD defining coverage by the surface resolution achieved. For structure-from-motion the convention is 80 % forward and 70 % side overlap, higher (85/80 or more) over vegetation, water margins, and low-texture surfaces.

Overlap is not waste: it is the only part of the survey in which the same ground is measured twice from different geometry, and therefore the only part in which roll, pitch, heading, range-scale, and timing errors become visible without external control. **Crosslines** (hydrography) or **cross-strips** (lidar) extend this test across the whole block: run perpendicular to the main lines through the full range of depths or terrain, each crossing samples a main line at a different time, heading, and beam angle, so an error constant within a line — tide, sound-speed drift, a heading-dependent roll bias, a GNSS height offset on one day — appears as a crossing difference. Hydrographic offices generally require crosslines of a few per cent of main-scheme line kilometres (NOAA's HSSD: approximately 4 % with full bathymetric coverage, 8 % with less); the LBS requires cross-strips sufficient for calibration and relative-accuracy assessment, and 5–10 % is the working norm. Run them on different days and in different sea states from the lines they cross, and through the deepest and shallowest parts of the area, because depth-dependent errors only show where depth varies.

### 26.2.2 Flying height, speed, density, and feature detection

For a lidar with pulse rate $f_p$ (Hz), ground speed $v$ (m s$^{-1}$), and swath $W$ (m), single-swath nominal density is $\rho = f_p/(vW)$ points m$^{-2}$. Higher altitude widens the swath and lowers density, weakens the return and enlarges the footprint, and lengthens the lever arm through which attitude errors act: a 0.005° roll error at 1,500 m above ground at 20° off nadir displaces a point by about $1{,}500 \times \tan 20° \times 0.005° \times \pi/180° \approx 4.8$ cm. The LBS QL2 requirement of ≥ 2 points m$^{-2}$ is easily met by modern sensors; the real constraint is often the spatial-distribution test and the density under canopy. For a multibeam, along-track sounding spacing is $v/r$ at ping rate $r$, and $r$ falls with depth as the two-way travel time grows. A feature-detection requirement (S-44 Order 1a: 2 m cubes to 40 m depth) demands that along- and across-track spacing, after beam-footprint growth with range, still place several soundings on the feature; this frequently forces slower speed or a narrower sector in deeper water, and the plan must say which.

Photogrammetry adds illumination: plan for sun elevation above about 30° to limit shadows and below the angle that produces specular hot-spots on water and wet surfaces; at high latitude in winter the usable window may be two hours. Shallow-water surveys are planned to the tide: the shallowest lines on the highest water, beach-extending topobathy lidar on low water, crosslines at a different stage; in hydrography the tide also enters the error budget directly unless you survey to the ellipsoid (Section 26.7).

> **Worked example.** *Lidar block design.* Requirement: USGS QL2 (ANPD ≥ 2 pts m$^{-2}$, NVA ≤ 10 cm RMSE$_z$, VVA ≤ 30 cm at the 95th percentile) over a 20 km × 30 km block (600 km²) of farmland and deciduous woodland, leaf-off. Sensor: 500 kHz effective pulse rate, 40° field of view, flown at 1,500 m above ground at 60 m s$^{-1}$ (≈ 117 kn).
> Swath: $W = 2 \times 1{,}500 \times \tan 20° = 1{,}092$ m. Single-swath density: $500{,}000/(60 \times 1{,}092) = 7.6$ pts m$^{-2}$ — margin for canopy loss and edge clipping.
> Spacing at 30 % sidelap: $S = 0.7 \times 1{,}092 = 764$ m. Lines along the 30 km axis: $20{,}000/764 = 26.2$ → 27 intervals plus a half-swath margin at each edge → 28 lines × 30 km = 840 main-line km.
> Cross-strips: three across the 20 km axis (quarter, half, three-quarters) = 60 km, 7 % of main-line km; one passes over the calibration site.
> Flight time: 900 km at 60 m s$^{-1}$ ≈ 4.2 h on-line, plus ≈ 30 turns at 2.5 min = 1.3 h, plus transit — two 4-hour sorties with a 25 % weather margin, or three if cloud base forces a lower altitude on one day (which changes swath and spacing; have a second line plan ready).
> Control: one GNSS base at the block centre on a published mark (all baselines ≤ 18 km) and a second at a corner for redundancy; 30 NVA checkpoints on open, flat, hard ground spread across the block and 20 VVA points under the dominant canopy types, surveyed to ≤ 3 cm RMSE$_z$ (ASPRS requires checkpoints at least three times more accurate than the product tested, i.e. ≤ 3.3 cm). Boresight calibration flights over a building-rich site at the start and end of the project and after any sensor or aircraft change.

> **Worked example.** *Multibeam survey design.* Requirement: S-44 Order 1a, full seafloor search, 2 m cube detection, over a 10 km × 4 km coastal strip deepening roughly linearly from 10 m inshore to 40 m offshore. Vessel: 8 kn (4.1 m s$^{-1}$), usable sector ±60°, so $W = 3.46d$.
> Order 1a TVU at 95 %: $\sqrt{a^2 + (bd)^2}$ with $a = 0.5$ m, $b = 0.013$: 0.52 m at 10 m, $\sqrt{0.25 + 0.27} = 0.72$ m at 40 m. THU: 5 m + 5 % of depth = 7 m at 40 m.
> Lines parallel to the contours (along the 10 km axis), spacing for 25 % overlap at the shallow edge of each line: $S = 0.75 \times 3.46\,d_{\min} = 2.6\,d_{\min}$. By depth band: 10–15 m (1 km wide), $S \approx 26$ m → 39 lines; 15–25 m (1 km), $S \approx 39$ m → 26 lines; 25–40 m (2 km), $S \approx 65$ m → 31 lines. Total ≈ 96 lines × 10 km = 960 main-line km.
> Crosslines: 5 % → 48 km → 12 crosslines of 4 km across the full depth range, run on different days from the mains.
> Time: 1,008 km at 14.8 km h$^{-1}$ = 68 h on-line; with turns (a 4 km line takes 16 min, a turn 3–4 min) ≈ 85 h; at 10 productive hours per day ≈ 9 days, plus 1 day patch test and reference surface, 1 day tide-gauge installation and levelling, and a 30 % weather allowance → 15 days.
> Sound speed: full-depth casts at least every 4 hours and whenever the surface probe differs from the last cast by more than about 2 m s$^{-1}$; more often across the river plume at the north end. Tide: a project gauge levelled to tidal benchmarks, or GNSS-ellipsoid reduction with a separation model (Section 26.7). Feature detection: at 40 m depth the across-track footprint of a 1° beam at 60° is ≈ 2.8 m, so the 2 m-cube requirement is demonstrated over a known target and the sector narrowed to ±55° where the demonstration fails.

## 26.3 Control design

Control is the set of observations that tie the survey to the reference frame and later prove the result: the positioning base, the calibration observations, the checkpoints, and (for hydrography) the water levels.

### 26.3.1 Base stations and baselines

PPK or RTK positioning of the platform requires a reference station of known coordinates. Site it on a **published mark** with a documented position in the project frame (an NGS datasheet, a CORS, a national pillar) or establish one with a multi-hour static occupation processed against CORS (OPUS in the United States) and a second independent occupation on another day. The base coordinate's error propagates one-for-one into every point of the survey; it is the single largest avoidable systematic error in airborne work. Keep baselines short: ambiguity resolution degrades with distance as tropospheric and ionospheric decorrelation grows, and 20–30 km is a common upper limit for centimetre-level PPK in quiet conditions, less at high latitude or during geomagnetic storms. For larger blocks use two or more bases (also redundancy if one logs nothing) or a network (VRS) solution; PPP with ambiguity resolution ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) is a base-free alternative with convergence and frame-epoch considerations of its own. Log at ≥ 1 Hz with a 10° mask, on a tripod or pillar with the antenna height to the antenna reference point recorded and the ANTEX model named.

### 26.3.2 Checkpoints and ground control

Distinguish **ground control points (GCPs)**, used in the solution (bundle or strip adjustment, bias removal), from **checkpoints**, withheld and used only to test; a point used in the adjustment cannot be a checkpoint. Lidar adjusted by boresight calibration and strip adjustment needs few GCPs but needs checkpoints; SfM needs GCPs for datum and scale and separate checkpoints to report accuracy. James et al. (2017) showed for UAV SfM that GCP precision and distribution — edges and interior, not one cluster — matter more than raw count.

Counts follow from the statistics to be reported. ASPRS Edition 2 tabulates minimum checkpoint counts by project area; the long-standing NDEP/ASPRS guideline of at least 20 per land-cover class, and many programs' preference for 30, reflect the fact that a 95th percentile from 20 points is decided by one point and that RMSE confidence intervals narrow only as $1/\sqrt{n}$ ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)). Distribution matters as much: stratify by land cover and slope, spread across the block so that every flight line and day is sampled, and resist putting all the points along the access road. NVA points go on open, flat, hard, unambiguous ground; VVA points go under canopy, surveyed by total station from GNSS-controlled open points because GNSS under trees is itself uncertain. Record the method, occupations, processing, estimated accuracy, and a photograph for every point; ASPRS Addendum II is the practical reference. Some checkpoints should be **independent in provenance** — surveyed by a different crew and not revealed to the acquisition contractor until after delivery. National control, ICESat-2 ATL06/ATL08 terrain heights, and prior lidar co-registered on stable terrain (Nuth & Kääb 2011) are free supplements ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)), not substitutes.

### 26.3.3 Calibration events, benchmarks, and water levels

Every **mobilization** — a sensor installed on a platform — is a new calibration state. Plan, at minimum: a boresight and lever-arm calibration flight (lidar: a building-rich site flown in opposing directions and at two altitudes; camera: a target field with self-calibration), a **patch test** for every MBES installation (roll, pitch, heading, latency, over a slope and a distinct feature, in water deep enough for the geometry to be observable — a flat featureless seabed cannot observe heading or latency; Hughes Clarke 2003), a bar or lead-line check for single-beam, and a **reference surface** surveyed densely in several directions at the start, after any equipment change, and at the end, whose repeatability is the survey's own precision estimate. Repeat after any transducer, antenna, IMU, or mount change, after a hard grounding or heavy landing, and monthly on long projects regardless.

Surveys reduced to a tidal datum need water-level observations with uncertainty an order of magnitude below the TVU. Where the nearest operating gauge is too far or hydrodynamically different (a river mouth, a shelf edge), install a project gauge before the survey, level it to at least three tidal benchmarks with closed loops, and run it long enough to establish datums by simultaneous comparison with a control station (a month is a common minimum). Zoning between gauges can introduce decimetre errors in complex coasts; ERS (Section 26.7) removes most of it.

## 26.4 Environmental windows

The environment is a parameter of the measurement, and the plan should state the windows within which the data will meet specification ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md) treats the physics). **Leaf-off:** deciduous canopy cuts lidar ground returns by factors of two to ten, biasing DTMs upward and leaving voids; define leaf-off by phenology verified with photographs or satellite NDVI at flight time, not by calendar month, and remember that a wet winter brings saturated ground, standing water in fields, and low sun. **Snow-free:** a 30 cm snowpack is a 30 cm DTM error that validates perfectly against checkpoints surveyed on the snow. **Low flow and low turbidity:** bathymetric lidar penetration scales with clarity (commonly about 1.5–3 Secchi depths depending on sensor and bottom; [Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)); runoff, blooms, and dredging schedules belong in the plan. **Sea state:** beyond the motion sensor's range come bubble sweep-down, transducer aeration, and loss of outer beams that editing cannot repair; state the limit (around 1–1.5 m significant wave height for small launches, depending on hull and heading) and the stand-down rule. **GNSS geometry and ionosphere:** plan windows for PDOP below about 3 using almanac tools with a terrain mask, and avoid hours around forecast geomagnetic storms (Kp ≥ 5) when ambiguity fixing on long baselines fails; scintillation near the equator and at auroral latitudes is worst after local sunset. **Weather:** cloud base below flying height, precipitation (noise for topographic lidar, a stop for bathymetric lidar), wind above the drone's limit, fog on the water. Climatology for the site and month should set the contingency in Section 26.9, and the weather actually encountered goes into the metadata. The cheapest error-reduction decision in a survey is the choice of month; surveys scheduled by fiscal year rather than by leaf-off and low-flow calendars routinely carry a bias no processing removes.

## 26.5 Operational QC

Operational quality control exists to find out *during* the survey, while platform and crew are on site, whether the data will pass the acceptance tests. A re-flight discovered the next morning costs a sortie; discovered after demobilization it costs a mobilization and often a season.

A daily loop for lidar: verify the trajectory (forward/backward separation of the GNSS–INS solution, satellites, PDOP, ambiguity-fix status along every line; separation above 5–10 cm for more than a few seconds flags the segment); check coverage against the plan (gaps at turns, cloud shadows as voids); compute density and the spatial-distribution test per swath; compute **overlap residuals** — vertical differences between adjacent swaths on flat hard surfaces — and read their signature (a constant offset implies height or lever-arm error; a difference growing toward the swath edge implies roll or range scale; one that changes sign with flight direction implies pitch or timing); compare cross-strips to main lines. Record the numbers against thresholds from the specification (LBS inter-swath RMSD$_z$ ≤ 8 cm for QL2; many projects set an internal 5 cm trigger). A daily loop for multibeam: review motion, heave, and positioning flags; confirm the sound-speed profile applied to each line and the surface-probe agreement; examine crossline differences by beam angle and depth, not one RMS; look for refraction "smiles" at line edges; verify tide application and gauge status; check coverage holidays against the required resolution. A change in crossline statistics from one day to the next means something moved — a mount, an antenna cable, a fouled sound-speed probe.

State **re-flight and re-run triggers** in advance so the decision is not negotiated under schedule pressure: trajectory separation above threshold for more than a stated duration; density or distribution below specification over more than a stated area; overlap or crossline differences beyond threshold after calibration; voids above a stated size not attributable to water; precipitation or cloud on the line; sea state beyond the limit; a calibration residual change after an incident. Field logs — weather, personnel, serial numbers, incidents, casts, antenna heights, gauge checks — are the only way to diagnose a systematic error found months later, and are required deliverables under HSSD and LBS.

> **Try it.** *Overlap residuals from two lidar swaths with PDAL.* Build a 1 m ground-only DEM from each of two adjacent strips and difference them; a systematic offset or a cross-track trend is the calibration diagnostic.
>
> ```bash
> for s in strip_017 strip_018; do
>   pdal translate "$s.laz" "${s}_dem.tif" range gdal \
>     --filters.range.limits="Classification[2:2]" \
>     --writers.gdal.resolution=1.0 --writers.gdal.output_type=mean \
>     --writers.gdal.data_type=float32
> done
> gdal_calc.py -A strip_017_dem.tif -B strip_018_dem.tif --outfile=diff.tif \
>   --calc="A-B" --NoDataValue=-9999
> gdalinfo -stats diff.tif | grep -E "MINIMUM|MAXIMUM|MEAN|STDDEV"
> ```
>
> Expected outcome on a well-calibrated project: mean within ±2–3 cm and σ of a few centimetres on hard flat ground. A mean of 8 cm, or a σ that doubles toward one swath edge, is a trigger for recalibration before the next sortie.

## 26.6 Design for validation

Validation designed into acquisition is cheap; bolted on afterwards it is expensive and often impossible. **Reserve independent checks before collecting.** Decide at planning time which checkpoints will be withheld from any adjustment, who will survey them, and that they will not be used to "fix" the data. If the data must be adjusted to control, use a separate GCP set and report both the adjustment residuals and the untouched checkpoint statistics; a report in which the same points appear in both roles describes only the adjustment's ability to fit itself. Where feasible, hold out a flight line or a survey day for a repeatability test.

**Plan what you will later remove.** Every product removes something from the observations: the water surface and column from bathymetry, the vessel's draft and squat, canopy and buildings from a DTM, cars from a parking lot ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)), tide from a sounding. Each removal has an uncertainty that can be measured in the field at the time — draft and squat trials at survey speed, a waterline measurement at each fuel state, ground truth under canopy, timestamped photographs of the parking lot. The removal you did not plan to measure becomes the residual you cannot explain. **Collect evidence of the environment:** Secchi or turbidity readings during bathymetric lidar, snow and leaf-state photographs, sea state, the casts with times and positions. These cost minutes and become indispensable when a reviewer asks why the north-east quadrant sits 15 cm high.

> **Rule of thumb.** Allocate 5–10 % of the acquisition budget to independent validation (checkpoints, crosslines, reference surfaces, repeat lines). Below about 3 % the accuracy statement is a statement of hope; above 15 % the money is usually better spent on more overlap or a second base. The range is empirical, from conventional airborne and vessel program budgets; satellite products spend a larger fraction because acquisition is sunk cost.

## 26.7 Surveying to the ellipsoid and reducing later

**Ellipsoidally referenced surveying (ERS)** positions the sensor directly in ellipsoidal height $h$ with GNSS–INS and reduces to the product datum afterwards with a separation model — $N$ for a geoid, or a chart-datum-to-ellipsoid surface such as NOAA's VDatum — rather than measuring relative to the water surface and applying tide, heave, draft, and settlement in the field. In hydrography the gain is large: tide zoning errors, gauge failures, and dynamic-draft uncertainties, which together can approach the full Order 1a allowance in shallow water, are replaced by GNSS height uncertainty (a few centimetres with PPK on short baselines) and the separation model's uncertainty, which is well characterised near well-gauged coasts and poorly characterised in remote areas. In topography ERS is the default — lidar is positioned in $h$ and converted to $H = h - N$ with a named geoid — and the decisions are which geoid, whether to deliver $h$ as well (always yes), and how to document model and epoch ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)).

ERS is a planning decision because it changes what you collect. Water levels are still observed, but as a check and for validating the separation model rather than as the primary reduction; GNSS must be continuous and high quality, because a one-minute outage under a bridge is a one-minute hole in the height reference that heave and tide would have bridged; the antenna-to-transducer lever arm must be known to the centimetre and the INS must carry height through attitude; and the plan needs a rule for where the separation model's stated uncertainty rises to decimetres — there a project gauge is still required. HSSD and FPM allow either path where its uncertainty budget is demonstrated; report the budget for whichever you choose.

## 26.8 Permits and constraints

Regulatory constraints shape geometry and schedule and must be resolved before mobilization. **Airspace:** in the United States, small-UAS operations fall under 14 CFR Part 107 (visual line of sight, ≤ 400 ft above ground or structure, waivers for night and beyond-visual-line-of-sight, LAANC authorization in controlled airspace); crewed survey flights need air-traffic coordination and often a NOTAM for low-level grids, and are constrained near airports, over refuges, and under temporary flight restrictions; EASA's open/specific categories and national authorities apply elsewhere, and foreign-registered survey aircraft commonly need diplomatic clearance. **Marine:** hydrographic surveys in a foreign EEZ or territorial sea are **marine scientific research** under UNCLOS Part XIII and require the coastal state's consent, typically sought through diplomatic channels six months ahead; port and waterway work needs harbour-master coordination; protected areas may prohibit anchoring a gauge or restrict sonar; some states restrict publication of high-resolution bathymetry ([Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)). **Land:** access permission for checkpoints and bases; archaeological and indigenous-land protocols; privacy obligations for imagery and for point clouds dense enough to show people ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)). Build the permit timeline into the schedule; a consent that arrives after leaf-on is a season lost.

## 26.9 Risk and cost planning

The cost of a survey is dominated by **mobilization** — a calibrated platform, crew, and sensor at the site — and by platform hours, not by line length ([Chapter 28](ch28-reducing-cost.md)). Two consequences follow. Incremental overlap, crosslines, and calibration time are cheap once mobilized; cutting them saves a small fraction of cost and removes most of the evidence of quality. And the dominant risk is schedule: weather, equipment failure, permit delay, the closing of an environmental window. Derive weather contingency from climatology for the site and month, not a flat percentage — a coastal multibeam survey in a temperate winter may need 40–50 %, a desert lidar block in spring 10 % — and state the rules for stand-down and for abandoning the window (if leaf-on arrives first, is partial delivery acceptable or does the block wait a year?).

Plan **re-survey economics** explicitly. If a systematic error is found after demobilization, what does a return cost? If the answer is prohibitive, tighten the QC thresholds of Section 26.5 and make calibration events more frequent, because the plan cannot rely on a second chance. Share mobilizations and checkpoint surveys with neighbouring programs where schedules align. Carry spares for single points of failure — a base receiver, a sound-speed probe, an IMU cable — and know their lead times.

> **Uncertainty budget.** *What the plan is buying.* Indicative 1σ contributions to the vertical uncertainty of a 1,500 m AGL lidar DTM point on open flat ground, and the planning lever that controls each (derivations in [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)).
>
> | Component | Typical 1σ | Planning lever |
> |---|---|---|
> | GNSS trajectory height (PPK, ≤ 20 km baseline, quiet ionosphere) | 3–5 cm | Base siting, baseline length, PDOP/Kp window, second base |
> | INS attitude at swath edge | 1–3 cm | Flying height, FOV clipping, boresight per mobilization |
> | Range and timing | 1–2 cm | Calibration site, cross-strips |
> | Lever arm and boresight residual | 1–3 cm | Calibration geometry, repeat after any change |
> | Geoid model ($N$) | 1–3 cm regional, more in mountains | Model choice; deliver $h$ too |
> | Checkpoint survey | ≤ 3 cm (≤ ⅓ of product) | Control design, repeat occupations |
> | Ground definition on flat ground | 1–2 cm | Leaf-off window, density margin |
> | Root-sum-square | ≈ 5–8 cm | Modest margin within a 10 cm RMSE$_z$ requirement |
>
> The same table for a survey flown leaf-on, from a base on an unverified mark 45 km away, during a Kp 6 storm, has the first and last rows at 10–20 cm each and no margin at all.

## 26.10 Documentation that starts before the first line

Three artefacts should exist before the first line is flown or sailed. **Project instructions** (NOAA's term; "survey plan" elsewhere): the governing specification and edition; the area with planned lines as a GIS layer; sensor configuration (model, serial, firmware, settings, lever arms and boresight values with calibration dates); the control plan with mark descriptions; datum, frame, epoch, geoid or separation model; environmental windows and stand-down rules; QC thresholds and re-run triggers; the deliverables list with the acceptance test for each; and the people responsible. The HSSD Descriptive Report and the LBS project metadata are both easier to produce if the instructions were written to their outline. **A metadata skeleton** ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)): an ISO 19115 or FGDC record, or a STAC item template, with every field known at planning time already filled and the fields to be filled by acquisition and processing identified; lineage ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)) starts with the calibration certificates. **Naming and file conventions**: line identifiers encoding date and sequence; raw-data directories mirrored nightly and never modified; a version convention for processed products; a fixed-column daily log; a checksum manifest generated at the end of every day. Raw data overwritten by "corrected" data are unrecoverable, and it happens on every project where the convention was not set in advance ([Chapter 29](ch29-processing-pipelines.md)).

### 26.10.1 Planning checklist

The full checklist is in [Appendix G](../appendices/appendix-g-checklists.md); the planning subset, in the order the decisions are usually forced:

1. Use and requirement written down; governing specification and edition named; deliverables and acceptance tests listed with the dataset that satisfies each.
2. Product datum, frame, epoch, geoid or separation model, and surface definition (DSM/DTM, bridges, water) decided and recorded.
3. Geometry designed at the limiting case: swath or footprint, spacing and overlap at the shallowest depth or lowest usable altitude, crosslines ≥ 5 % of line km through the full depth or terrain range on different days, density and feature-detection margin, line order against tide, sun, and wind.
4. Control designed: base station(s) on published or independently established marks within baseline limits; checkpoints by land cover and slope, counts sufficient for the statistics to be reported, withheld from adjustment, surveyed to ≤ ⅓ of the product tolerance; tide gauge and benchmarks or ERS plan; calibration flight or patch test and reference surface per mobilization.
5. Environmental windows stated with verification method: leaf-off, snow-free, low flow, turbidity, sea state, PDOP, Kp, cloud base.
6. Operational QC loop defined: daily checks, thresholds, re-run triggers, log format, backup routine.
7. Removals planned and their measurement scheduled: draft and squat, waterline, canopy ground truth, transient objects.
8. Permits, consents, airspace, access, and privacy obligations obtained or in progress with dates.
9. Risk: weather contingency from climatology, spares, re-survey decision rules, shared-mobilization opportunities.
10. Documentation: project instructions, metadata skeleton, naming conventions, checksum manifest — all created before mobilization.

## Then & now

Survey planning as a discipline is older than any sensor in this book. Nineteenth-century triangulation and levelling were planned around closure: the U.S. Coast Survey under Hassler (from 1816) and the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (from 1802) ⟨H⟩ designed networks so that every figure closed and every level line returned to a benchmark — the same principle as a crossline. Lead-line hydrography planned spacing against chart scale and ran check lines; the first IHO accuracy standard (S-44, 1968) ⟨H⟩ codified line spacing and crossline practice for echo sounders. Aerial photogrammetry formalised 60/30 overlap and control layout for block adjustment, carried through the analogue era by the ASPRS *Manual of Photogrammetry* (first edition 1944) ⟨H⟩. GPS changed the plan more than any sensor: kinematic positioning of aircraft in the late 1980s and GPS–INS integration in the 1990s ⟨H⟩ made ground control a check rather than the solution, moved the critical decision to base siting and baseline length, and with CORS networks and PPP began to remove the base altogether. Multibeam brought the patch test and depth-dependent swath planning; NOAA's HSSD and FPM made project instructions and operational QC explicit deliverables; the USGS Lidar Base Specification (v1.0, 2012; 2024 rev. A) turned density, overlap, and relative-accuracy tests into contract language. This decade adds ERS as the hydrographic default, ICESat-2 (2018) ⟨H⟩ as free supplementary control, cloud-based QC in the field, and drone operations whose planning is dominated by airspace rules rather than fuel. The underlying question has not changed since Hassler: how will you know, before you leave the field, that the survey closes?

## Validation & uncertainty

Planning errors are systematic errors with a lead time: the plan determines which error sources will be observable, which bounded, and which invisible until a user finds them.

**Pre-acquisition: the planned budget.** Write the uncertainty budget (as in Section 26.9) for the product at its most demanding location — swath edge, deepest line, densest canopy, furthest point from the base — and check that the root-sum-square leaves margin against the specification. If not, the plan changes: lower altitude, narrower sector, second base, more frequent casts. A plan with no margin passes only if every component behaves nominally, which no survey achieves. Include the checkpoints' own uncertainty: with checkpoint σ$_c$ and product σ$_p$ the observed RMSE is approximately $\sqrt{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_c^2}$, which is why standards require σ$_c$ ≤ σ$_p/3$ (inflating the observed value by only about 5 %).

**Observability analysis.** For each systematic error source, name the observation that would reveal it: trajectory height bias → crossline and checkpoint offsets; roll → inter-swath differences growing with scan angle and reversing with flight direction; pitch and timing → along-track displacement of edges between opposing lines; sound speed → refraction curvature in the outer beams on flat seabed; tide → day-to-day junction offsets; heading → feature displacement between adjacent opposite-direction lines. An error source with no observation in the plan must either get one or be declared in the metadata as bounded only by prior calibration.

**Checkpoint statistics.** For normally distributed errors the 95 % confidence interval on σ from $n$ samples spans approximately $\sigma\,[1 \mp 1.96/\sqrt{2n}]$: ±31 % for $n = 20$, ±25 % for $n = 30$, ±14 % for $n = 100$. A reported "RMSE$_z$ = 8.2 cm" from 20 checkpoints is consistent with a true value between about 5.7 and 10.8 cm, and the 95th percentile used for VVA from 20 points is set by the single worst point. These are the reasons for the minimum counts and for not pooling land-cover classes with different error distributions.

**During acquisition: relative accuracy is a proxy.** Overlap and crossline statistics measure repeatability, not closeness to truth; a survey can be internally consistent to 2 cm and biased by 20 cm by a wrong antenna height or geoid. Treat the daily numbers as necessary, not sufficient; run the first checkpoint comparison as soon as checkpoints have been overflown, because a partial comparison on day two is worth more than a complete one after demobilization.

**What to report.** The planned budget beside the realised statistics; calibration values with dates and the residuals of each event; crossline and overlap statistics by day and line; checkpoint results by land-cover class with $n$, bias, σ, RMSE, and the appropriate 95 % statistic; environmental conditions encountered against the windows planned; every deviation from the project instructions and why. The report should let a reader reconstruct which lines were flown under which conditions with which calibration — exactly what [Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md) describes a reviewer trying to do, usually without this information.

> **Worked example.** *Was the crossline test adequate?* A multibeam survey reports crossline–mainline differences with σ = 0.18 m and mean −0.04 m at 30 m depth under Order 1a (TVU$_{95}$ = $\sqrt{0.5^2 + (0.013 \times 30)^2}$ = 0.63 m at 30 m, so σ$_{\text{allowed}}$ ≈ 0.32 m). A crossing difference is between two independent soundings, so the single-sounding σ is $0.18/\sqrt{2} = 0.13$ m, comfortably within 0.32 m. But the mean was pooled over all crossings; split by day, day 3 shows +0.11 m and day 5 shows −0.19 m — a 0.30 m difference the pooled statistic hid. The log shows the tide gauge was re-levelled between those days. The pooled statistic passed; the design (crosslines on different days from the mains) made the error visible; the reporting rule (never pool across days without also showing the breakdown) caught it.

## Pitfalls

- **No crosslines ("we had no time").** They look unproductive under schedule pressure. → Without them systematic errors are unobservable and the acceptance review finds this first. Give crosslines their own line numbers and run some early.
- **Checkpoints clustered near the office.** Access drives placement. → They sample one line, one day, one land cover and cannot detect a line- or day-dependent bias. Stratify and spread; audit the layout map before the crew leaves.
- **Base station on an unknown mark.** A convenient pillar with a coordinate from an old file. → Every point inherits a constant bias that overlap and crosslines cannot see. Use published marks or two independent static occupations to CORS.
- **Patch test skipped after a transducer change.** "The offsets were measured and entered." → Offsets are not angular alignment; a 0.5° roll error at 60° in 30 m of water is a 0.5 m depth error at the swath edge. Re-run after any hardware change and verify on the reference surface.
- **Season chosen by budget year.** → Leaf-on, snow, high flow, or storm season deliver a biased DTM or gappy bathymetry. Negotiate carry-over or narrow the scope inside the right window.
- **Antenna height blunder.** Wrong reference, slant for vertical, or unrecorded. → A constant bias across the whole survey. Measure twice, photograph the setup, record ARP and ANTEX model.
- **One base for a 100 km block.** → Ambiguity fixing fails at the far edge, especially in active ionosphere; the trajectory flags show it but nobody looks until processing. Add bases or use a network solution; plan windows by Kp.
- **Line spacing planned on average depth.** → Coverage gaps appear over the shoal, exactly where features matter. Plan on the shallowest depth each line meets, drawn on the best existing bathymetry.
- **Raw data overwritten in the field.** → No reprocessing when a calibration error is found. Write-once raw directories, mirrored nightly, checksummed.

## Key takeaways

- Observability of error is designed in: overlap, crosslines, calibration events, and independent checkpoints are the only evidence of quality, and they are cheap once the platform is mobilized.
- Name the governing specification, the deliverables, and the acceptance test for each before designing geometry; every test implies a dataset you must collect.
- Plan geometry at the limiting case — shallowest depth, densest canopy, swath edge, furthest baseline — and keep margin in the planned error budget.
- Base coordinates, antenna heights, and the geoid or separation model are constant biases invisible to overlap tests; control them by procedure and test them with checkpoints early.
- Checkpoint counts follow from the statistics to be reported (confidence on σ scales as $1/\sqrt{2n}$; percentiles need more); stratify by land cover, spread across lines and days, keep them out of any adjustment.
- The choice of month is the cheapest error reduction available; verify the window with evidence collected at the time.
- Run a daily QC loop with pre-agreed re-run triggers; a problem found after demobilization costs a mobilization.
- Decide ERS versus water-level reduction, and what will later be removed, at planning time, and schedule the measurements each requires.
