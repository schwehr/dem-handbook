# Chapter 16 — Platforms: feet, cars, boats, drones, kites, aircraft, satellites, fixed infrastructure

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** This chapter opens the sensor part of the book by asking *where the sensor sits* before asking what it measures; the chapters that follow ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md) through [Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)) take up the physics of each sensor class.

**In this chapter.** A sensor's platform fixes its vantage point, and the vantage point fixes most of the error envelope: standoff range, look geometry, trajectory quality, dwell time, revisit, and the regulatory and cost regime come with the platform, not with the laser or the transducer. You will be able to lay out the platform families from a surveyor's boots to a geodetic satellite, state for each the typical standoff, swath, resolution, accuracy class, and operating constraints, and explain why the same sensor behaves differently on a backpack, a car, a drone, and a helicopter. You will be able to use the platform-selection matrix at the end to go from a requirement (area, resolution, accuracy, environment, cadence, budget, permits) to a short list of candidate platforms, and you will know where fixed infrastructure and the crowd — tide gauges, CORS, permanent scanners, coastal cameras, fishing-vessel sounders, phones — fit as cheap, continuous, but trust-limited sources of elevation and water-level information.

## 16.1 Humans on foot

The oldest platform is still the reference against which the others are judged. A surveyor with a **GNSS rover** on a 2 m pole, a **total station**, or a **levelling** staff produces one coordinate per occupation, slowly, at an accuracy no moving platform matches: millimetres for geodetic levelling over a kilometre, 1–3 cm for RTK heights in open sky ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)), sub-centimetre for a total station over a few hundred metres. These become the **checkpoints** against which every other platform's NVA and VVA are computed ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)). The limitation is throughput — perhaps 100–300 checkpoints a day on open terrain, far fewer in forest, none on a cliff face or under water.

The foot platform has been augmented in two directions. The first is **backpack and handheld laser scanning**: a spinning multi-beam lidar with an IMU and a SLAM solver ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)) producing a dense cloud of everything within 30–100 m of the walker, including under-canopy structure that airborne systems see only sparsely. Relative precision is typically 1–3 cm; absolute accuracy is whatever the trajectory can be tied to — decimetres with GNSS in the open, relative only under canopy or indoors until control or co-registration to an airborne cloud pins it down. Drift without loop closure is of the order of 0.1–1 % of path length, so survey-grade workflows close loops and scan targets.

The second is **handheld photogrammetry and phone lidar**. A phone camera walked around an outcrop or a façade, processed with structure from motion ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)), yields a textured mesh whose scale and orientation are arbitrary until control, a scale bar, or the phone's own GNSS/IMU tags constrain them. Since 2020 some phones carry a short-range direct time-of-flight lidar with a usable range of about 5 m; evaluations find centimetre precision on close targets but systematic scale errors and drift over tens of metres (Luetzenburg et al. 2021). Treat phone lidar as a dense-texture sensor for metre-scale objects, not as a mapping instrument.

## 16.2 Cars and rail

A **mobile mapping system (MMS)** mounts lidar scanners, panoramic and metric cameras, a GNSS receiver, a tactical- or navigation-grade IMU, and a wheel odometer on a road vehicle. The scanners are typically 2D profilers spinning at 100–250 Hz with ranges of 100–400 m, sweeping a helix along the road; at 50 km/h and 200 Hz the along-track profile spacing is about 7 cm, the within-profile spacing at 10 m range millimetres, and densities of thousands of points per square metre on the pavement are normal. Google Street View launched in May 2007 in five US cities and expanded internationally in 2008 ⟨H⟩, carrying lidar from the early vehicle generations onward.

The platform's weakness is its trajectory. Roads run through **urban canyons**, under trees, and through tunnels, exactly where GNSS is multipath-ridden or absent ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)), and the IMU must bridge the gaps: a navigation-grade unit holds a few centimetres over a 60 s outage, a tactical-grade unit degrades to decimetres ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). Vertical accuracy is typically 2–5 cm in open streets and 10–30 cm downtown unless pavement control targets are used in a post-processed adjustment. Because the sensor looks sideways from 2 m above the road, an MMS maps the corridor superbly and nothing behind the first building line or a parked truck ([Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md)).

A **rail clearance scanner** rides a fixed, smooth, repeatable trajectory; track geometry is measured by contact or optical sensors and the lidar profiles the clearance envelope at millimetre relative precision. Georeferencing is often by chainage along a track-centreline model rather than by GNSS, which makes rail scans excellent relative products and awkward to merge with map-projected DEMs unless the centreline is itself surveyed.

## 16.3 Boats, ships, USVs, AUVs, ROVs, submarines, divers

On water the platform choice is governed by depth, because sonar footprint and swath scale with range to the bottom ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). A **survey launch** (8–12 m) with a hull-mounted or pole-mounted multibeam covers 0–200 m depths; a **ship** with a gondola-mounted deep-water system (12–30 kHz) covers the abyssal ocean at swaths of 3–5 times water depth but with footprints of 50–150 m in 4,000 m of water; an **AUV** flying 50 m above the seabed with a 200–400 kHz system gets back to sub-metre footprints at any depth it can reach, at the cost of covering a swath of only 100–200 m and of positioning that depends on acoustic tracking and dead reckoning ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)). **ROVs** add a tether and a surface vessel but can hover and station-keep for structured-light, laser-line, or photogrammetric micro-bathymetry at millimetre resolution over areas of square metres. Submarines have mapped under ice (the US Navy SCICEX cruises of 1993–1999 produced the first systematic Arctic bathymetry under permanent ice). Divers with tape, slate, and camera still produce the closest-range bathymetry of reefs and wrecks.

Two platform families have changed the economics in the 2010s–2020s. **Uncrewed surface vessels (USVs)** range from 1–2 m portable catamarans carrying a single-beam or small multibeam into ponds, tailings dams, and river reaches, to 12–24 m autonomous ships (Saildrone Surveyor, Ocean Infinity's Armada fleet, XOCEAN) carrying full deep-water multibeams for weeks at a time with a fraction of a crewed ship's fuel and day-rate. They share the crewed vessel's physics — motion, sound speed, waterline — with smaller hulls that move more in the same sea state. **Low-cost ROVs** began with OpenROV, founded in 2011 and crowdfunded in 2012 ⟨H⟩, which put a tethered camera platform in the hands of citizen scientists for a few hundred dollars; their mapping value is visual rather than geodetic, but they lowered the barrier to under-water observation the way consumer drones did above it.

**Ships of opportunity and crowdsourced bathymetry (CSB)** are the ocean's version of the crowd. Any vessel with a navigation echo sounder and a GNSS receiver produces depth–position pairs; IHO publication **B-12** defines how volunteers log them, which metadata are mandatory (transducer draft, antenna offset, sound speed assumed, tide applied or not), and how they are archived at the IHO DCDB at NOAA NCEI. Seabed 2030 counts CSB among the sources needed to map the three quarters of the ocean floor still lacking modern soundings (Mayer et al. 2018). The platform point is that a fishing vessel's sounder has an unknown draft, an uncalibrated sound speed, and no motion sensor: depths good to a few percent, positions to tens of metres, and a vertical reference of instantaneous water level unless someone reduces it. A 2 m draft on a sounding in 10 m of water is a 20 % bias — larger than the entire S-44 Order 1a allowance — which is why B-12 makes platform metadata mandatory. Useful for discovering an uncharted seamount; useless for a harbour chart ([Chapter 2](ch02-uses-bathymetry.md), [Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)).

## 16.4 Drones

Small uncrewed aircraft systems (UAS, "drones") now collect more elevation projects by count than any other platform, if not by area. DJI, founded in 2006 ⟨H⟩, made the multirotor a commodity from roughly 2013 onward; the survey industry built RTK/PPK, lidar, and multispectral payloads on top. Three airframe classes matter:

| Class | Typical endurance | Speed | Payload | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multirotor | 20–45 min | 5–15 m/s | 0.5–3 kg (up to ~10 kg heavy-lift) | Sites < 1 km², corridors, vertical structures, hover |
| Fixed-wing | 60–120 min | 15–25 m/s | 0.5–1.5 kg | Open areas 1–20 km² per day, SfM |
| VTOL fixed-wing | 60–180 min | 15–25 m/s | 1–3 kg | Large areas without a runway; lidar and SfM |

Payloads divide into **drone SfM** — a 20–60 MP RGB camera, flown at 60–120 m above ground for ground sample distances of 1–3 cm, processed to a DSM and orthomosaic ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) — and **drone lidar**, from survey-grade scanners (Riegl miniVUX/VUX series, 905–1550 nm, 100–750 kHz) through mid-range units to automotive-derived spinning lidars (Livox, Velodyne/Ouster) integrated with a tactical IMU. The practical differences are in [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md); the platform-level summary is that SfM gives a DSM of the visible surface at the cost of bare-earth under vegetation and of systematic doming when geometry is weak, while lidar gives ground returns through gaps in canopy at a higher price and with an error budget dominated by the IMU and the GNSS trajectory rather than the laser.

Georeferencing has moved to **RTK/PPK direct georeferencing** (camera or scanner position to 2–5 cm from a carrier-phase solution, [Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) with a few control points retained as checks. Independent evaluations consistently find that RTK/PPK SfM without any control can leave a vertical bias of several centimetres to a decimetre from camera-calibration and lever-arm errors, and that one or a few control points remove it; a project with *zero* checkpoints has a prediction, not an accuracy statement. Good projects reach 2–5 cm RMSEz on hard open ground with either SfM or lidar; SfM degrades rapidly on vegetation and on snow, sand, and water, lidar mostly with IMU grade and flight height.

Regulation shapes what drones can survey. In the United States, FAA **Part 107** (2016) governs commercial small UAS (< 25 kg): visual line of sight, 400 ft (122 m) above ground or a structure, airspace authorisation (LAANC) in controlled airspace, Remote ID since 2023, and waivers for beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations. In the European Union, **EASA** Regulations 2019/947 and 2019/945 (applicable from December 2020) define Open, Specific, and Certified categories; most mapping falls in Open A2/A3 or in Specific with a SORA risk assessment. The flight height, the distance from the pilot, and hence the area per sortie are legally bounded, and permits add weeks near people or controlled airspace. Wind is the environmental bound: a multirotor holding position in 10 m/s gusts tilts 15–25°, image blur at 10 m/s and 1/1000 s is 1 cm, comparable to the GSD, and lidar noise rises with angular jitter — a drone campaign's stated accuracy is a fair-weather figure.

> **Rule of thumb.** For drone SfM, expect vertical RMSE on hard, textured ground of roughly 1–2 × GSD when the block has good overlap (≥ 75 % forward, ≥ 65 % side), oblique or cross-strip imagery, calibrated camera, and well-distributed control. Expect 3–5 × GSD or worse when any of those is missing, and no bare-earth at all under closed canopy. The rule says nothing about vegetated or low-texture surfaces, where SfM error is governed by matching failure, not by GSD.

## 16.5 Kites, balloons, blimps, and poles

The first aerial photograph was taken from a tethered balloon by Gaspard-Félix Tournachon — "Nadar" — over the Petit-Bicêtre valley near Paris in 1858 ⟨H⟩; the earliest surviving one is James Wallace Black's 1860 view of Boston. Arthur Batut flew a camera on a kite in 1888, and George Lawrence's 1906 kite panorama of San Francisco after the earthquake remains the famous demonstration of the technique. These platforms did not disappear when aircraft arrived; they occupy a niche that drones have not entirely closed. A kite or a helium blimp has no battery limit and no noise, can be flown where drone regulations forbid powered aircraft, and in steady 4–10 m/s wind a kite is a stable camera mount at 50–150 m. A telescoping **pole** of 6–15 m, with a camera triggered by intervalometer, is the lowest and cheapest aerial platform and produces SfM DSMs of excavation trenches, beach profiles, and gully heads at millimetre GSD. All of these yield imagery whose exterior orientation is unknown and must be solved from ground control or from SfM's own bundle adjustment; none carries a trajectory worth the name. They are SfM platforms by definition, and their accuracy is the accuracy of the control.

## 16.6 Crewed aircraft and helicopters

The crewed aircraft remains the workhorse for regional elevation mapping because its combination of altitude, speed, payload, and endurance maximises area per hour at the point densities national specifications demand. A twin-engine survey aircraft (Cessna 404/Conquest, Piper Navajo, King Air, Diamond DA42) carries a 50–100 kg lidar or a large-format camera, flies 4–8 hours, and covers at 120–180 kn (60–90 m/s). Swath width is $2h\tan(\theta/2)$ for flying height $h$ and full field of view $\theta$: at 1,500 m AGL and 40° FOV the swath is about 1,090 m, and with 30 % sidelap the net strip width is about 760 m, so one hour at 70 m/s covers roughly 190 km². Point density falls with height and speed and rises with pulse rate; a 1 MHz scanner at that height and speed lays down about 13 pulses/m² before overlap, which is why USGS QL1 (≥ 8 pts/m²) and QL2 (≥ 2 pts/m²) are routinely met from 1,000–2,500 m AGL ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)).

The trade-offs are physical. Higher flight gives wider swath, fewer turns, and lower cost per km², but larger footprints (0.25 mrad divergence × 2,000 m = 0.5 m), lower density, weaker returns from dark targets, and more eye-safety constraints; lower flight gives density and small footprints at the cost of more lines and, in mountains, terrain-following plans. **Helicopters** fly slowly (30–60 kn) and low (100–500 m) for corridors — power lines ([Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)), pipelines, rivers, coastal strips — where 20–100 pts/m² and flexible routing matter more than area rate; their vibration is harder on IMUs and their day-rate higher.

Regional programs standardise this platform. The USGS **3D Elevation Program (3DEP)**, built on the 2012 National Enhanced Elevation Assessment and operational from about 2014, has procured QL2-or-better lidar over the conterminous United States through contractors flying crewed aircraft to the **Lidar Base Specification**, with IfSAR for Alaska; the US Army Corps' **Joint Airborne Lidar Bathymetry Technical Center of Expertise (JALBTCX)** runs the **National Coastal Mapping Program (NCMP)**, flying topobathymetric lidar and imagery along US coasts on a recurring cycle ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)). The Netherlands' AHN, England's Environment Agency national lidar, Switzerland's swissSURFACE3D, and Denmark's national DTM are national equivalents, all flown from crewed aircraft at 2–20 pts/m².

> **Worked example.** Flight-line count for a 400 km² block at QL2 with a 1,000 kHz scanner. Choose 1,800 m AGL, 36° FOV, 65 m/s ground speed. Swath $= 2 \times 1800 \times \tan 18° = 1170$ m. With 30 % sidelap, line spacing $= 0.7 \times 1170 \approx 820$ m. For a 20 km × 20 km block, lines across the short dimension: $20{,}000 / 820 \approx 25$ lines of 20 km, plus turns (~2 min each) → flight time $\approx 25 \times (20{,}000/65\,\text{s} + 120\,\text{s}) = 25 \times 428\,\text{s} \approx 3.0$ h. Density before overlap $= 1{,}000{,}000 / (1170 \times 65) \approx 13$ pts/m²; with 30 % overlap the aggregate nominal density is about 17 pts/m², comfortably QL1. If the client wants only QL2, fly higher (2,500 m AGL: swath 1,620 m, ~9 pts/m²) and save about a third of the lines — if the scanner's maximum range and eye-safety class allow it.

## 16.7 High-altitude platforms and pseudo-satellites

Between aircraft and satellites sit **high-altitude pseudo-satellites (HAPS)**: solar-electric fixed-wing aircraft (Airbus Zephyr, whose 2022 flight exceeded 60 days before it was lost) and stratospheric balloons loitering at 18–22 km, above weather and controlled airspace. Their appeal for elevation is persistent, agile, cloud-top viewing of a region a few hundred kilometres across; their limits are payload mass and power, night-time energy, and the fact that at 20 km an optical GSD or a lidar footprint is closer to a satellite's than to an aircraft's. As of the mid-2020s HAPS have flown demonstration imaging payloads; no operational DEM product is produced from them, and claims of imminent persistent mapping should be read as prospective.

## 16.8 Satellites

Satellite platforms trade proximity for coverage. Nearly all elevation-relevant satellites fly in **sun-synchronous low Earth orbit** (500–800 km, inclination about 97–99°, fixed local overpass time), repeating their ground track every 1–30 days. **Revisit** differs from repeat: an agile satellite rolling ±30–45° off nadir revisits a target in 1–3 days from a 16- or 26-day repeat orbit, at the cost of oblique geometry; and revisit is not cloud-free revisit, which over temperate and tropical coasts can be a fifth or a tenth of the nominal rate. **Swath versus resolution** is the other constraint: a 30 cm GSD sensor images a 13–15 km swath; a 10 m GSD sensor images 290 km.

For DEMs the satellite families are:

- **Optical stereo.** Ikonos (launched September 1999, 0.82 m pan) ⟨H⟩ established commercial sub-metre stereo; QuickBird (2001), WorldView-1/2/3 (2007/2009/2014, down to 0.31 m), Pléiades 1A/1B (2011/2012) and Pléiades Neo (2021), SPOT 6/7, and the first WorldView Legion satellites (2024) ⟨H⟩ followed. Agile pointing enables same-pass in-track stereo and tri-stereo with convergence angles of 15–45°, from which DSMs at 1–4 m posting with vertical accuracies of about 1–4 m without control and sub-metre with control are produced ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)). Lower-resolution stereo (ASTER, 15 m; SPOT 5 HRS) built the global ASTER GDEM and much of the pre-SRTM archive.
- **SAR.** Seasat (1978) demonstrated spaceborne SAR for 105 days ⟨H⟩; ERS-1/2 (1991/1995) made repeat-pass InSAR routine; Sentinel-1A (April 2014) ⟨H⟩ made C-band SAR free and systematic; TanDEM-X (2010) flew in formation with TerraSAR-X to produce the first consistent global 12 m DEM by single-pass bistatic interferometry; ALOS-2 (2014) and ALOS-4 (2024) carry L-band; NISAR (launched July 2025) carries L- and S-band for systematic deformation and biomass; ESA's Biomass (launched April 2025) ⟨H⟩ is the first P-band SAR in orbit, aimed at forest biomass and sub-canopy topography ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)). The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (February 2000, 11 days) used a 60 m mast on the Space Shuttle to produce the first near-global 30/90 m DEM — a platform that existed for one flight.
- **Lidar.** ICESat (January 2003, GLAS, 70 m footprints every 170 m, until 2009) measured ice-sheet elevation change; ICESat-2 (September 2018) ⟨H⟩ with ATLAS photon-counting lidar produces six ground tracks of ~11 m footprints at 0.7 m spacing; GEDI (December 2018) ⟨H⟩ on the International Space Station fires 25 m footprints along eight tracks between ±51.6° latitude, for canopy structure and ground elevation ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)). These are profiling instruments: sparse, accurate truth rather than wall-to-wall DEMs.
- **Radar altimetry.** Geosat (1985) ⟨H⟩ produced the marine gravity field that underlies all predicted bathymetry; TOPEX/Poseidon (1992) ⟨H⟩ set the standard for ocean altimetry; the Jason series, CryoSat-2 (2010, SAR-interferometric mode for ice margins), Sentinel-3 and Sentinel-6 continue it; SWOT (December 2022) carries the KaRIn wide-swath interferometric altimeter that images water-surface elevation over 120 km swaths at ~10 cm precision over 1 km² ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md) for altimetry-predicted bathymetry; [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md) for water surfaces).
- **Gravity.** GRACE (2002–2017), GRACE Follow-On (2018–), and GOCE (2009–2013) measure the geoid and its time variation; they define the vertical datum against which every other satellite height is reduced ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

The platform-level uncertainty for satellites is set by orbit determination (now centimetres radially with GNSS and laser ranging), attitude knowledge, and atmospheric delays. Agility makes attitude the weakest link: a 1″ (4.8 µrad) pointing error at 700 km is 3.4 m on the ground, which is why satellite DSMs need control points or a reference DEM to remove biases.

## 16.9 Fixed infrastructure that senses the world

Not every platform moves. Fixed installations provide the long time series that moving platforms cannot, and several of them are elevation sensors in disguise.

**CORS and tide gauges** are the backbone. A continuously operating GNSS reference station measures the motion of its own monument in the terrestrial reference frame to millimetres per year ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)); a tide gauge measures water level relative to its benchmark to a centimetre every six minutes ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). Together they are how a survey's heights and depths are tied to a datum and how subsidence and sea-level rise are separated. Co-located gauges and CORS (GNSS at tide gauges, "GNSS@TG") exist at a few hundred sites worldwide; every coastal DEM project should know where the nearest ones are.

**Permanent terrestrial laser scanners** mounted on a building, a mast, or a hut and scanning a cliff, a slope, a glacier front, or a snow field every hour produce 4D point-cloud time series that resolve rockfall magnitude–frequency distributions down to decimetre volumes (Williams et al. 2018, on the North Yorkshire coast) and snow depth distributions at hourly cadence. Their value is temporal: a scanner that returns every hour catches the precursory deformation that a yearly airborne survey averages away. Their error model is dominated by atmospheric refraction over the 100–1,000 m range, by scanner thermal drift, and by registration between epochs rather than by range noise.

**Ground-based radar interferometers** (IDS IBIS, GroundProbe SSR, Reutech) are the standard monitoring tool for open-pit slopes and some landslides: a Ku-band radar on a fixed base scans a slope every few minutes and measures line-of-sight displacement to sub-millimetre precision by phase differencing — terrestrial InSAR ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)), with atmospheric correction from stable reflectors. They do not make DEMs; they make the displacement field that tells you when the DEM is about to change.

**Coastal video** turned ordinary cameras into shoreline and nearshore-bathymetry sensors. Argus stations (Holman & Stanley 2007), installed over beaches from 1992 onward, produce time-exposure and time-stack images from which shoreline position, sandbar location, run-up, and — via wave-celerity inversion — nearshore bathymetry are derived ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)). **CoastSnap** (Harley et al. 2019) replaced the fixed camera with a fixed phone cradle and the public's phones. Traffic and security webcams can serve the same way for snow, flood stage, and shoreline — if someone surveys the camera.

**Seismometers, weather radars, harbour sonars, and river gauges** round out the list: seismic networks size the events that move the ground ([Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)); weather radars document the rain and snow that change a DSM; fixed harbour and dam-face sonars monitor scour and siltation continuously; river gauges fix the water surface in a riverine DEM ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). None is an elevation sensor by design; each constrains a surface the DEM must represent at some epoch.

## 16.10 The crowd as platform

Several billion people carry a GNSS receiver, a barometer, an IMU, and cameras. The **phone** as platform has produced three genuinely elevation-relevant data streams. Phone GNSS, especially since dual-frequency chipsets (2018) and raw-measurement access on Android, yields horizontal positions to 1–5 m and heights to several metres — useful for mapping where things are, not how high. Phone **barometers**, fused with GNSS and map matching, give relative height changes to 1–2 m over minutes, which is how indoor floor-level positioning and some "elevation gain" metrics are produced, and how crowd pressure fields have been used in meteorology. Phone cameras feed the SfM and CoastSnap pipelines above. In 2024 a Google team used GNSS measurements from millions of Android phones to map ionospheric total electron content at a density exceeding the scientific monitoring network in many regions (Smith et al. 2024) ⟨H⟩ — a demonstration that the crowd can calibrate the error sources that limit everyone else's positioning.

**Vehicle fleets** with forward cameras, radars, and sometimes lidar produce road-corridor 3D at a cadence of hours in cities, held privately by manufacturers and realised mainly in HD maps rather than DEMs. **Fishing-vessel and recreational sounders** are the marine crowd (Section 16.3). **OpenStreetMap mappers** contribute little elevation directly, but their footprints, centrelines, and water bodies are the semantics that DSM-to-DTM and hydro-enforcement workflows consume ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md), [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)).

Trust and privacy are the costs. A crowd measurement arrives with unknown instrument and calibration from a contributor who may be careless or adversarial, and it is personal — a vessel track is a fishing ground, a phone track a home address. B-12 handles the first by mandating platform metadata and the second by anonymised identifiers; [Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md) treats crowd data as the extreme case of evaluating data without the full story and [Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md) the privacy obligations. The crowd must be modelled statistically — robust estimators, cross-validation against surveyed data, contributor reputation — rather than trusted per observation.

| Satellite SAR (InSAR DEM) | 10⁵–10⁶ km² | 5–30 m | 2–10 m (TanDEM-X ~2 m LE90 relative) | all-weather; penetrates dry snow/vegetation partly | repeat 6–46 d | open (Sentinel) to licensed |

| Fixed TLS / radar / camera | one site | cm–dm | mm–cm relative | one slope, beach, glacier | minutes–hours | site access, power |
| Crowd (phones, CSB) | opportunistic | sparse | metres (phone); % of depth (CSB) | wherever people go | continuous | privacy; provenance |

Reading the matrix from a requirement: a 2 km² quarry needing 5 cm volumes monthly is a drone (lidar if the benches are vegetated); a 5,000 km² county needing a QL2 DTM is a crewed aircraft under a national program; an eroding 500 m cliff needing warning of failure is a permanent TLS or radar; a 40 m-deep reef pass with clear water is bathymetric lidar, and the same pass in a turbid estuary is a USV with a multibeam. The matrix cannot resolve the common case where the environment defeats every platform at the required accuracy — a mangrove fringe in turbid water, a cloud-covered rainforest ridge — and the honest answer there is lower accuracy, longer time, or a different question ([Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md)).

## Then & now

The platform story is one of standoff increasing and then, recently, decreasing again. Nadar's tethered balloon in 1858 ⟨H⟩ gave a vantage point of a few hundred metres; the first aerial photography from aircraft in the 1910s, driven by the First World War, made systematic mapping from 1–5 km practical, and the photogrammetric plotters of the 1920s–1950s turned it into contours. The Corona reconnaissance satellites, first launched in 1959 with the first film recovered in 1960 ⟨H⟩, took the camera to orbit, and Landsat 1 in 1972 ⟨H⟩ made civil orbital imaging continuous. The Shuttle in February 2000 flew SRTM and gave the world a DEM in eleven days. Each step traded resolution and accuracy for coverage.

The reversal began with the consumer drone in the early 2010s — DJI's Phantom (2013) and the RTK/PPK payloads that followed — which brought the camera back to 100 m above the ground, cheaply and often. SmallSat constellations (Planet's Doves from 2014; later the agile Pléiades Neo and WorldView Legion fleets) brought daily revisit to the orbital tier. In the water the equivalent is the 2020s fleet of USVs and AUVs mapping continuously for Seabed 2030 and offshore wind. The current state is layered: satellites for coverage and change, crewed aircraft for regional accuracy, drones for sites, feet for truth — with fixed infrastructure and the crowd filling the time axis between campaigns. What has not changed is that each tier's product is validated by the tier below it.

## Validation & uncertainty

The platform contributes to the uncertainty budget in four ways, and a validation plan should test each separately.

**Trajectory.** For every moving platform the sensor's position and attitude at each measurement are estimated, not known, and their errors scale with standoff range ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md), [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). The test is internal consistency: overlapping strips, swaths, or image blocks should agree within the expected random noise, and a systematic offset between them is a trajectory or calibration error. Report overlap statistics for every airborne, mobile, and marine survey; a well-calibrated airborne lidar project shows inter-swath RMS differences of 3–6 cm on flat hard surfaces, a multibeam survey crossline agreement within the S-44 TVU.

**Geometry.** The platform's look geometry determines occlusion, incidence angle, and footprint, and therefore which parts of the surface are measured at all and at what effective resolution. Validate coverage, not just accuracy: compute the void fraction and its spatial pattern (a mobile mapping dataset with no roof points is complete for its platform and incomplete for a DSM), and compute the density or footprint map rather than quoting the nominal value.

**Environment and epoch.** Platforms that cannot fly in cloud or wind, or sail in sea state 5, sample the environment selectively, and the resulting DEM is a fair-weather or a leaf-off product. State the acquisition window and the environmental conditions (wind, sea state, Secchi depth, snow cover, tide stage) with the data; they define the epoch and the conditions under which the stated accuracy holds ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md)).

**Independent check.** The product must be compared with measurements from a *different* platform of higher accuracy: ground checkpoints for airborne and drone products (ASPRS asks for at least 30 per land-cover class, [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)), crosslines or a reference surface for marine surveys, ICESat-2 or airborne lidar for satellite DEMs. A product validated only against itself has a precision, not an accuracy.

## Software

**Open source:** mission-planning and platform-side tools — QGroundControl and Mission Planner (ArduPilot) for drone flight planning and logs; OpenDroneMap for drone SfM; RTKLIB and PRIDE PPP-AR for rover and platform GNSS post-processing ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)); MB-System for marine platform data ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)); PDAL and CloudCompare for every point cloud regardless of platform; OpenSfM/COLMAP for kite, pole, and phone imagery. Caveat: open flight planners leave regulatory compliance (airspace, Remote ID) to the operator.

**Free but closed:** DJI Pilot/Terra planning modes (with purchased processing), Pix4Dcapture, Litchi for mission planning; NOAA's CSB Logger guidance and the DCDB submission tools; the FAA's B4UFLY and LAANC-approved apps for airspace authorisation.

**Commercial:** UgCS and DJI Terra for mission planning; Applanix POSPac and NovAtel Inertial Explorer for trajectory processing on cars, aircraft, and vessels (the trajectory is where platform error lives); Trimble Business Center and Leica Infinity for rover and total-station reductions; HYPACK and QPS Qinsy for vessel configuration; Maxar, Airbus, Planet, and ICEYE tasking portals. Caveat: each vendor's lever-arm or vessel file uses its own sign and axis conventions, and the geometry error from mixing them is the most common systematic error in mobile and marine data.

## Standards & guides

- **FAA 14 CFR Part 107** (2016, amended 2021 for operations over people and night, Remote ID from 2023) — US small UAS operating rules; sets ceiling, line-of-sight, and airspace constraints for drone mapping.
- **EASA Regulations (EU) 2019/947 and 2019/945** (applicable December 2020; Easy Access Rules consolidated) — EU UAS operational categories (Open/Specific/Certified) and product classes; SORA for Specific category.
- **IHO B-12 Guidance on Crowdsourced Bathymetry**, Edition 3.0.0 (2023) — metadata, logging, and submission for CSB; defines required vessel/platform metadata.
- **USGS Lidar Base Specification** (2024 rev. A and online successors) — platform-independent product requirements that drive crewed and drone lidar flight planning.
- **NOAA Office of Coast Survey Field Procedures Manual** (current edition) and **Hydrographic Surveys Specifications and Deliverables** — vessel configuration, offset surveys, and platform-related calibration (patch test, settlement/squat) for NOAA and contract surveys.
- **UNCLOS Part XIII** (1982, in force 1994) — marine scientific research; coastal-state consent regime for surveys in foreign EEZs and continental shelves; relevant to any ship, USV, or AUV campaign outside national waters.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data**, Edition 2 (2023) — checkpoint requirements that validate the output of any platform.

## Pitfalls

- **Platform chosen by availability rather than requirement** → the firm owns a drone, so the 50 km² project becomes 40 drone sorties over three weeks with inconsistent conditions → detect by comparing the requirement matrix (Section 16.11) with the proposal before mobilising; avoid by costing the crewed-aircraft alternative honestly.
- **Drone campaigns in wind** → blur, IMU noise, and reduced overlap when the airframe crabs → detect through image sharpness metrics and SfM reprojection error, and through lidar strip-to-strip noise; avoid by setting a wind limit (typically 8–10 m/s for mapping multirotors) and logging it.
- **Satellite revisit confused with cloud-free revisit** → a "daily" constellation delivers one usable scene a month over a monsoon coast → detect by querying the archive's cloud statistics before promising a cadence.
- **Hull-mounted sonars blinded by aeration** → bubble sweep-down in a head sea drops outer beams or the whole swath → detect by watching the water-column display and the coverage map in real time; avoid by slowing down, changing heading, or choosing a gondola or drop-keel mount.
- **Crowd data without provenance** → depths of unknown draft, phone heights of unknown source, mixed into a product as if surveyed → detect by requiring platform metadata (B-12) and by cross-validating against surveyed data; avoid by weighting or excluding records that lack it.
- **Mobile mapping in urban canyons reported at open-sky accuracy** → the spec sheet's 2 cm becomes 20 cm downtown → detect with pavement control targets and loop-closure residuals; report accuracy per environment class.
- **Lever-arm and offset sign conventions mixed between software packages** → a constant vertical or along-track bias in every line → detect with the patch test or boresight calibration and with overlap statistics; avoid by maintaining one vessel/aircraft configuration file with an explicit axis diagram.
- **Regulation discovered after mobilisation** → the site is in Class B airspace, the EEZ requires consent, the helicopter cannot fly at night → avoid by making permits the first task on the schedule.
- **Fixed-infrastructure data assumed stationary** → the tide gauge benchmark subsides, the webcam is bumped, the TLS pillar heaves in frost → detect by periodic re-survey of the installation (levelling to the benchmark, scanning fixed targets); include the installation's own stability in the uncertainty.
- **Platform geometry omitted from the deliverable** → nobody can later reproduce the lever arms, the flying height, or the vessel draft → avoid by archiving the configuration files and the trajectory with the product ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)).

## Key takeaways

- The platform sets the error envelope before the sensor is switched on: standoff range multiplies every attitude error, and the trajectory, not the laser or the transducer, is usually the largest term.
- Each platform tier is validated by the tier below it — satellites by aircraft and ICESat-2, aircraft by drones and ground, drones by GNSS rovers, ships by launches and crosslines. Plan the check platform with the survey platform.
- Coverage is a platform property as much as accuracy: occlusion, nadir gaps, surf zones, canopy, cloud, and sea state decide what is measured at all. Validate void fraction and effective resolution, not just RMSE.
- Regulation (Part 107, EASA, UNCLOS Part XIII, airspace) bounds what the platform can legally measure and belongs at the top of the schedule.
- Fixed infrastructure — CORS, tide gauges, permanent scanners, coastal cameras, harbour sonars — is under-used, cheap, and continuous, and it is the only source of the time axis between campaigns.
- Crowd platforms are valuable for discovery and calibration (ionosphere, uncharted seamounts, shoreline change) and dangerous for charting; they need mandatory platform metadata and a trust model, not per-observation faith.
- Archive the platform configuration (lever arms, offsets, draft, flying height, trajectory) with the product; without it the product cannot be re-reduced or re-validated.
