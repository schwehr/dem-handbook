# Chapter 6 — Time as a coordinate: epochs, clocks, synchronization

> **Part II — Vocabulary and the foundations of correctness.** This chapter makes "when" an explicit part of "where": the time scales that stamp observations, the clocks that realize them, the synchronization that ties sensors together, and the epochs that date coordinates and datums.

**In this chapter.** You will learn to read and convert the time scales that appear in elevation data — TAI, UTC with its leap seconds, GPS/GLONASS/Galileo/BeiDou system times, Unix time, GPS week — and to recognize their classic failure modes (the 10⁹ s LAS offset, week rollovers, leap-second mishandling). You will understand why clocks matter to geometry: how a timestamp error $\Delta t$ on a moving platform becomes a position error $v\,\Delta t$ and an attitude-latency error $\dot\theta\,\Delta t\,R$, with numbers for aircraft, vessels, cars, and drones; how PPS, NMEA ZDA, PTP, and NTP achieve synchronization at nanosecond, microsecond, and millisecond levels; and why relativity is a daily 38 μs correction in GNSS. You will see that a coordinate without an epoch is incomplete on a moving plate (Australia moves ~7 cm/yr), how datums such as NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00, GDA2020, and ITRF2020 handle this, what the 19-year tidal datum epoch is, and how acquisition dates, per-pixel dates, and validity windows should be recorded. Finally, you will learn how time is encoded in LAS, GSF, SBET, EXIF, and NetCDF, and how to validate a dataset's timing.

## 6.1 Time scales

A **time scale** is a convention for assigning numbers to instants. Elevation work encounters at least six, and they differ by seconds — a trivial amount for a calendar and a disastrous one for a sensor moving at 60 m/s.

**International Atomic Time (TAI)** is the continuous, uniform scale maintained by the BIPM from a weighted ensemble of several hundred atomic clocks; its unit is the SI second (defined since 1967 by the cesium-133 hyperfine transition at 9 192 631 770 Hz), and its origin was set so that TAI ≈ UT1 at the start of 1958. TAI has no leap seconds and is never adjusted; it is the scale against which the others are defined.

**Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)** runs at the TAI rate but is kept within 0.9 s of **UT1** — the Earth-rotation time that navigators and astronomers need — by inserting **leap seconds**, announced by the IERS and applied at the end of June or December. Since 1972, 27 leap seconds have been inserted, the most recent at the end of 2016; thus TAI − UTC = 37 s as of this writing. Every one is a discontinuity: the UTC minute 23:59 of a leap day has 61 seconds, software that represents time as "seconds since an epoch, 86 400 per day" cannot express 23:59:60, and systems handle it by repeating a second, "smearing" it over 24 h (as Google and Amazon do), or crashing (several documented outages accompanied the 2012 and 2015 insertions). Because the Earth's rotation has recently *sped up*, a **negative** leap second — a 59-second minute — became plausible in the 2020s, a case almost no software has been tested against. The 27th CGPM (2022, Resolution 4) resolved that the maximum tolerated UT1 − UTC be increased by or before 2035, which in practice ends leap seconds; the replacement tolerance is to be decided. Until then, every timestamp conversion involving UTC needs a leap-second table, and that table must be *current*.

**GPS Time (GPST)** is continuous, with origin 1980-01-06 00:00:00 UTC, at which instant it coincided with UTC; because it ignores subsequent leap seconds, GPST − UTC = 18 s (and GPST = TAI − 19 s, a constant). It is realized by the GPS control segment's clock ensemble, steered to UTC(USNO) modulo integer seconds, and distributed as a **week number** plus **seconds of week** (0–604 799, resetting at Saturday/Sunday midnight GPST). The legacy navigation message carries the week number in 10 bits, so it rolls over every 1024 weeks (19.6 years): on 1999-08-21/22 and 2019-04-06/07; the next is in November 2038 (the modernized CNAV message uses 13 bits). Receivers with stale firmware have interpreted post-rollover weeks as 1999 or 1980, producing impossible dates and, in some receivers, position failures because the ephemeris-age computation breaks. **Galileo System Time (GST)** is also continuous, offset from TAI by the same 19 s, with origin 1999-08-22 00:00 UTC and a 12-bit week counter; **BeiDou Time (BDT)** is continuous with origin 2006-01-01 00:00 UTC, so BDT = GPST − 14 s; **GLONASS time** uniquely follows UTC(SU) *with* leap seconds, offset by +3 h, so multi-GNSS processing must apply the leap-second table to GLONASS alone.

**Unix time** (POSIX time) counts seconds since 1970-01-01 00:00:00 UTC ⟨H⟩ — *nominally*. POSIX defines it as 86 400 × days + seconds of day, which means it ignores leap seconds: it is not a count of elapsed SI seconds but an encoding of a UTC calendar date, and across a leap second it either repeats a value or stalls. Unix time therefore differs from "TAI seconds since 1970" by the number of leap seconds (37 − 10 = 27 as of 2017, since TAI − UTC was already 10 s in 1972). Signed 32-bit Unix time overflows on 2038-01-19, which matters for embedded sensors and old file formats. Unix time is the native scale of GSF, NetCDF-CF defaults, and most logging software.

**Civil, nautical, and calendar time.** Local civil time adds a UTC offset and, in many jurisdictions, daylight-saving shifts that make local timestamps ambiguous twice a year — the reason ISO 8601 timestamps in elevation metadata should always carry an explicit offset (`2024-06-15T14:03:22Z` or `…+02:00`), never a bare local time. **Nautical time** — the convention of 15°-wide time zones at sea, with the ship's clock set to the zone of its longitude — was agreed at the Anglo-French Conference on Time-keeping at Sea in 1917 ⟨H⟩; historical sounding logs and ships' journals before and after that date record local apparent time, local mean time, or zone time, and which one is often undocumented. Historical surveys also raise the **calendar** question: the Gregorian reform of 1582 ⟨H⟩ dropped ten days and was adopted by different countries over the following 340 years (Britain and its colonies in 1752, dropping eleven days; Russia in 1918; Greece in 1923). A date on an eighteenth-century chart or a Russian survey from 1917 may be Julian ("Old Style"); when such data are used for long-baseline change studies or tidal-datum history, the calendar must be identified. Astronomers and planetary scientists avoid the issue with the **Julian Date** (days since noon, 1 January 4713 BCE, Julian proleptic calendar); the Modified Julian Date MJD = JD − 2 400 000.5 (origin 1858-11-17 00:00) appears in GNSS products and in IERS tables.

| Scale | Continuous? | Leap seconds | Offset (2017–present) | Typical appearance in elevation data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TAI | Yes | No | — | Reference only; IERS/BIPM tables |
| UTC | No | Yes | TAI − 37 s | Metadata, ISO 8601 strings, NMEA |
| GPST | Yes | No | TAI − 19 s; UTC + 18 s | LAS, SBET, RINEX, trajectory files |
| GST (Galileo) | Yes | No | Same as GPST (sub-ns steering differences) | RINEX |

> **Definitions that bite.** "GPS time" in a file may mean (a) seconds of the GPS week, (b) continuous seconds since 1980-01-06, (c) continuous seconds minus 10⁹ (LAS "Adjusted Standard GPS Time"), or (d) UTC as output by a GPS receiver's NMEA sentences, which already has the leap seconds applied. The four differ by up to 10⁹ s, by the week number × 604 800 s, and by 18 s. A trajectory in (b) matched to a scanner log in (d) places every point 18 s — at 60 m/s, more than a kilometer — down the flight line.

## 6.2 Clocks

The history of positioning is in large part the history of clocks, because longitude is time ([Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md)). Huygens's **pendulum clock** (1656) ⟨H⟩ reduced daily errors from a quarter of an hour to around ten seconds, good enough for astronomy but useless at sea. John Harrison's **H4 marine chronometer**, tested on voyages to Jamaica (1761–62) and Barbados (1764), kept time to within a few seconds over weeks, making longitude by time transfer practical (Sobel 1995; Howse 1980). The chronometer remained the standard until radio time signals (from 1904–1910) and then **quartz** oscillators (Marrison, 1927) brought stabilities of 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹⁰ per day. Essen and Parry's **cesium beam** standard at the National Physical Laboratory (1955) ⟨H⟩ achieved 10⁻¹⁰ and led, in 1967, to the redefinition of the SI second; commercial cesium clocks reach 10⁻¹³–10⁻¹⁴, hydrogen masers 10⁻¹⁵ over hours, and **optical lattice and ion clocks** now demonstrate instabilities and systematic uncertainties near 10⁻¹⁸ — a level at which a clock raised by 1 cm runs measurably faster, the basis of *chronometric levelling* ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md), [Chapter 73](ch73-open-problems.md)). A redefinition of the second in terms of an optical transition is anticipated around 2030.

Clock quality is described not by a single number but by the **Allan deviation** $\sigma_y(\tau)$ (Allan 1966): the stability of the fractional frequency $y$ over averaging time τ (see Mathematics). A GNSS receiver's temperature-compensated crystal (TCXO) has $\sigma_y \sim 10^{-9}$ at 1 s and drifts by parts in 10⁶ with temperature, which is why it must be disciplined by the satellite signals; an oven-controlled crystal (OCXO) reaches 10⁻¹¹–10⁻¹² at 1–100 s and is the usual choice for timing-critical sensor heads; a chip-scale atomic clock (CSAC) holds 10⁻¹¹ with a few-hour holdover good to about a microsecond. For a mapping sensor, the question is holdover: how long after losing the reference does the local clock stay within budget? An OCXO drifting at 10⁻¹⁰ accumulates 8.6 μs per day, 0.36 μs per hour — irrelevant for a 60 m/s aircraft (22 μm) but not for a 1500 m/s acoustic ranging system where 1 μs is 1.5 mm, nor for a time-of-flight laser rangefinder where 1 ns is 15 cm of range.

### 6.2.1 Relativity in GNSS

GNSS is the one everyday technology in which both special and general relativity must be engineered for (Ashby 2003). A GPS satellite clock in a ~20 200 km orbit moves at ~3.87 km/s, so time dilation slows it by about 7.2 μs/day relative to a ground clock; it sits higher in Earth's gravitational potential, which speeds it by about 45.8 μs/day; the net is a gain of about **38.6 μs/day**, a fractional frequency offset of $+4.465 \times 10^{-10}$. Uncorrected, this would accumulate a ranging error of $c \times 38.6\ \mu\text{s} \approx 11.6$ km per day, so satellite clocks are set to 10.229 999 995 43 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz before launch. Two smaller effects are corrected in the receiver: the **eccentricity** term (the clock's speed and potential vary around a non-circular orbit; up to ~46 ns, i.e., ~14 m, for $e = 0.02$) and the **Sagnac** effect (the Earth rotates during the ~67 ms signal flight, moving the receiver by up to ~30 m; corrected by computing in an Earth-centered inertial frame or adding $\omega_E (x_s y_r - y_s x_r)/c^2$, up to ~133 ns). Ground clocks differ in rate with height by $g h / c^2 \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-16}$ per meter — negligible for navigation, decisive for optical clocks. The relativistic corrections are *part of the time scale*: GPST is defined for a clock at rest on the geoid, and TAI/TT are coordinate times whose definitions (IERS Conventions 2010, ch. 10) include these terms.

## 6.3 Synchronization on moving platforms

A mapping system is a set of clocks: the GNSS receiver (disciplined to GPST), the IMU (its own oscillator, typically 100–2000 Hz), the laser scanner or sonar (its own high-rate clock), cameras (often no clock at all, only an exposure signal), and a logging computer running an operating system with a clock that is only loosely tied to anything. The product's geometry requires that each measurement be associated with the platform's position and attitude *at the instant of measurement*, so every sensor must share a time base to within a tolerance set by the platform dynamics.

### 6.3.1 Mechanisms

- **PPS (pulse per second)**: a hardware edge emitted by the GNSS receiver at the top of each GPST (or UTC) second, accurate to tens of nanoseconds (survey receivers specify ~20–50 ns RMS; cable propagation adds ~5 ns/m). It provides the *tick* but not the *label*.
- **NMEA ZDA / GGA / RMC**: serial sentences that provide the label — the UTC date and time of the most recent PPS — but arrive with a latency of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, variable from sentence to sentence. The correct pattern is "PPS for the edge, ZDA for the name of the edge"; the incorrect pattern — stamping sensor data with the time the ZDA sentence *arrived* — introduces the latency as a timing error. ZDA is preferred over GGA because it carries the full date; GGA carries only time of day.
- **Event marks / event inputs**: survey GNSS receivers and most INS units accept a TTL pulse from a sensor (camera mid-exposure, laser scan start, sonar transmit) and log its GPST to sub-microsecond precision. This is the gold standard for cameras; the critical detail is *which* instant the pulse marks (shutter open, mid-exposure, flash sync) and the camera's internal delay, which for some DSLRs is tens of milliseconds and variable.
- **PTP / IEEE 1588** (2008; revised 2019): network time transfer with hardware timestamping at the Ethernet PHY, achieving sub-microsecond (with hardware support, tens of nanoseconds) synchronization across a LAN. Modern multibeam sonars, lidar scanners, and INS units support PTP with a GNSS-disciplined grandmaster; the 2019 revision added security options and improved profiles. Without hardware timestamping, PTP degrades toward NTP performance.
- **NTP** (Mills, from 1979 ⟨H⟩; RFC 5905 for version 4, 2010): software time transfer over networks, typically ~1 ms on a LAN and 1–50 ms over the internet, with chrony and ntpd as the common daemons. Adequate for logging computers and metadata, not for sensor fusion. A GNSS receiver with PPS feeding chrony on a local machine can discipline the OS clock to a few microseconds, which is adequate for many vessel systems if — and only if — the sensor data are timestamped in the kernel on arrival rather than in user space.
- **Free-running with post-hoc alignment**: when a sensor cannot be synchronized (consumer cameras, some bathymetric single-beams, phones), time offsets are estimated after the fact by correlation — matching a camera's image motion with INS angular rate, or a sonar's depth profile with a second sensor's. This recovers constant offsets well and drifts poorly, and it should be reported as a calibration with its own uncertainty.

### 6.3.2 Latency becomes geometry

If a measurement is stamped $\Delta t$ late (or early), the platform position used for it is wrong by the displacement during $\Delta t$:

$$\Delta x = v\,\Delta t \quad\text{(along track)},\qquad \Delta\theta = \dot\theta\,\Delta t \quad\text{(attitude)},\qquad \Delta z = \dot z\,\Delta t \quad\text{(heave)}.$$

An attitude error $\Delta\theta$ becomes a position error of $R\,\Delta\theta$ at range $R$, so for a lidar or multibeam with a long lever to the ground, *attitude latency* is often the larger term:

| Platform | Speed $v$ | Typical angular rate $\dot\theta$ | $\Delta t$ | Along-track $v\Delta t$ | Attitude $R\,\dot\theta\,\Delta t$ (range R) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-wing lidar | 60 m/s | 2°/s (turbulence) | 1 ms | 0.06 m | 0.035 m at R = 1000 m |
| Fixed-wing lidar | 60 m/s | 2°/s | 10 ms | 0.60 m | 0.35 m |
| Survey vessel, multibeam | 4 m/s (8 kn) | 5°/s roll in seaway | 10 ms | 0.04 m | 0.044 m at R = 50 m |
| Survey vessel, multibeam | 4 m/s | 5°/s | 100 ms (serial NMEA) | 0.40 m | 0.44 m |
| Mobile mapping car | 25 m/s | 10°/s (turn) | 1 ms | 0.025 m | 0.009 m at R = 50 m |
| UAS lidar | 8 m/s | 20°/s (gusts) | 5 ms | 0.04 m | 0.087 m at R = 50 m |
| UAS photogrammetry (no event mark) | 8 m/s | — | 50 ms (camera delay) | 0.40 m | — |

Heave adds a vertical term: a vessel heaving with amplitude 1 m at a 6 s period has a peak vertical velocity of $2\pi \times 1/6 \approx 1.05$ m/s, so a 100 ms latency misassigns heave by up to 10 cm — directly into depth. Multibeam further requires separate time tags for *transmit* and *receive*: in 1000 m of water the two-way travel time is about 1.33 s, during which a vessel at 4 m/s moves 5.3 m and may roll several degrees, so the beam's launch attitude and the receive attitude differ and both must be known ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)).

Timing errors have a recognizable signature. A constant latency produces errors that *reverse sign with heading*: on reciprocal lines the along-track displacement is opposite, so a feature surveyed in both directions appears doubled, separated by $2v\Delta t$. Attitude latency produces cross-track errors that correlate with roll rate, visible as a wobble along the swath edges in rough conditions and absent in calm ones. These are what the lidar boresight/latency calibration and the multibeam **patch test** (which includes an explicit latency line run at two speeds over a slope) are designed to estimate ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)).

> **Worked example.** A multibeam patch-test latency line is run over a 10° slope at 3 m/s and again at 6 m/s in the same direction. The slope's apparent along-track position differs by 0.45 m between the two runs. Since $\Delta x = v\Delta t$, the difference is $(6 - 3)\,\Delta t = 0.45$ m, giving $\Delta t = 0.15$ s — a 150 ms latency, typical of position fed by serial NMEA without PPS. At survey speed (4 m/s) this displaces every sounding 0.60 m along track; on the 10° slope that is a depth error of $0.60 \tan 10° = 0.11$ m, which on its own consumes most of a Special Order TVU budget in 20 m of water ($\sqrt{0.25^2 + (0.0075 \times 20)^2} = 0.29$ m at 95 %, i.e., 0.15 m at 1σ).

## 6.4 Epochs of coordinates and datums

A point on the Earth's surface moves. Tectonic plates carry it at 1–10 cm/yr horizontally; glacial isostatic adjustment lifts Hudson Bay and Fennoscandia by about 1 cm/yr and lowers their forebulges; subsidence from groundwater and hydrocarbon extraction drops parts of the Central Valley, Jakarta, and the Gulf Coast by centimeters to decimeters per year; earthquakes displace regions by meters in seconds ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md), [Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)). A coordinate is therefore a function of time, and a complete position is **coordinate + epoch + velocity** (or a deformation model): $\mathbf X(t) = \mathbf X(t_0) + \dot{\mathbf X}\,(t - t_0)$ plus any co-seismic or post-seismic terms. The **epoch** is the instant $t_0$ to which the coordinate refers, conventionally written as a decimal year (2010.00 = 2010-01-01 00:00 UTC; 2020.5 ≈ 2 July 2020).

Datums handle this in three ways:

- **Plate-fixed (static) datums** attach the frame to a plate so that coordinates of points on that plate change slowly or not at all. **NAD83(2011)** is fixed to the North American plate and its coordinates are published at **epoch 2010.00**; a GNSS observation made in 2024 must be transformed from the ITRF of the observation (at epoch 2024.x) to NAD83 *and* propagated to 2010.00 using a velocity model (NGS's HTDP), or the result will be displaced by 14 years of intraplate motion — small (millimeters per year) in the stable interior, several centimeters per year in California and Alaska. **ETRS89** is fixed to the stable part of the Eurasian plate at epoch 1989.0; **GDA94** was fixed to the Australian plate at epoch 1994.0.
- **Global, time-dependent frames**: **ITRF2020** (Altamimi et al. 2023) and the WGS 84 realizations aligned to it give each station a position, a velocity, and (new in ITRF2014/2020) post-seismic deformation models and seasonal terms; coordinates are valid *at the stated epoch* and must be propagated to any other. Two ITRF coordinates of the same monument at epochs ten years apart differ by a decade of plate motion.
- **Dynamic and semi-dynamic datums**: Australia is the canonical case. The Australian plate moves north-northeast at about 7 cm/yr, so by 2020 GDA94 coordinates were about 1.8 m from their ITRF equivalents — larger than the accuracy of a phone. **GDA2020** reset the static datum to ITRF2014 at epoch 2020.0, and the **Australian Terrestrial Reference Frame (ATRF)** provides a fully dynamic alternative in which coordinates carry the observation epoch and a plate-motion model does the rest. New Zealand's **NZGD2000** is semi-dynamic: nominally at epoch 2000.0 with a national deformation model, including patches for the Canterbury and Kaikōura earthquakes, that converts between observation and reference epochs. The forthcoming US **NATRF2022** (and its Pacific, Caribbean, and Mariana siblings) will be plate-fixed with a published Euler pole and an **Intra-Frame Velocity Model**, reference epoch 2020.00, and an expectation that users state epochs — the "dynamic datums" preview that [Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md) and [Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md) develop.

For elevation data specifically, the vertical component of motion is what matters, and it is less well modeled than the horizontal. Vertical land motion of a few mm/yr is common and invisible in a single survey but accumulates to decimeters over the life of a vertical datum (NAVD 88 was realized at a 1991 adjustment; in parts of the Gulf Coast and Central Valley the ground has since moved by more than a meter relative to it). A DEM's heights therefore carry the *acquisition* epoch as an implicit property, and comparing DEMs or control from different decades requires either a VLM model or an explicit acknowledgment that the datum has drifted.

### 6.4.1 Tidal datum epochs

Tidal datums — MLLW, MHW, MSL, LAT — are *averages over a defined interval*, and that interval is an epoch of a different kind. The United States **National Tidal Datum Epoch (NTDE)** is the 19-year period 1983–2001 over which hourly water levels are averaged to define MLLW and the other datums; 19 years (actually 18.6) spans the lunar nodal cycle so that the averages are not biased by its ±4 % modulation of tidal range. NOAA updates the NTDE roughly every 20–25 years to track sea-level rise and VLM; as of this writing 1983–2001 remains the official epoch, with a 2002–2020 analysis completed and adoption of the next NTDE planned for later in the decade (NOAA has indicated about 2029). When it is adopted, MLLW will rise by the local relative sea-level trend times the ~19-year shift between epoch midpoints — typically 5–10 cm along most US coasts, more where subsidence is rapid — so every chart depth, topobathymetric merge, and MLLW-referenced flood map changes by that amount without any physical change in the seafloor. LAT, used as chart datum in much of the world, is computed by harmonic prediction over a 19-year span (or by nodal-factor correction of a shorter record) and is likewise tied to the span used. The point for DEM work: a depth "relative to MLLW" is relative to MLLW *of a stated epoch*, and metadata should name it ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md), [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

> **Case file.** GDA94 to GDA2020 in Australia. Between 1994 and 2020 the Australian plate moved about 1.8 m, so GDA94 coordinates differed from contemporary GNSS (ITRF/WGS 84 G1762) coordinates by that amount — enough that satellite imagery, lidar, and precise-point-positioned survey data visibly disagreed with the national cadastre. The Intergovernmental Committee on Surveying and Mapping (ICSM) introduced GDA2020 (gazetted 2017) as a new static datum aligned to ITRF2014 at epoch 2020.0, together with conformal and conformal-plus-distortion grids for the 1.8 m transformation, and ATRF as the dynamic companion. The GDA2020 Technical Manual (ICSM, v1.8, 2023) documents the ~7 cm/yr rate and the transformation; a dataset labeled simply "GDA" without "94" or "2020" is ambiguous by up to 1.8 m.

## 6.5 Temporal metadata for elevation

An elevation product has several dates, and conflating them is a common metadata failure:

- **Acquisition start and end**: the interval during which the measurements were made — for an airborne survey, typically days to weeks; for a national program, years; for a global DEM composite, a decade or more. The start and end bound the surface's *validity*; a DEM "of 2020" acquired between November 2019 and March 2021 represents no single moment, and in a region with 10 cm/yr subsidence its own tiles are inconsistent by more than a decimeter.
- **Per-pixel or per-tile dates**: for composites and mosaics, a companion raster giving the acquisition date of the source that supplied each cell (Copernicus DEM provides per-tile source dates; ArcticDEM strips and mosaics carry acquisition dates per strip; the USGS 3DEP "project date" is per work unit). This is the only way a user can reason about change detection across a composite, and products without it should be treated as of unknown epoch for change work ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).
- **Processing and publication dates**: when the product was generated and released, which may follow acquisition by years and which determines which geoid model, datum realization, and software were used. A DEM acquired in 2012 and reprocessed in 2023 with GEOID18 has heights that differ from its 2013 release.
- **Reference epochs**: the epoch of the horizontal datum (NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00), of the vertical datum (NAVD 88 as realized; NTDE 1983–2001 for MLLW), and of the geoid model.
- **Validity window**: the interval during which the product may be assumed to represent the surface for a given purpose — not a property of the data but of the use ([Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md)): a coastal dune DEM may be invalid for navigation after the next storm season; a bedrock DEM is valid for decades; an open-pit mine DEM is stale in a week.

ISO 19115 provides the fields (`temporalExtent`, `dateType` of creation/publication/revision, and in 19115-1 an `acquisition` package); INSPIRE and STAC ([Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md)) require `datetime` or `start_datetime`/`end_datetime` per item. The practical minimum for any DEM delivered or catalogued is: acquisition start and end in ISO 8601 with time zone, per-source dates for composites, processing date, and the epochs of all datums and models used ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

## 6.6 Time in formats

- **LAS/LAZ**: each point carries a double-precision `GPS Time`. In LAS 1.0 this was **GPS week time** (seconds of the week, 0–604 800, with no week number stored — the week must come from the flight log). Since LAS 1.2, bit 0 of the header's **Global Encoding** field indicates whether the time is week time (0) or **Adjusted Standard GPS Time** (1), defined as continuous GPST seconds since 1980-01-06 minus 1 × 10⁹ s; the offset keeps the value small enough to preserve microsecond precision in a double (10⁹ s with double precision gives ~10⁻⁷ s resolution; full GPST would be ~1.4 × 10⁹ s and still fine, but the convention is fixed). GPST seconds = 1 × 10⁹ s corresponds to 2011-09-14 01:46:40 GPST, so Adjusted Standard times are negative for earlier data. A file with Global Encoding bit 0 unset but times around 4 × 10⁸ (a 2024 acquisition in Adjusted Standard time) is mislabeled; conversely, week-time values interpreted as Adjusted Standard place the data in 2011. PDAL and LAStools read the flag; many home-grown parsers do not.
- **GSF (Generic Sensor Format)** for multibeam: time as a `struct timespec` — seconds since the Unix epoch (UTC) plus nanoseconds — per ping, with the leap-second ambiguity that Unix time carries. **Kongsberg .all/.kmall**, **Reson s7k**, and other native sonar formats store UTC date and time of day in milliseconds or microseconds, often with a separate clock-status datagram recording the PPS/ZDA synchronization state, which is worth checking before trusting the timestamps.
- **SBET (Smoothed Best Estimate of Trajectory)** from Applanix POSPac and similar: binary records of GPS seconds of week (double), latitude, longitude, ellipsoidal height, velocities, roll, pitch, heading, with the week number *not* stored in the file — it comes from the file name or the project. Matching an SBET to LAS points requires converting one to the other's convention and knowing the week; a one-week error is $604\,800$ s and is usually obvious, but a mis-set week at a Saturday/Sunday midnight boundary mid-flight splits a mission.
- **EXIF** in images: `DateTimeOriginal` is a *local* time string with one-second resolution and, before EXIF 2.31 (2016), no time-zone field; `SubSecTimeOriginal` adds fractional seconds where the camera supports it; `GPSTimeStamp`/`GPSDateStamp` carry UTC from an attached receiver. Cameras' internal clocks drift by seconds per day and are set by hand, so EXIF time should be used only to associate images with an externally recorded event-mark time, never as the time itself. Photogrammetric workflows that geotag images by interpolating a trajectory at the EXIF time inherit the camera clock's offset as an along-track position error ($v\,\Delta t$: at 8 m/s and 1 s of clock error, 8 m).
- **NetCDF / CF conventions**: time is a numeric coordinate with a `units` attribute of the form `"seconds since 1970-01-01T00:00:00Z"` (or `days since …`) and a `calendar` attribute (`standard`/`gregorian`, `proleptic_gregorian`, `julian`, `noleap`, `360_day`, …). Two traps: the reference date string is parsed by software with varying rigor (missing time zones default to UTC; `days since 0000-01-01` is a notorious case that depends on the calendar), and CF time is a *calendar* encoding like Unix time — leap seconds are not representable. Zarr follows CF via xarray.
- **BAG / S-102**: ISO 19115 metadata with acquisition dates as ISO 8601 strings; S-102 adds `dateTimeOfFirstRecord`/`dateTimeOfLastRecord` and an issue date in the product header.
- **RINEX**: observation epochs in GPST (or the system time stated in the header) as calendar fields; the header's `LEAP SECONDS` record is the receiver's current count — a common place to find a stale value in old receivers.

> **Try it.** Decode a LAS file's time convention and convert its first point's time to UTC, applying the leap-second count correctly, with PDAL and Astropy.
>

## Then & now

Positioning and timing have been one problem since the longitude prize: the Board of Longitude's 1714 challenge was solved by a clock (Harrison's H4, 1761 ⟨H⟩) as much as by lunar tables, and nineteenth-century geodetic surveys transferred time by telegraph to fix longitude differences between observatories (the US Coast Survey's "American method" of the 1840s–1850s). Standard time zones (railways, 1883 in North America; the Washington Meridian Conference, 1884) and nautical zone time (1917 ⟨H⟩) made timestamps comparable across a continent and an ocean. Pendulum (1656 ⟨H⟩) → chronometer (1761) → quartz (1927) → cesium (1955 ⟨H⟩) → optical (2000s–) each improved stability by two to four orders of magnitude, and each shifted the definition of the second: from a fraction of the mean solar day, to a fraction of the tropical year 1900 (ephemeris time, 1956–1967), to the cesium transition (1967). TAI was formally defined in 1971 and UTC with integer leap seconds began in 1972; GPS time began in 1980 and became, through receivers' PPS outputs, the most widely distributed precise time scale in history. Unix time (1970 ⟨H⟩) and NTP (1979 ⟨H⟩; RFC 958 in 1985; NTPv4 in RFC 5905, 2010) gave computers a common, if leap-second-blind, clock; PTP/IEEE 1588 (2002, 2008, 2019) brought sub-microsecond synchronization to Ethernet and thereby to sonar and lidar systems that had depended on dedicated PPS wiring.

For coordinates, the shift is from *static* to *epoch-tagged*: NAD 27 and NAD 83(1986) had no epoch; ITRF88 onward published velocities; NAD83(CORS96) and NAD83(2011) attached epochs (2002.00 and 2010.00); GDA2020 (2017) and NATRF2022 make the epoch a first-class part of the coordinate; ATRF and NZGD2000's deformation model make it operational. The CGPM's 2022 resolution to end leap seconds by 2035 will, if executed, remove the most common timing discontinuity from the systems this book describes — and introduce a transition that every leap-second table, firmware, and format will have to survive.

## Mathematics

**Latency to position and attitude.** For platform velocity $\mathbf v$, angular rate $\boldsymbol\omega$, and timing error $\Delta t$: position error $\Delta\mathbf x = \mathbf v\,\Delta t$; attitude error $\Delta\boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\omega\,\Delta t$; resulting ground-point error at lever/range vector $\mathbf r$ is $\Delta\mathbf x + \Delta\boldsymbol\theta \times \mathbf r$, with magnitude $\le v\Delta t + \omega R\,\Delta t$.

**Patch-test latency from two speeds** over a feature: $\Delta t = \dfrac{\Delta x_{\text{apparent}}}{v_2 - v_1}$.

**Time scales.** $\text{GPST} = \text{TAI} - 19\ \text{s}$; $\text{UTC} = \text{TAI} - \Delta_{AT}(t)$ with $\Delta_{AT} = 37$ s since 2017-01-01; $\text{GPST} - \text{UTC} = \Delta_{AT} - 19$ s $= 18$ s. LAS Adjusted Standard GPS Time: $t_{\text{adj}} = t_{\text{GPST}} - 10^9$ s. GPS week $W$ and seconds of week $s$: $t_{\text{GPST}} = 604\,800\,W + s$; the 10-bit broadcast week is $W \bmod 1024$. Decimal-year epoch: $t = Y + \dfrac{\text{(day of year)} - 1 + \text{fraction of day}}{365 \text{ or } 366}$.

**Relativistic clock rate for a circular orbit** of radius $r$ (Ashby 2003): fractional frequency offset of the satellite clock relative to a clock on the geoid
$$\frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx \frac{W_0}{c^2} - \frac{3GM}{2rc^2}, \qquad W_0 \approx 6.2637\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}$$
(the geoid's effective potential magnitude, including the rotational term; $\Phi_0 = -W_0$); for GPS, $r = 26\,562$ km gives $3GM/(2r) = 2.251\times10^{7}$ m²s⁻² and $\Delta f/f = +4.465\times10^{-10}$, i.e., $+38.6\ \mu$s/day. Eccentricity correction $\Delta t_r = -\dfrac{2\sqrt{GM\,a}}{c^2}\,e\sin E$ (up to ~46 ns for $e = 0.02$). Sagnac correction $\Delta t_S = \dfrac{\omega_E}{c^2}(x_s y_r - y_s x_r)$ (up to ~133 ns). Gravitational rate difference between two ground clocks separated in height by $\Delta h$: $\Delta f/f = g\,\Delta h/c^2 \approx 1.09\times10^{-16}$ per meter.

**Allan variance** (Allan 1966). For fractional frequency samples $\bar y_k$ averaged over consecutive intervals of length τ,
$$\sigma_y^2(\tau) = \tfrac12\,\big\langle(\bar y_{k+1} - \bar y_k)^2\big\rangle,$$
or equivalently from time-error samples $x_k$ spaced τ apart, $\sigma_y^2(\tau) = \dfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\langle(x_{k+2} - 2x_{k+1} + x_k)^2\rangle$. White frequency noise gives $\sigma_y \propto \tau^{-1/2}$, flicker frequency a flat floor, random-walk frequency $\tau^{+1/2}$; the time error accumulated over holdover τ is of order $\tau\,\sigma_y(\tau)$. The overlapping and modified Allan deviations are the standard reporting forms (IEEE Std 1139).

**Coordinate propagation in time.** $\mathbf X(t) = \mathbf X(t_0) + \dot{\mathbf X}(t - t_0) + \sum_i \delta\mathbf X_{\text{PSD},i}(t)$ for ITRF2020-style post-seismic terms; for plate-rigid motion, $\dot{\mathbf X} = \boldsymbol\Omega \times \mathbf X$ with the plate's Euler vector $\boldsymbol\Omega$.

## Validation & uncertainty

Timing errors are geometric errors in disguise, and they are validated the same way as other geometric errors — by redundancy — with the added advantage that their signatures are distinctive.

**How timing errors arise.** (1) *Scale mix-ups*: GPST vs UTC (18 s), week vs adjusted standard time (10⁹ s), week number errors (604 800 s), local vs UTC (hours), Julian vs Gregorian (10–13 days). These are large, discrete, and usually catastrophic rather than subtle; they are caught by sanity checks on dates and by the impossibility of the resulting geometry. (2) *Constant latency*: a fixed delay between a sensor's measurement and the time it is stamped, from serial transmission, internal buffering, or camera shutter lag — typically 1–200 ms. (3) *Variable latency / jitter*: operating-system scheduling, USB polling, network queueing — milliseconds, random. (4) *Clock drift*: an unsynchronized sensor clock running fast or slow — seconds per day for a camera, microseconds per day for an OCXO — producing a latency that grows linearly through the mission. (5) *Leap-second events*: a mission spanning 23:59:60 UTC on 30 June or 31 December, during which different components may apply the step at different instants (receiver firmware, logging software, post-processing tables), producing a 1 s discontinuity — 60 m at aircraft speed — in part of the data. (6) *Epoch mismatches* between coordinate sets: decimeters to meters depending on plate velocity and elapsed years.

**How they propagate.** Through $v\,\Delta t$ and $\omega R\,\Delta t$ (§6.3.2, Mathematics) into position and attitude, and from there into every derived product. A latency that is constant appears as a *systematic* along-track offset whose sign depends on heading; a drift appears as a tilt or progressive misregistration along the mission; jitter appears as along-track noise with amplitude $v\sigma_{\Delta t}$ — at 60 m/s and 1 ms jitter, 6 cm of along-track scatter, which becomes vertical scatter of 6 cm × slope. For a sonar, a timing error in the *sound-speed-corrected ray trace* also matters: a 1 ms error in two-way travel time at 1500 m/s is 0.75 m of range.

**How to test.** Reciprocal-heading overlap is the primary diagnostic: survey the same feature on opposite headings and measure the along-track separation ($2v\Delta t$ for constant latency). The multibeam patch test formalizes this with a dedicated latency line pair at two speeds; lidar calibration flights include reciprocal and cross lines over buildings whose roof edges reveal along-track shifts; mobile mapping uses targets passed in both directions. For cameras, photograph a running stopwatch or an LED timing board triggered by PPS; the difference between the image's event-mark time and the displayed time is the shutter latency, repeatable to a few ms. For whole-system checks, compare strip-to-strip residuals as a function of heading and of angular rate: a latency error correlates with $\dot\theta$; a boresight error does not. Statistical checks on timestamps themselves are cheap and should be routine: the distribution of inter-sample intervals (should be a spike at the nominal period; outliers reveal dropouts and jitter), monotonicity (a timestamp that goes backward indicates a clock reset or a week rollover), and the range of dates against the flight log.

**Epoch validation.** For coordinates, compare control-point coordinates propagated to a common epoch with HTDP (North America), the ITRF/plate-motion models, or the national deformation model, and look for residual offsets aligned with the plate velocity vector; an offset in the direction of plate motion with magnitude ≈ velocity × years is diagnostic of an epoch mismatch. For tidal datums, confirm which NTDE the published datum values refer to, and if merging data referenced to different epochs, apply the published datum-shift values.

**What to report.** The time scale and format of every file; the synchronization mechanism for each sensor (PPS + ZDA, event mark, PTP, free-running + post-hoc) and its estimated residual latency and uncertainty; the leap-second table version; the acquisition start/end and any leap-second or rollover events within them; the epoch of every coordinate set and the velocity or deformation model used to propagate; and the tidal datum epoch for any water-level-referenced heights.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Timing contributions to point position for an airborne lidar at 60 m/s, range 1000 m, moderate turbulence (2°/s), with a well-integrated system (illustrative; obtain actuals from the calibration report).
>
> | Source | $\Delta t$ (1σ) | Horizontal effect | Vertical effect (10 % slope) | Character |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | PPS edge + cable | 50 ns | 3 μm | — | Negligible |
> | Scanner time-tag resolution | 1 μs | 60 μm | — | Negligible |
> | IMU–scanner residual latency after calibration | 0.2 ms | 12 mm along track; 7 mm attitude | ~2 mm | Systematic per heading |
> | IMU data-age jitter | 0.1 ms | 6 mm | 0.6 mm | Random |
> | Laser range timing (1 ns ≈ 15 cm) | 0.1 ns | — | 15 mm in range | Random; this is the "range noise" of Chapter 5 |
> | Camera event mark (if used) | 0.5 ms | 30 mm | — | Systematic |
> | **Total (RSS)** | | **≈ 15 mm along track** | **≈ 15 mm** | Small *because* synchronized; without PPS the first row becomes 100 ms and 6 m |

The budget's lesson is that a properly synchronized system makes timing a negligible term, and an improperly synchronized one makes it the dominant term; there is little middle ground, and the difference is a cable and a configuration flag.

## Software

**Open source:** **chrony** and **ntpd** — NTP clients/servers; chrony's PPS and GNSS reference-clock support (via gpsd shared memory or serial PPS) disciplines a Linux host to a few microseconds. **linuxptp (ptp4l, phc2sys)** — IEEE 1588 with hardware-timestamp support; the usual grandmaster/slave stack on survey vessels and sensor racks. **gpsd** — parses NMEA/binary receiver output, exposes PPS to chrony/ntpd, and reports leap-second status; caveat: serial latency on its NMEA path is exactly what PPS exists to bypass. **NASA NAIF SPICE** ⟨H⟩ — the planetary community's time utilities (`str2et`, `et2utc`, `deltet`, leap-second kernels), rigorous about TAI/TT/TDB/UTC ([Chapter 67](ch67-planetary-dems.md)); its leap-second kernel must be kept current. **Astropy `time`** — conversions among UTC, TAI, TT, GPS, UT1 (IERS tables auto-downloaded), with leap seconds handled correctly, including 23:59:60. **RTKLIB** — GNSS processing with explicit GPST/UTC/GLONASS/BDT handling and week-rollover logic in `rtkcmn.c`; a good reference implementation to read. **PDAL** — reads/writes LAS Global Encoding and GpsTime; `filters.sort` and `filters.info` for timestamp sanity checks. **PROJ** — epoch-aware operations (`+t_epoch`, time-dependent Helmert, deformation grids such as NZGD2000 and GDA2020 conformal+distortion). **MB-System** — honors GSF/Kongsberg time fields and includes latency estimation in its patch-test tools. **Free but closed:** NGS **HTDP** (epoch propagation and frame transformations for North America); Geoscience Australia's GDA2020/ATRF tools; LINZ's NZGD2000 deformation-model service. **Commercial:** Applanix POSPac and NovAtel Inertial Explorer (trajectory processing in GPS week + seconds, with PPS/event-mark diagnostics); CARIS HIPS and QPS Qimera (patch-test latency tools; caveat: latency is often left at zero because the test was skipped); Terrasolid TerraMatch (estimates trajectory–scanner time offsets during strip adjustment).

## Standards & guides

- **BIPM** — *Circular T* (monthly TAI/UTC realization) and the SI Brochure (9th ed., 2019) for the definition of the second; the leap-second announcements come from **IERS Bulletin C**.
- **CGPM 2022, Resolution 4** — on the use and future development of UTC; sets 2035 as the deadline for increasing the UT1 − UTC tolerance (i.e., ending leap seconds).
- **IERS Conventions (2010)**, Petit & Luzum (eds.), IERS Technical Note 36 — time scales, relativistic conventions, reference-frame and epoch definitions, Earth orientation.
- **IEEE Std 1588-2019** — Precision Time Protocol (PTP); and **IEEE Std 1139-2008** — standard definitions of frequency and time stability (Allan deviation).
- **RFC 5905 (2010)** — Network Time Protocol version 4.
- **ISO 8601-1:2019 and 8601-2:2019** — date and time representations; use `Z` or an explicit offset.
- **ISO 19115-1:2014** — temporal extent and date-type metadata elements; **ISO 19111:2019** — coordinate epoch and dynamic datums.
- **IS-GPS-200 (current revision N, 2022)** — GPS interface specification: GPST, week numbering, UTC parameters in the navigation message.
- **ASPRS LAS 1.4 – R15** — Global Encoding bit 0 and GPS time definitions.
- **CF Conventions 1.11 (2024)** — time coordinate units and calendars for NetCDF/Zarr.
- **NOAA CO-OPS, *Tidal Datums and Their Applications* (Gill & Schultz 2000, NOAA Special Publication NOS CO-OPS 1)** and the NTDE documentation — definition and epoch of US tidal datums (1983–2001 remains official; next epoch adoption planned about 2029).

## Pitfalls

- **LAS week time mistaken for Adjusted Standard GPS Time (or vice versa)** → Global Encoding bit 0 ignored by a parser or set wrongly by a writer → check the magnitude of the time values against the header creation date; values < 604 800 are week time, values near 4 × 10⁸ (2020s) are adjusted standard; fix the flag, not the data.
- **18 s GPST–UTC offset applied twice, or not at all** → trajectory in GPST, sensor log in receiver-UTC → an along-track displacement of $18v$ (1.1 km at 60 m/s) that is heading-dependent; sanity-check by overlaying reciprocal lines.
- **Unsynchronized camera clock used to geotag images** → EXIF time trusted as event time → along-track error $v\,\Delta t$ that grows with the camera's drift; use event marks, or calibrate the offset against a PPS-triggered display at the start and end of the mission.
- **Serial NMEA arrival time used as the time of the fix** → PPS not wired, or software stamps on sentence arrival → 50–300 ms latency, variable; visible as $2v\Delta t$ doubling on reciprocal lines; wire the PPS and set the latency in the acquisition software.
- **Attitude latency ignored because position latency was calibrated** → the two can differ (separate paths from INS to logger) → look for cross-track residuals correlated with roll rate; include a rough-water or turbulent segment in the calibration.
- **Leap-second mishandling at a year boundary** → missions spanning 31 December with mixed firmware/tables → a 1 s step in part of the trajectory; check for a 60 m (aircraft) or 4 m (vessel) along-track discontinuity near 00:00 UTC and confirm the receiver's leap-second count in the RINEX header.
- **GPS week rollover in old receivers or firmware** → 10-bit week counter → dates in 1999/2000 or 1980 for 2019+ data; impossible dates in metadata are the first symptom; update firmware or correct the week by +1024.
- **Comparing coordinates from different epochs on a fast plate** → epoch omitted from metadata → offsets aligned with plate velocity (≈ 7 cm/yr × years in Australia; 1.8 m for GDA94 vs GDA2020); demand the epoch, propagate with the appropriate model, and record the operation.
- **Treating NAD83(2011) coordinates as "now"** → the epoch 2010.00 is invisible in most software → in California or Alaska the ground has moved several decimeters since; propagate observed ITRF coordinates to 2010.00 before transforming, and state both epochs.
- **MLLW from different tidal epochs merged** → datum values updated between surveys → 5–10 cm step at the boundary; record the NTDE for every water-level-referenced dataset and apply the published epoch shift.
- **Local time without offset in metadata** → ISO 8601 string without `Z` or `±hh:mm` → ambiguous by hours, and by one hour twice a year; always write the offset.
- **Unix/CF time assumed to count SI seconds** → leap seconds are not representable → when computing intervals across a leap second in a 20-year time series, convert through TAI (Astropy or SPICE) rather than subtracting Unix timestamps.

## Key takeaways

- A position is a position *at a time* in a frame: coordinate + epoch + velocity (or deformation model) is the complete statement; on fast plates the epoch term is meters.
- Know your time scale: TAI is continuous; UTC has leap seconds (37 behind TAI, ending by 2035); GPST is 18 s ahead of UTC; Unix and CF time are calendar encodings that cannot represent leap seconds.
- LAS stores either GPS week time or Adjusted Standard GPS Time (GPST − 10⁹ s), signaled by Global Encoding bit 0; check it before anything else.
- Synchronization errors are geometric errors in disguise: $v\,\Delta t$ along track, $\omega R\,\Delta t$ from attitude latency; at survey speeds a millisecond is centimeters and 100 ms is a meter.
- PPS for the tick, ZDA for the label, event marks for cameras, PTP for networks, NTP for logging computers only; the quality of the system is set by its worst-synchronized sensor.
- Relativity is engineered into GNSS: +38.6 μs/day net satellite clock offset, plus eccentricity and Sagnac corrections; the time scale itself is defined for a clock on the geoid.
- Datums have epochs: NAD83(2011) at 2010.00, GDA2020 at 2020.0, ITRF2020 at the observation epoch, NTDE 1983–2001 for US tidal datums; record and propagate them.
- Elevation products have several dates — acquisition start/end, per-source dates in composites, processing date, datum epochs, and a use-dependent validity window; metadata must carry all of them with explicit time zones.
- Validate timing with redundancy: reciprocal lines, two-speed latency tests, residuals versus angular rate, timestamp interval statistics, and impossible-date checks.
