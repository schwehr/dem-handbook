# Chapter 25 — Calibration: targets, stations, networks, benchmarks, reference surfaces

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** The sensors of [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md) to [Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md) produce numbers; this chapter is about the infrastructure — active stations, passive marks, test fields, targets, reference surfaces, and procedures — that makes those numbers traceable to a definition of the metre and comparable with numbers produced by someone else, somewhere else, at another time. It prepares the ground for survey planning in [Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md) and for the ground-truth and accuracy-assessment chapters of Part XI.

**In this chapter.** A measurement without a calibration chain is an opinion with units. You will be able to distinguish calibration from validation and verification and say what traceability to SI means for a range, a position, and a height; use active infrastructure — CORS/IGS, GNSS at tide gauges, NTRIP casters, the GGOS core sites — and know what each actually provides and at what latency; read a benchmark datasheet and judge whether a mark's published height can still be trusted; find and use calibration ranges and targets for cameras, lidars, SARs, radiometers, and altimeters; run a multibeam patch test and a lidar boresight calibration and recognise when the geometry makes a parameter unobservable; verify a sound-speed probe, a GNSS antenna model, an IMU, and a camera; use ICESat-2, GEDI, and stable terrain as global quasi-truth; exploit redundancy — crosslines, strip overlap, loop closures — as continuous self-calibration; and design a calibration program with documentation and recalibration triggers. The Validation section turns the chapter's single most-violated rule — checkpoints must be independent of control — into a test you can apply to your own and others' reports.

## 25.1 Definitions and traceability

The international metrology vocabulary (JCGM 200, the VIM) is precise where everyday usage is not. **Calibration** is the operation that, under specified conditions, establishes the relation between the values indicated by an instrument and the corresponding values realised by standards, with their uncertainties — and then uses that relation to obtain a measurement result from an indication. Calibration *determines* a correction (a boresight angle, a range offset, a lever arm, a sound-speed bias); it does not by itself certify that the instrument is good. **Verification** provides objective evidence that an item fulfils specified requirements — that a lidar system, after calibration, meets the 10 cm RMSE$_z$ the contract demands, for example. **Validation** is verification where the specified requirements are adequate for an intended use — that the 10 cm product actually serves the floodplain model. In DEM work the words are used loosely and often interchangeably; the distinction matters because a calibration report says nothing about fitness, a verification against the specification says nothing about whether the specification was right, and only validation against the use closes the loop ([Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

**Traceability** is the property of a result whereby it can be related to a reference through a documented, unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the uncertainty. For elevation data the chain ends at two SI definitions: the **second**, defined since 1967 by the caesium-133 hyperfine transition frequency, and the **metre**, defined since 1983 as the distance light travels in vacuum in 1/299,792,458 s. Every ranging instrument — lidar, radar, GNSS receiver, total station — is therefore ultimately a clock, and its range traceability runs through its oscillator's calibration and the group refractive index of the medium ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md)). Sonar is the exception: its "metre" is sound speed times time, and sound speed is not an SI constant but a measured property of the water, so sonar traceability runs through the sound-speed sensor's calibration (itself a time-of-flight device over a fixed path, or a CTD whose conductivity and temperature cells are traceable to standards) — which is why Section 25.5 spends so long on it. Positions and heights are traceable through the terrestrial reference frame: the ITRF is realised by the GGOS techniques, national frames are tied to it, CORS coordinates are tied to the national frame, and your RTK rover is tied to the CORS. Each link has an epoch and an uncertainty, and the chain is only as traceable as its least documented link ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

A **calibration certificate** states what was calibrated, against what reference, under what conditions, with what result and uncertainty, by whom, and when; laboratories accredited to **ISO/IEC 17025** issue certificates that other accredited bodies accept. **Calibration intervals** are set by the instrument's drift history and the consequence of being wrong: manufacturers suggest one to two years for total stations and GNSS antennas, but the operative triggers are events — a hard landing, a sensor swap, a firmware change, a transport across the world, an unexplained residual — rather than the calendar (Section 25.9).

> **Definitions that bite.** *Control* and *check* are different roles, not different points. A **control point** (GCP, tie, calibration target) is used to determine parameters of the solution; a **checkpoint** is withheld and used only to assess it. The same physical mark can serve as control on one project and check on another, but never both on the same adjustment; and a point surveyed with the same equipment, from the same base, in the same session as the control shares its systematic errors and is a weak check even if withheld from the adjustment. Section 25.7 and the Validation section give the test.

## 25.2 Active stations

**Continuously operating reference stations (CORS)** are permanently installed GNSS receivers whose data are archived and whose coordinates are determined and monitored in a reference frame. The **International GNSS Service (IGS)**, operational since 1994, coordinates a global network of some 500 stations, computes precise orbits and clocks, and — through the ITRF — defines the frame that everything else hangs on; national networks (the US NOAA **CORS** network of about 1,800 stations contributed by many agencies, Snay & Soler 2008; EUREF Permanent Network; GEONET in Japan with about 1,300 stations; national RTK networks in most countries) densify it. CORS provide three distinct services, with distinct latencies: raw observation files for post-processing (hourly to daily), from which services such as **OPUS** (NGS), **AUSPOS**, **CSRS-PPP**, and **APPS** compute positions of user receivers; **real-time corrections** broadcast by **NTRIP** casters (the RTCM Networked Transport of RTCM via Internet Protocol, standardised 2004) for network RTK and PPP-RTK; and the **coordinate time series** of the stations themselves, which is the record of plate motion, subsidence, and uplift that [Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md) uses and that tells you the velocity you must apply to bring a CORS coordinate from its reference epoch to your survey date ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md), [Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)).

**GNSS at tide gauges** is the junction of the terrestrial and marine height systems. A tide gauge measures sea level relative to its own benchmark; a co-located GNSS measures that benchmark's ellipsoidal height and its vertical land motion, so that the gauge's record can be separated into sea-level change and land motion, and so that chart datum (derived from the gauge) can be related to the ellipsoid — which is what every ellipsoidally referenced bathymetric survey needs ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). **SONEL** (Système d'Observation du Niveau des Eaux Littorales, hosted by the University of La Rochelle) is the GLOSS data centre for GNSS at tide gauges; the IOC's **GLOSS** core network (established 1985) comprises about 300 gauges, and national networks such as NOAA's **NWLON** (around 210 stations, with the datum-computation rules in the NOAA tidal datum manuals) and the UK National Tide Gauge Network provide the primary determinations of tidal datums. The IOC *Manual on Sea Level Measurement and Interpretation* (Manuals and Guides 14, Volumes I–V, 1985–2016) specifies the levelling of gauge sensors to benchmarks, the frequency of checks, and the documentation.

The **core geodetic sites** of **GGOS** (the IAG's Global Geodetic Observing System) co-locate VLBI (which defines the frame's orientation and scale), SLR (which defines its origin at the geocentre), DORIS, and GNSS, with the local ties between instruments surveyed to millimetres. There are a few dozen such sites; the ITRF's accuracy — about 1 mm in origin and 0.1 ppb in scale for ITRF2020, with 0.1 mm/yr stability goals — rests on them. A practitioner rarely touches a core site, but their existence is why "WGS 84 (G2296)" and "ITRF2020 at epoch 2024.5" are not arbitrary labels ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

## 25.3 Passive marks

A **benchmark** (bench mark) is a physical monument — a brass disk in bedrock or a concrete pier, a steel rod driven to refusal in a sleeve, a bolt in a structure — whose height (and, for horizontal marks, position) has been determined and published. The US **National Spatial Reference System (NSRS)** descends from the Coast Survey marks of the early nineteenth century; the "Buttermilk" mark of 1833 ⟨H⟩ is the oldest surviving US survey mark. The **NGS datasheet** for each mark gives its published coordinates and heights with their datum, epoch, method, and order; its description and recovery history; its stability classification (A: bedrock or deep rod; B: probably stable; C: may hold; D: questionable); and, crucially, the date of the last recovery and whether the mark was found, found disturbed, or not found. A published NAVD 88 height is the result of an adjustment of levelling observations, most of which were made between the 1930s and the 1980s, held fixed at one tide-gauge-based point (Father Point/Rimouski) — and it is known to carry a continental tilt and a mean bias of several decimetres relative to the geoid, which is why NAVD 88 is being replaced by the geoid-based NAPGD2022 ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)).

**Marks decay.** Frost heave, soil swelling, subsidence (groundwater withdrawal lowers marks by metres in the San Joaquin Valley, Houston, Jakarta, and Mexico City), tectonic motion (the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake moved marks by metres horizontally and up to 1.2 m vertically; [Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)), construction, and vandalism all change or destroy marks; NGS estimates that a large fraction of historical marks are gone or disturbed, and the published height of a surviving mark is the height *at the epoch of the adjustment*, not today. Treat a benchmark as a hypothesis to be verified: occupy at least two independent marks and check their height difference against the published difference; a disagreement larger than the levelling tolerance means at least one has moved. **Tidal benchmarks** — the marks to which a tide gauge is levelled, typically five or more per station — are the physical realisation of chart datum at that place, and their stability is checked by periodic levelling between them. Horizontal marks (triangulation stations, with their reference marks and azimuth marks) are less used for elevation work but remain valuable as photo-identifiable checkpoints when their monumentation is visible from the air. **OPUS-Share** (NGS, since 2008) publishes user-submitted OPUS solutions for marks, giving modern ellipsoidal heights with documented occupation; a shared solution is not NGS-adjusted control, but it is a dated, documented observation, which is often better than a 1960s datasheet height.

## 25.4 Calibration ranges and targets

Calibration ranges are places where the truth is known well enough, and maintained long enough, that instruments can be compared against it. The **USGS/CEOS test-site catalog** (2007 ⟨H⟩; maintained for the CEOS Working Group on Calibration and Validation) lists sites for geometric and radiometric calibration of satellite sensors; national mapping agencies and universities maintain their own.

**Geometric targets for cameras.** The **Siemens star** (a radial pattern of alternating black and white sectors, in use since the 1930s) measures the resolving power and the point-spread function of an imaging system: the radius at which the sectors blur into grey gives the limiting resolution directly in the image, and the pattern's centre is a precise point. Bar targets (the USAF 1951 tri-bar), edge targets for modulation-transfer-function (MTF) measurement, and large painted resolution targets on airfields and rooftops (a Siemens-star resolution target painted on a rooftop at Google's Mountain View campus in 2010 ⟨H⟩ was imaged by many satellites and aircraft; **QR-code roof targets** have been used since about 2015 ⟨H⟩ to give both a geometric reference and a machine-readable identifier (attributed to NPS in the gis-history list; verify)) serve the same purpose for spaceborne sensors. For **GCPs** the requirements are different: a target must be identifiable to a fraction of a GSD in the image and surveyable to better than the product accuracy on the ground, so checkerboard panels (two black, two white quadrants meeting at the surveyed point), circular coded targets for close range, painted crosses on pavement, and photo-identifiable features (manhole covers, paint-stripe corners) are used, sized at 5–10 GSD across. The **Sioux Falls** aerial camera range at USGS EROS (and before it, the Ohio State and Casa Grande ranges) provides a dense field of surveyed targets for metric camera certification; the Finnish Geospatial Research Institute's **Sjökulla** permanent test field (Honkavaara et al. 2008) and the University of Stuttgart's **Vaihingen/Enz** field are the European references for aerial sensor geometry and radiometry, with permanent targets, resolution patterns, and reflectance panels.

**Lidar calibration sites** are areas with precisely known surfaces — flat, hard, textured ground (a runway or parking lot) for vertical checks; buildings with planar roofs at several orientations and a steep gable for roll, pitch, and heading observability; and overlapping strips flown in opposite directions and across. Most airborne lidar operators maintain a local boresight site near their base and fly it at the start and end of each project (Section 25.6). **Terrestrial laser scanners** are checked on indoor ranges with spheres and planar targets at known distances, following ISO 17123-9.

**Corner reflectors for SAR.** A trihedral **corner reflector** returns radar energy to its source from a wide range of incidence angles with a known radar cross-section ($\sigma = 4\pi a^4/(3\lambda^2)$ for a triangular trihedral of leg $a$; a 1.5 m reflector at C-band gives about 38 dBm²), so its phase centre is a point of known position and brightness in every SAR image. Arrays of surveyed reflectors — Geoscience Australia's **Surat Basin** array of about 40 reflectors in Queensland (installed 2014–2015), DLR's sites in Germany, and NASA/JPL's for NISAR — calibrate geolocation (to better than a metre for Sentinel-1 and TerraSAR-X after atmospheric and solid-Earth-tide corrections), radiometry, and, by their persistence, serve as reference points for InSAR time series ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)). **Transponders** (active radar calibrators) do the same with controllable delay.

**Radiometric sites.** **Railroad Valley** (Nevada), a flat, bright, homogeneous playa, is the primary CEOS radiometric calibration site for optical sensors in the Americas; Libya-4, Algeria-3, and other pseudo-invariant desert sites provide long-term stability monitoring. Radiometry affects DEMs indirectly — through image matching quality and through intensity-based classification ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)).

**Altimeter cal/val sites.** Satellite altimeters are calibrated against in-situ sea level at dedicated sites under their ground tracks: the **Harvest** oil platform off California (TOPEX/Poseidon through Sentinel-6 since 1992), **Corsica** (Senetosa and Ajaccio), **Bass Strait** (Tasmania; GNSS buoys and moorings), **Gavdos** (Crete), and for inland and ice applications **Lake Issykkul** (Kyrgyzstan) and the **Salar de Uyuni** (Bolivia), whose flat, stable salt surface was surveyed by kinematic GNSS to serve as a reference for ICESat and CryoSat-2. Each site yields the altimeter's absolute **range bias** (typically a few centimetres, mission-specific) and drift, and inter-mission biases that must be applied before combining records ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)).

## 25.5 Hydrographic calibration

A multibeam system is a lattice of sensors — transducer, motion sensor, GNSS, sound-speed probe at the transducer, sound-speed profiler, heading — whose relative positions, orientations, and timing must be known. The **vessel offset survey** (by total station or laser scanner in dry dock or at the pier, to centimetres) establishes the lever arms; the **patch test** establishes the angular misalignments and timing of the sonar relative to the motion sensor. The classic patch test (Godin 1998; Hughes Clarke 2003) uses four line pairs over chosen seabed: **latency** from two lines in the same direction at different speeds over a slope or feature (a time delay displaces the feature along track by speed × delay); **pitch** from reciprocal lines at the same speed over a slope or feature along track; **roll** from reciprocal lines over flat seabed (a roll error tilts the swath, so reciprocal swaths cross like scissors); and **yaw (heading)** from parallel lines offset by about half a swath width over a feature in the outer beams. The parameters are determined in that order because each contaminates the next; modern tools (CARIS, Qimera, Hypack; in MB-System the offsets are measured interactively in `mbeditviz` and applied with `mbprocess`) solve them by minimising the misfit between overlapping swaths, and a well-executed test yields roll to about 0.02°, pitch and yaw to 0.1–0.2°, and latency to a few milliseconds. The geometry matters: on flat, featureless seabed pitch, yaw, and latency are **unobservable** — the lines agree perfectly whatever the values — which is the Pitfalls' point about patch tests that "pass" on mud flats. A patch test is repeated after any change to the installation and checked at the start of each project by a **reference surface** comparison.

The **reference surface** (or calibration area) is a patch of stable, preferably flat and hard seabed, surveyed densely with the system at the highest achievable quality (multiple orthogonal lines, calm conditions, fresh sound-speed profile), gridded, and kept as a local truth. Thereafter, each day's data over the area is differenced against the surface: the mean difference tests the vertical reference chain (draft, tide/ellipsoid height, sound speed), the standard deviation tests the noise, and the across-track shape of the residual tests roll and refraction. NOAA's Field Procedures Manual and the US Multibeam Advisory Committee procedures prescribe these checks; the IHO S-44 orders set the tolerances. Two independent systems surveying the same reference surface should agree within their combined TVU; when they do not, the surface is the arbiter.

**Sound speed** is the sonar's metre. The probe at the transducer face (needed for beam steering on flat arrays) is checked against the top of the CTD cast, against a second probe, and — most tellingly — against the appearance of the outer beams over flat seabed, which curl up ("smile") or down ("frown") when the surface sound speed is wrong. Profilers are compared against a reference CTD annually and by inter-comparison casts within a survey; refraction residuals in overlapping swaths are the continuous check ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)).

Older and simpler checks remain valuable because they are independent. The **bar check** lowers a bar or plate to measured depths below a single-beam transducer and compares; it calibrates the transducer draft and the effective sound speed together. The **lead line** — a weighted, graduated line — is slow and crude (decimetres) but has no electronics to fail and is still used to verify a single-beam system and to sound alongside wharves. **Waterline, draft, and squat trials** determine the transducer's depth below the water surface at rest and its change with speed (squat and settlement of a few centimetres to decimetres on survey launches, more on large vessels), measured by observing a GNSS antenna's height change or by reciprocal runs over the reference surface at several speeds; an uncorrected 10 cm squat is a 10 cm bias in every depth. For fisheries and scientific echosounders, **calibration spheres** of tungsten carbide (38.1 mm is standard at 38 kHz) or copper, suspended beneath the transducer, give a known target strength from which the system gain is calibrated (Demer et al. 2015); the same spheres are useful geometric targets for multibeam water-column checks.

## 25.6 Sensor-level calibration

**GNSS antennas.** The electrical phase centre of a GNSS antenna is not a fixed point: it varies with frequency, elevation, and azimuth of the incoming signal by millimetres to centimetres. **Phase-centre offsets and variations (PCO/PCV)** are determined by robot calibration (Geo++ and others) or anechoic-chamber measurement and distributed in the IGS **ANTEX** format (`igs20.atx` for the current frame); using the wrong antenna model, or a type-mean model for an antenna with a radome the model does not cover, biases heights by up to several centimetres ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)). The antenna reference point to the mark (the "antenna height") is the single most common source of gross height error in GNSS surveying, and it is a measurement, not a calibration: measure it twice, in two units, and photograph the setup.

**IMUs.** An inertial unit's biases, scale factors, misalignments, and noise characteristics are determined by the manufacturer on rate tables and refined in the field by the navigation filter. **Allan variance** analysis of static data (IEEE Std 952) separates white noise (angle/velocity random walk), bias instability, and rate random walk, and is the standard way to characterise a unit and to detect degradation; the **six-position static test** (each axis up and down) determines accelerometer bias and scale factor; and the **lever arm** from the IMU to the GNSS antenna and to each sensor must be measured (total station or scanner) to the centimetre, since a 5 cm lever-arm error becomes a 5 cm position error that rotates with heading ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). The **boresight** — the small rotation between the IMU's axes and the sensor's — is not measurable by tape and is calibrated in flight.

**Lidar boresight and range.** Airborne lidar boresight (roll, pitch, heading misalignment between scanner and IMU) is calibrated by flying a site with planar surfaces at several orientations in overlapping strips in opposite and crossing directions and solving for the angles that minimise the strip-to-strip discrepancies on the planes; Skaloud & Lichti (2006) formalised this as a rigorous least-squares self-calibration with the plane parameters estimated jointly, and TerraMatch, RiPROCESS, and the manufacturers' tools implement variants. Results of 0.001–0.005° are achievable, and a residual 0.005° roll error at 1,500 m range is 13 cm at the swath edge. **Range** calibration — a constant offset and a scale factor — is checked against a surveyed hard flat surface and against targets at known distances; **intensity** calibration against reference reflectance panels (needed if intensity is to be used quantitatively; Kaasalainen et al. 2009). Terrestrial scanners are checked on ranges with spheres and planes per ISO 17123-9; their **targets** — spheres (whose centre is found by fitting regardless of viewing direction), planar checkerboards, and retroreflective discs — are the tie and control points for registering scans, and the sphere radius must be known and the fitting model stated.

**Cameras.** Section 22.1 of [Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md) covers the interior orientation. For calibration the choice is between a **test field** (checkerboard or coded targets, imaged from many angles with roll diversity; Zhang 2000 for the planar method, with OpenCV's `calibrateCamera` and Kalibr's camera–IMU calibration as the standard implementations) and **self-calibration** in the project block; the former gives stable, reproducible parameters that can be fixed or tightly weighted in the adjustment, the latter risks the doming of [Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md). Metric aerial cameras are certified on calibration ranges (Section 25.4) with reports stating the principal distance, principal point, and distortion with uncertainties.

**Sound-speed sensors, pressure sensors, and tide gauges** are calibrated against reference instruments at intervals set by drift: pressure sensors drift by millibars per year (centimetres of depth), and a tide gauge's datum is re-levelled to its benchmarks at least annually.

> **Worked example.** *Boresight observability and the cost of a small roll error.* A lidar at 1,200 m above ground with a 40° full scan angle has a half-swath of $1200 \tan 20° = 437$ m. A roll boresight error of $\delta\omega = 0.01°$ ($1.75 \times 10^{-4}$ rad) tilts the swath, producing a height error at the swath edge of $437 \times 1.75 \times 10^{-4} = 7.6$ cm, of opposite sign on the two sides, and zero at nadir. Flying reciprocal (opposite-direction) overlapping strips doubles the apparent discrepancy on the overlap (15 cm), which is easily measured on a flat surface — so roll is well observed from flat ground alone. Pitch, by contrast, displaces every point *along* track by $1200 \times 1.75 \times 10^{-4} = 21$ cm for the same 0.01°; on flat ground this changes no height and is invisible; on a 30° roof slope facing along track it produces a 12 cm height discrepancy between reciprocal strips, and is observable. Heading error displaces points along track proportionally to their across-track distance (7.6 cm at the swath edge) and is observable only on sloped or edged surfaces in the outer swath, from crossing strips. Hence the design of a boresight site: flat ground for roll, roofs with slopes in several directions for pitch and heading, and both reciprocal and crossing lines.

## 25.7 Global quasi-truth

Three sources of near-global reference heights have changed what "independent check" means for DEMs.

**ICESat-2** (launched September 2018) carries ATLAS, a photon-counting green laser altimeter with six beams in three pairs, 0.7 m along-track sampling, and about 11 m footprints. Its **ATL06** land-ice and **ATL08** land/vegetation products give along-track surface heights with an accuracy of a few centimetres over flat ice and roughly 0.5–1 m over vegetated or sloped land after terrain filtering (Neuenschwander & Pitts 2019; Brunt et al. 2019), referenced to the WGS 84 ellipsoid (ITRF2014) with precise geolocation (≈ 3–6 m horizontal). ATL08 separates terrain from canopy photons, which is what makes it useful under forest. **GEDI** (on the ISS, 2019–2023 and resumed 2024; 25 m footprints, 51.6° N–S coverage) gives ground elevation (RH0/lowest-mode) and canopy height, with geolocation of ≈ 10 m and ground-height accuracy of a few metres on slopes but decimetres in flat terrain (Dubayah et al. 2020). Neither is a DEM — they are sparse profiles — but across any area larger than a few tens of square kilometres they provide thousands of independent height samples that cost nothing, carry documented uncertainty, are tied directly to the ITRF, and were not used to make any national DEM. The SlideRule and icepyx services make extraction and on-the-fly filtering practical. Three caveats: the reference is ellipsoidal, so the comparison must handle $N$ ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)); the footprints average over slopes, so compare on gentle terrain or use the ATL08 segment slope; and ICESat-2 surface heights over vegetation and snow have their own biases, so stratify.

**Stable terrain co-registration** (Nuth & Kääb 2011) is the method of using the Earth itself as the reference: for two DEMs of the same area, residuals on terrain assumed not to have changed (bedrock, roads, unglaciated ground) are related to slope and aspect by $\Delta h / \tan\alpha = a\cos(b - \psi) + c$, where $\alpha$ is slope, $\psi$ aspect, and $(a, b, c)$ encode a horizontal shift and vertical offset. Solving for them, applying, and iterating removes the horizontal misregistration that otherwise produces slope-correlated apparent elevation change, and the residual on stable terrain after co-registration is an empirical estimate of the DEM-pair's noise — the basis of every glacier mass-balance and landslide-volume study and of [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md). It is a relative calibration (it ties one DEM to another, not to SI), and its assumption — that "stable" terrain is stable and that the two DEMs measure the same surface there — must be checked (penetration bias over firn is not stable; [Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)).

**Geoid Slope Validation Surveys** (GSVS11 in Texas, GSVS14 in Iowa, GSVS17 in Colorado; Smith et al. 2013 for GSVS11) are NGS campaigns along 300 km lines with geodetic levelling, GNSS, absolute and relative gravity, and deflections of the vertical at hundreds of marks, designed to test whether airborne-gravity-based geoid models reach 1 cm differential accuracy. Their results underpin the uncertainty statements of the modernised US vertical datum; for the DEM user they are the rare case in which $h$, $H$, and $N$ were all measured independently along the same line, and they are the reference against which a geoid model's regional error can be judged ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

## 25.8 Redundancy as self-calibration

The cheapest calibration infrastructure is observing the same thing twice in a way that exposes the error. **Loop closures** in levelling and traversing — returning to the start and comparing — detect blunders and drift and give the misclosure that classical standards (FGCC 1984; FGDC-STD-007.2) convert into an order and class. **Crosslines** in hydrography — lines run perpendicular to the main scheme, approximately 4 % of the main-scheme line length where bathymetric coverage is 100 % or more and approximately 8 % where it is less, under NOAA's HSSD — sample every main-scheme swath at a different time, heading, and often tide state, and the crossline-minus-mainscheme statistics are the survey's primary internal accuracy evidence. **Strip overlap** in airborne lidar and photogrammetry (20–30 % sidelap, plus crossing strips at the ends of a block) gives strip-to-strip discrepancies that TerraMatch-style **strip adjustment** uses to estimate residual boresight, GNSS/IMU drift per strip, and even range scale; a project's relative accuracy is reported from these overlaps, and the ASPRS standards specify the "intra-swath" and "inter-swath" relative accuracy values for lidar ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)). **Tie points** in photogrammetry and **loop closure** in SLAM ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)) play the same role: the redundancy makes the systematic parameters observable, and the residual after adjustment is the measure of the noise.

The limit of self-calibration is that it can only find errors that *differ* between the redundant observations. Two strips flown with the same wrong antenna model agree perfectly; two crosslines reduced with the same wrong tide file agree perfectly; a block adjusted with a self-calibrated camera has consistent tie points on a domed surface. Redundancy certifies precision and exposes some systematics; external control and independent checkpoints are still needed for accuracy ([Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md)).

## 25.9 Designing and maintaining a calibration program

A calibration program is a document, a schedule, and a set of triggers. The **document** lists every sensor and sub-system, its calibration parameters, how each is determined (laboratory, range, in-flight/in-water procedure), the reference it is traceable to, the achieved uncertainty, the date and the data of the last determination, and where the raw data and reports are archived. The **schedule** sets intervals by drift history: GNSS antenna models when the ANTEX changes or the antenna is replaced; IMU Allan-variance characterisation annually and after any shock; lidar boresight at the start and end of each project and after any removal; multibeam patch test after any installation change and reference-surface comparison daily or weekly; camera calibration per project (self-calibration) or annually (test field) for metric cameras; sound-speed and pressure sensors annually against a reference, with field inter-comparisons every cast; tide gauges levelled to benchmarks annually. The **triggers** are events that invalidate a calibration regardless of schedule — sensor swap, mount disturbance, firmware or software version change, transport, hard landing or grounding, lightning strike, and any unexplained systematic residual in the redundancy checks of Section 25.8.

The program's output is published *with the data*: the calibration report, the parameters applied, and the residual statistics belong in the project metadata ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)), because a user evaluating the data later ([Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md)) cannot otherwise distinguish a 10 cm product from a 10 cm claim. The absence of a boresight report, a patch-test record, or a sound-speed log in a delivery is itself a finding.

> **Case file.** A 2009 airborne lidar project over a coastal county delivered a DEM that met its 15 cm RMSE$_z$ specification at all 40 checkpoints — every one of them located on paved roads surveyed by the same RTK crew, from the same base station, as the GCPs used to adjust the strips. A subsequent comparison with NGS first-order benchmarks found a uniform 11 cm bias: the base station's published height had been taken from a superseded datasheet entry. The checkpoints, sharing the base, could not see it. The remedy adopted in later contracts was to require at least some checkpoints surveyed from a different base or by a different method (static GNSS to CORS via OPUS, or levelling from a verified benchmark), and to require that the base station coordinates be reported with their source and date. (Composite of reported incidents; see [Chapter 56](ch56-case-files.md).)

## Then & now

- **1791–1853.** The Principal Triangulation of Great Britain and the US Coast Survey (1807) establish national control networks; marks are set in bedrock and published in descriptions.
- **1833 ⟨H⟩.** The "Buttermilk" mark, the oldest surviving US survey mark, is set.
- **1929.** The Sea Level Datum of 1929 (later NGVD 29) adjusts US and Canadian levelling to 26 tide gauges.
- **1930s.** Siemens-star resolution targets come into use for optical testing; aerial camera calibration ranges follow in the 1940s–50s.
- **1967 and 1983.** The second (caesium) and the metre (speed of light) acquire their current SI definitions; every ranging instrument becomes a traceable clock.
- **1984–1985.** FGCC publishes *Standards and Specifications for Geodetic Control Networks*; the IOC establishes GLOSS and publishes Manuals and Guides 14 Volume I.
- **1991–1994.** NAVD 88 adjustment published (1991); the IGS begins operations (1994); the first CORS stations are installed by NGS.
- **1998.** Godin's thesis formalises the multibeam patch test; FGDC-STD-007 geospatial positioning accuracy standards published.
- **2000–2004.** Zhang's planar camera calibration (2000) spreads through OpenCV; NGS OPUS goes online (2001); NTRIP standardised (2004).
- **2006–2008.** Skaloud & Lichti rigorous lidar boresight self-calibration (2006); the USGS/CEOS test-site catalog (2007 ⟨H⟩); OPUS-Share begins (2008).
- **2010–2015.** Google rooftop resolution target (2010 ⟨H⟩); GSVS11 (2011); Surat Basin corner-reflector array installed (2014–15); QR-code roof targets (2015 ⟨H⟩).
- **2018–2019.** ICESat-2 (2018) and GEDI (2019) launch, providing free global quasi-truth heights.
- **2022–2025.** NGS prepares the modernised NSRS (NATRF2022, NAPGD2022), replacing passive-mark-defined datums with CORS- and geoid-defined ones; ITRF2020 released (2022).

The direction is from passive monuments to active stations and from national to global references: a benchmark's height was once the definition of the datum and is now one more observation to be checked against it.

## Validation & uncertainty

Calibration infrastructure is itself a source of uncertainty, and the honest report traces each product uncertainty back to the infrastructure it rests on.

**The independence test for checkpoints.** Ask four questions of any accuracy report. (1) Were the checkpoints withheld from every adjustment and processing decision? (2) Were they surveyed from a different base station, or by a different method (static-to-CORS, levelling), than the control? (3) Do they sample the places where systematic error is expected to be largest — block edges, swath edges, steep slopes, under canopy, far from the base — rather than only where access is easy? (4) Is their own uncertainty stated and at least three times smaller than the accuracy being claimed? A "no" to (1) invalidates the report; a "no" to (2) means the report cannot detect datum and base-station errors; a "no" to (3) means it cannot detect deformation; a "no" to (4) means the reported RMSE is inflated by the checkpoints themselves and the product may be better than claimed — or the checkpoints may be worse than assumed.

**Propagating infrastructure uncertainty.** Each link in the chain contributes to the product. For an RTK-controlled lidar DEM reported in NAVD 88:

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical uncertainty contributions from calibration infrastructure for an airborne lidar DEM, flat open terrain (1σ, cm; representative values, to be replaced by project-specific ones).
>
> | Link | Typical magnitude | How it is bounded |
> |---|---|---|
> | CORS/base coordinate (frame realisation, epoch, velocity) | 1–2 | NGS/IGS published coordinate uncertainty; velocity applied |
> | Base-station antenna height blunder | 0 or gross | double measurement, photo, independent checkpoint from another base |
> | Antenna PCO/PCV model (base and rover/aircraft) | 0.5–2 | correct ANTEX entry incl. radome |
> | Kinematic GNSS of aircraft (PPK) | 3–5 | baseline length, PDOP, fixed-ambiguity ratio |
> | IMU–GNSS lever arm | 0.5–1 | surveyed to 1 cm |
> | Boresight residual (0.003° roll at 1,200 m, swath edge) | 2–3 | boresight site and strip overlap |
> | Range calibration | 1–2 | hard flat surface check |
> | Geoid model (N) regional | 2–5 | GEOID18 stated uncertainty; GSVS results |
> | Checkpoint survey (RTK, network) | 2–3 | repeat occupations, second base |
> | Resulting DEM RMSE_z (checkpoint-reported) | ≈ 5–8 | ASPRS NVA at 95 % ≈ 10–16 |

The columns show why a 5 cm product is at the limit of what the infrastructure supports and why a 2 cm claim for an airborne product should prompt a request for the budget.

**Tests and reporting.** For active infrastructure: report the stations used, their coordinates' frame and epoch, the velocity model applied, the antenna models, and the processing service and date. For passive marks: report each mark's PID or equivalent, the published height's datum and date, the stability code, the recovery date and condition, and the result of the between-mark check. For calibrations: report each parameter determined, the method and site, the date, the residuals, and the comparison with the previous determination (a boresight angle that changed by 0.02° between projects without a physical cause is a warning). For reference surfaces: report the surface's own uncertainty, the number of comparisons, and the time series of mean difference and standard deviation. For global quasi-truth: report the product and version (ATL06 v6, ATL08 v6, GEDI L2A v2), the filters applied, the geoid used to compare, and the statistics by slope and land-cover class. For redundancy: report crossline or strip-overlap statistics separately from checkpoint statistics, and never substitute one for the other.

> **Rule of thumb.** The uncertainty of a checkpoint should be no more than one third of the accuracy being tested; the number of checkpoints should be at least 20 (ASPRS: 20 per land-cover class, scaled by area); and at least a quarter of them should be surveyed from different infrastructure than the control. If any of the three fails, the reported accuracy is a lower bound on the true error, not an estimate of it. The rule is a minimum, not a design; [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) gives the statistics.

## Pitfalls

- **Calibrating once and never re-verifying after a sensor swap, remount, or firmware change.** The parameters belong to the installation, not the sensor model. Make physical and software changes triggers for recalibration; keep a log.
- **Patch tests on flat, featureless seabed.** Pitch, yaw, and latency are unobservable there and the solver returns noise or defaults. Choose a slope and a discrete feature; check observability before trusting the numbers.
- **Checkpoints surveyed from the same base, by the same crew, in the same session as the control.** They inherit the base-station and antenna errors and cannot detect them. Survey a subset from independent infrastructure.
- **Checkpoints on a different datum or epoch than the product.** A NAVD 88 checkpoint against an ellipsoidal product, or a 2010-epoch coordinate in a moving region against a 2024 survey, produces a spurious bias. Reduce both to one datum and epoch and document the transformation.
- **Targets whose own coordinates are undocumented or unverified.** A painted cross with a coordinate from a handheld GPS is a checkpoint with 3 m uncertainty. Record how every target was surveyed, from where, and with what uncertainty.
- **Benchmark heights from a datasheet taken as current.** Marks subside, heave, and are destroyed. Check recovery dates and stability codes, occupy two marks, and compare their height difference.
- **Antenna height blunders.** The most common gross height error in GNSS work. Measure twice in two units, photograph the setup, and compare at least one checkpoint from an independent base.
- **Wrong or missing antenna calibration model.** Type-mean versus individual, radome versus none, old versus current ANTEX: centimetres of height. Match the model to the exact antenna and radome.
- **Trusting self-calibration or redundancy statistics as accuracy.** Strip-to-strip agreement, crossline statistics, and reprojection residuals are precision; they cannot see shared systematic errors. Add external checkpoints.
- **Reference surfaces that are not stable.** Sand waves migrate, mud consolidates, seagrass grows. Choose hard, flat seabed or known structures; re-survey the surface periodically and track its own time series.
- **Boresight calibrated at one range or scan angle and used at another.** Residual angular errors scale with range; a calibration from a 600 m site does not prove a 2,000 m project. Fly the boresight site at project altitude or include a project-altitude check.
- **Comparing to ICESat-2 or GEDI without handling the geoid and the footprint.** Both are ellipsoidal; GEDI footprints are 25 m and slope-sensitive. Convert, filter by slope and quality flags, and stratify.
- **Delivering data without the calibration records.** The user cannot distinguish the calibrated product from the uncalibrated one. Include boresight, patch test, offsets, sound-speed logs, and antenna models in the metadata.

## Key takeaways

- Calibration determines corrections; verification tests against a specification; validation tests against the use. Report which you did.
- Traceability runs to the SI second and metre through clocks and the refractive index; sonar's chain runs through sound-speed sensors, which is why they need their own calibration.
- Active stations (CORS/IGS, GNSS at tide gauges, NTRIP) carry the frame, its epoch, and its velocities; passive marks are observations to be verified, not definitions.
- Calibration ranges and targets exist for every sensor class — Siemens stars and checkerboards, corner reflectors, calibration spheres, altimeter sites — and their coordinates and their own uncertainties must be documented.
- The patch test and the lidar boresight calibration are geometry problems: parameters are observable only on terrain that makes them visible, and a solver's answer on flat ground is not a calibration.
- Reference surfaces turn every day's data into a calibration check; maintain them and track their time series.
- ICESat-2, GEDI, and stable-terrain co-registration provide free, independent, globally distributed checks — after geoid conversion and slope filtering.
- Redundancy (crosslines, strip overlap, loop closures) is continuous self-calibration for precision, not a substitute for independent checkpoints for accuracy.
- Checkpoints must be independent of control in use, in selection, and in survey infrastructure; apply the four-question test to every accuracy report.
- Plan calibration observations into every survey, set event-based recalibration triggers, and publish the calibration records with the data.
