# Chapter 2 — The many uses of bathymetry and topobathymetry

> **Part I — Why elevation? Uses and users.** The water-side companion to Chapter 1: what people do with depth data from harbour to hadal, fresh and salt, and what "depth" has to mean for each of them.

**In this chapter.** Bathymetry is elevation data with the sign flipped and the physics changed: the measurement is made through a medium that bends and absorbs the signal, the reference surface moves twice a day, and the consequences of error range from a research paper's revision to a ship on the rocks. After this chapter you will be able to name the principal uses of bathymetric and **topobathymetric** data — safety of navigation, dredging and ports, offshore energy, coastal management, hydrodynamic and hazard modelling, inland waters, habitat, geoscience, deep-sea policy, the cryosphere, defence, search and salvage, and the global effort to map the unmapped majority — and, for each, state what it assumes about the surface (shoalest, mean, or seabed-under-sediment), the resolution (which is inherently depth-dependent), the vertical and horizontal uncertainty (**TVU/THU**), the datum (chart, tidal, orthometric, or ellipsoidal), and the currency. You will understand why a navigation product and a science product made from the same soundings are different surfaces by design, and why the land–water seam is where most datum errors live.

## 2.1 Safety of navigation

The oldest reason to measure depth is to avoid hitting the bottom, and it remains the use that shapes most of hydrography's standards and vocabulary. A **nautical chart** — paper, raster, or an **Electronic Navigational Chart (ENC)** encoded in IHO S-57 and, from the mid-2020s, S-101 — presents selected **soundings**, depth contours, and depth areas, together with dangers, aids to navigation, and the shoreline. The ENC's depth information is deliberately sparse and deliberately *conservative*: the cartographer selects the shoalest soundings so that a mariner reading "no shoaler than 10 m" between the contours is right, and generalises contours *seaward* so that errors fall on the safe side. Where the source survey is uncertain, the ENC carries a **CATZOC** (category of zone of confidence) attribute that tells the navigator how much to trust it — and large parts of the world's ENCs are CATZOC C, D, or U, meaning the soundings may be from lead-line surveys of the nineteenth century ([Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md), [Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)).

The new generation of products changes what the mariner receives but not the philosophy. **S-102 Bathymetric Surface** delivers a gridded depth surface with an accompanying uncertainty grid, derived from the hydrographer's **Bathymetric Attributed Grid (BAG)**, so that a ship's system can compute **under-keel clearance (UKC)** against the actual surface, the predicted or observed tide, and the ship's squat. **Dynamic UKC** systems in ports such as Rotterdam, Port Hedland, and the Columbia River use high-resolution surveys and real-time water levels to let deep-draught ships load more or sail in narrower windows; a 0.1 m improvement in depth confidence can be worth thousands of tonnes of cargo per transit. Route planning, anchorage design, and grounding-risk assessment use the same surfaces.

What safety of navigation assumes:

- **Surface.** The **shoalest-depth surface** — the least depth that an object or the seabed presents within the cell or feature — not the mean. A gridded product made by averaging soundings in a cell *buries shoals*; navigation grids are built with a shoal-biased gridding rule or by preserving designated soundings ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)). In S-102, the surface is explicitly intended to be safe for navigation; the uncertainty grid is used to add a margin, not to replace the shoal bias.
- **Feature detection.** Beyond the depth of the surface, the survey must have *detected* objects of a stated size. IHO S-44 Edition 6 Special Order requires detection of cubic features of 1 m; Order 1a, 2 m up to 40 m depth and 10 % of depth beyond; Exclusive Order, 0.5 m; Order 1b and Order 2 do not require full feature detection (IHO 2020, 2022). This is a completeness requirement, not an accuracy requirement, and it drives sonar beam footprint, overlap, and line spacing ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md)).
- **Uncertainty.** S-44 states maximum allowable **total vertical uncertainty (TVU)** at 95 % as $\sqrt{a^2 + (b\,d)^2}$ with depth $d$ (Special Order $a$ = 0.25 m, $b$ = 0.0075; Order 1a/1b $a$ = 0.5 m, $b$ = 0.013; Order 2 $a$ = 1.0 m, $b$ = 0.023; Exclusive Order $a$ = 0.15 m, $b$ = 0.0075) and **total horizontal uncertainty (THU)** (Exclusive 1 m; Special 2 m; Order 1a/1b 5 m + 5 % of depth; Order 2 20 m + 10 % of depth). The product must carry per-node uncertainty so UKC can use it.
- **Datum.** **Chart datum (CD)** — a low-water tidal datum (LAT in most of the world, MLLW in the United States) below which the tide seldom falls, so that the charted depth is almost always available. CD is *local*: it varies by metres along a coast and is defined only where tides have been observed ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). Soundings reduced to CD using the wrong tide station or a model with 0.2 m error are 0.2 m wrong everywhere.
- **Currency.** Hard rock is forever; sand waves migrate metres per month; dredged channels silt within a season. Charts therefore carry the source diagram, and ports resurvey critical channels on a schedule.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Depth" on a chart is the vertical distance from chart datum *down* to the seabed, positive downward. In a DEM it is a negative elevation referenced to whatever vertical datum the DEM uses. In a BAG it may be stored as elevation (negative down) with CD, orthometric, or ellipsoidal reference. "Reduced sounding" means a measured depth corrected for draft, sound speed, heave, and tide to CD. A "controlling depth" is the least depth in a channel's navigable width, often quoted in feet in US Notices to Mariners. Merge any two of these without converting and you have introduced a step equal to the datum separation — typically 0.5–3 m at the coast.

## 2.2 Ports, dredging, and coastal engineering

A port is a place where the seabed is a managed, paid-for commodity. **Dredging** contracts are settled on the volume removed, computed by differencing a pre-dredge and a post-dredge survey against a design template (the channel's required depth plus allowable overdepth), and the money at stake makes dredge surveys among the most tightly specified in hydrography: USACE's EM 1110-2-1003 *Hydrographic Surveying* sets accuracy classes for "navigation and dredging support surveys" that correspond roughly to S-44 Special Order and demands repeatability between pre- and post-surveys because it is the *difference* that is billed. Channel-maintenance programmes track shoaling rates to schedule dredging; **scour** monitoring around bridge piers, quay walls, and offshore foundations looks for metre-scale holes that threaten structures; **breakwater** and **berth-pocket** surveys check as-built geometry against design.

The assumptions here differ from navigation's in one essential way: the engineer wants an *unbiased* surface whose *difference* between epochs is accurate, while the navigator wants a conservative surface. If a dredging contractor's payment survey is shoal-biased, the contractor is underpaid; if the port's acceptance survey is mean-gridded, a shoal in the channel is missed. Ports therefore maintain two products from the same soundings, or specify a gridding method per purpose. Volume accuracy is dominated by **bias** — in sound speed, draft, squat, tide, or datum — because a 0.05 m systematic error over a 1 km × 200 m channel reach is 10,000 m³ (see the worked example in §Validation). Resolution must resolve the dredge cut's side slopes and any ridges left between dredge passes (sub-metre horizontally); currency is days; the datum is CD for the channel's authorised depth but *consistency between the two surveys* matters more than its absolute value.

## 2.3 Offshore energy and infrastructure

Offshore wind farms, oil and gas fields, and the cables and pipelines connecting them generate most of the high-resolution commercial bathymetry in the world. The uses and their requirements stack by project phase:

- **Site screening and consenting** use existing bathymetry (national charts, GEBCO/EMODnet, regional surveys) at 25–100 m to exclude steep, mobile, or geohazard-prone areas.
- **Geophysical site surveys** acquire multibeam bathymetry and backscatter, side-scan sonar, sub-bottom profiler, and magnetometer data along the cable route and across the turbine field, typically to S-44 Special Order or Exclusive Order with full feature detection, because the deliverable includes **unexploded ordnance (UXO)** and boulder detection down to about 0.3–0.5 m objects.
- **Cable and pipeline route design** needs seabed slope and roughness, sediment type (from backscatter and grabs) for **burial depth** assessment, and the identification of **free spans** — lengths of pipeline unsupported by the seabed because of scour or rough topography — from pipeline-tracking surveys; free-span length tolerances are metres and depend on resolving the seabed to decimetres under the pipe.
- **Foundation design** requires the seabed surface at the metre scale plus sub-seabed geology; **scour** monitoring continues through operations.
- **Geohazards** — submarine landslides, fluid escape features (pockmarks), mobile bedforms, shallow gas — are mapped from the bathymetry's morphology at the 1–10 m scale.

Assumptions: an unbiased, high-resolution (0.25–1 m cell) seabed surface with full object detection; **ellipsoidal vertical referencing** (GNSS-based) increasingly used offshore because tide gauges are far away and tidal models uncertain, with conversion to CD or MSL via a separation model; horizontal accuracy at the sub-metre level because a cable must be found again for repair; currency tied to bedform mobility, which can be metres per year in tidal sand-wave fields.

## 2.4 Coastal management

The coastal manager measures change. **Shoreline change** rates are computed from historical maps, aerial photographs, and lidar shorelines; **beach nourishment** volumes are computed by differencing pre- and post-project topobathymetric surveys from the dune to the depth of closure (typically 6–12 m); **sediment budgets** sum those differences across littoral cells; **storm response** is quantified by differencing pre- and post-storm lidar; **inlets** and their ebb and flood deltas are surveyed to track channel migration; **marsh platforms** are mapped at the centimetre vertical scale because a few centimetres of elevation decides whether a marsh keeps up with sea-level rise ([Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

The defining requirement is **continuity across the waterline**. A nourishment volume computed from a lidar DTM that stops at the swash zone and a sonar survey that starts at 2 m depth has a gap in exactly the zone where most sand moves; topobathymetric lidar ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)) closes the gap in clear water, and in turbid water the gap is filled by wading or jet-ski single-beam surveys. The datum must be one surface for both sides — usually orthometric (NAVD 88 in the US) with bathymetry converted from CD via VDatum, or ellipsoidal throughout — and the uncertainty of that conversion (often 0.05–0.2 m, varying spatially) must be carried into the volume uncertainty. Marsh work adds a surface-definition problem: lidar last returns in dense *Spartina* are typically 0.1–0.3 m above the true marsh surface, a bias that is comparable to the entire signal of interest and must be corrected by vegetation class ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md), [Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md)).

## 2.5 Hydrodynamic and hazard modelling

Tide, current, storm-surge, wave, and tsunami models solve the shallow-water or Boussinesq equations on a domain whose geometry *is* the bathymetry and the coastal topography. **ADCIRC** (surge and tides), **SWAN** (spectral waves), **Delft3D** and **FVCOM** (coastal circulation), **MOST** and **GeoClaw** (tsunami) all require a seamless, topobathymetric surface on a single vertical datum — usually MSL or a local tidal datum for the ocean and the same datum carried onto land — and their outputs (water level, inundation extent, run-up) are compared with and overlaid on land DEMs ([Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). Tsunami inundation modelling uses nested grids from ocean-basin resolution (1–4 arc-minutes) down to 10–30 m at the shore (NOAA's tsunami DEMs, Eakins and Grothe 2014); surge models use unstructured meshes grading from kilometres offshore to tens of metres in estuaries.

The requirements are more about *structure* than about point accuracy. The model must see the continental shelf break, the channels that funnel surge, the barrier islands and dune crests that block it, the inlets, and the harbour breakwaters; a 5 m error on a dune crest is a different flood, and a channel that is one cell too narrow changes the tide in an estuary. The requirement for a **seamless topobathy** on a single datum with no artefacts at the seam is the single most common failure point: Eakins and Grothe (2014) and Danielson et al. (2016) describe the merging of dozens of sources of varying age, datum, and quality into coastal DEMs (NOAA's Coastal Relief Model and tsunami DEMs; USGS CoNED) and the artefacts — ridges and steps along source boundaries, datum offsets, and interpolation across data gaps — that must be found and suppressed. Models also need **roughness** (Manning's $n$) that depends on the land-cover and seabed classification tied to the same grid.

Assumptions: seamless topobathy DTM; resolution set by the narrowest feature that controls flow; MSL or tidal datum with the land DEM transformed onto it; currency relative to dredging, nourishment, and storms; and uncertainty expressed as ensembles of plausible bathymetries where it matters (shelf-break depth for tsunami travel time, inlet depth for surge).

## 2.6 Inland waters

Fresh-water bathymetry is less standardised and often more neglected than marine. **River bathymetry** for hydraulic models is the part of the floodplain DTM that airborne topographic lidar cannot see; it is supplied by boat-mounted single- or multibeam sonar, ADCP transects, wading surveys, bathymetric lidar in clear rivers, or — commonly — by synthetic channels estimated from width and discharge, which can dominate the model's uncertainty at low flows. **Reservoir capacity and sedimentation surveys** repeat a multibeam survey of the impounded area every few years to update the stage–storage curve and estimate sediment accumulation; USGS and the Bureau of Reclamation publish standard procedures, and the difference between the original topographic survey (often pre-dam, from contour maps on an old datum) and the modern sonar survey is where many "sedimentation" estimates acquire datum error. The **Great Lakes** use their own vertical datum, **IGLD 1985**, a dynamic-height datum that accounts for the lakes' size and glacial isostatic rebound, with a planned successor (IGLD 2020) tied to the new North American geopotential datum ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). **Inland navigation** on the Mississippi, Ohio, Rhine, and Danube is maintained by river commissions and USACE, whose **eHydro** programme publishes channel-condition surveys; the datum is often a *river stage* datum (low water reference plane) that slopes along the river. **Glacial lakes** in the Himalaya and Andes are surveyed to estimate outburst-flood volumes.

Assumptions: a true seabed/riverbed surface; resolution scaled to channel width (a 30 m river needs sub-5 m cells); vertical datum stated and *consistent with the floodplain DTM*, which is the inland version of the land–water seam problem; currency of the mobile bed; and for reservoirs, an explicit comparison of the datum and method of the baseline survey.

## 2.7 Habitat, fisheries, and marine spatial planning

Benthic ecology treats bathymetry and its derivatives — slope, rugosity, bathymetric position index, aspect — together with backscatter as the physical template on which habitats are classified. The US **Coastal and Marine Ecological Classification Standard (CMECS)** (FGDC 2012) has a Geoform component that is defined largely from morphology; coral-reef mapping, seagrass extent, and **essential fish habitat** designations under US fisheries law rely on habitat maps derived from multibeam, lidar, and satellite bathymetry; **marine protected area** boundaries and **aquaculture siting** use depth, slope, and exposure. Fisheries acoustics needs the bottom to separate fish echoes from the seabed, and demersal trawl surveys need it to avoid gear loss.

Habitat work values *texture* over depth: it needs the bathymetry at a resolution that resolves the habitat patch (metres on reefs, tens of metres on shelves), a backscatter mosaic co-registered to it, and an unbiased surface; absolute datum and currency matter little except where the habitat is itself mobile. The main risk is artefact: a sonar refraction "smile" or a motion artefact looks like rugosity to a classifier, and a seam between surveys of different resolution becomes a habitat boundary ([Chapter 42](ch42-object-detection-semantics.md)).

## 2.8 Geoscience

Marine geoscience was transformed by bathymetry twice. The first time was in the 1950s, when Bruce Heezen and Marie Tharp ⟨H⟩ turned echo-sounder profiles from Lamont's ships into physiographic diagrams of the ocean floor; Tharp's recognition of a continuous rift valley along the crest of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — initially dismissed as "girl talk" — became one of the founding observations of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics (Doel, Levin and Marker 2006). The second time was the arrival of multibeam and of satellite-altimetry-predicted bathymetry, which revealed the global pattern of ridges, fracture zones, and seamounts (Smith and Sandwell 1997 ⟨H⟩) and made morphology a primary dataset for tectonics, volcanology, and sedimentology.

Today's geoscience uses include **ridge and transform** studies at 1–50 m from ship and AUV multibeam; **seamount** census and the estimation of how many remain undiscovered (tens of thousands over 1 km high are predicted from altimetry but unmapped by ship); **submarine canyons** as sediment conduits; **submarine landslides** mapped from headwall scarps and debris lobes, important both as records of slope failure and as **tsunami sources** — the 1929 Grand Banks earthquake ⟨H⟩ triggered a submarine slide and turbidity current that broke twelve transatlantic cables in sequence, letting Heezen and Ewing (1952) compute its speed (up to about 60–100 km/h, approximate), and generated a tsunami that killed 28 people in Newfoundland; **gas hydrates** and fluid-escape features (pockmarks, mud volcanoes); **sediment transport** from bedform geometry; and **paleo-shorelines** and drowned landscapes on continental shelves that record past sea levels and human migration routes.

Science wants an **unbiased** surface at the finest resolution the depth allows, with the sonar's footprint — which grows linearly with depth, so that a 1° beam in 4,000 m of water is about 70 m across — governing what is resolvable ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). Absolute datum is usually irrelevant (MSL or ellipsoid, and a 1 m tidal error is invisible at 4,000 m), but **co-registration** between surveys matters for repeat studies of volcanoes and landslides, and **artefacts** matter enormously because refraction, motion, and gridding artefacts mimic geology. Deep-water geoscience also inherits the problem of navigation uncertainty for anything below the ship: an AUV's position is known to tens of metres unless acoustically aided ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)).

## 2.9 Deep-sea resources and policy

Two policy processes consume bathymetry at the scale of ocean basins. **Deep-seabed mineral exploration** — polymetallic nodules on the Clarion–Clipperton Zone abyssal plains, cobalt-rich crusts on seamounts, massive sulphides at hydrothermal vents — is licensed by the International Seabed Authority and requires baseline bathymetry and habitat mapping at scales from the exploration block (ship multibeam at 50–100 m) to the collector track (AUV at sub-metre). **UNCLOS Article 76** lets a coastal state claim continental shelf beyond 200 nmi where the natural prolongation of its land territory extends further, using formula lines defined by the **foot of the continental slope** (the point of maximum change in gradient at its base) plus 60 nmi, or by sediment thickness of at least 1 % of the distance to the foot of the slope, constrained by lines at 350 nmi from the baselines or 100 nmi beyond the **2,500 m isobath**. Bathymetry is therefore evidence before the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, and the position of the 2,500 m isobath and the foot of the slope must be documented with their uncertainty: a 1 % depth error at 2,500 m is 25 m vertically, which on a gentle rise can move the isobath by kilometres ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)).

Assumptions: unbiased, documented surfaces; morphology-preserving resolution at the scale of the slope (hundreds of metres); a *stated* vertical datum, because Article 76 practice uses MSL-referenced depths and sound-speed corrections must be documented; no particular currency; and provenance good enough for a legal submission ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)).

## 2.10 Cryosphere

Under ice shelves, in fjords, and beneath the ice sheets, bathymetry controls how the ocean melts the ice. **Sub-ice-shelf cavities** determine how warm circumpolar deep water reaches grounding lines; their shape is known from a handful of AUV and hot-water-drill transects, from gravity inversion, and increasingly from seismic and airborne radar; cavity-depth uncertainties of hundreds of metres remain in places and translate directly into uncertainty in projected ice loss. **Grounding lines** — where ice goes afloat — are located from InSAR tidal flexure and from the bed topography. **Fjord bathymetry** in Greenland sets which outlet glaciers warm Atlantic water can reach and has been mapped by NASA's Oceans Melting Greenland campaign. **Sub-ice bed topography** (BedMachine for Greenland and Antarctica; Bedmap) is a DEM of the rock beneath kilometres of ice, derived from radar sounding profiles interpolated with mass-conservation constraints ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md), [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

The assumptions are unusual: the surface may be the seabed under water under ice; the resolution is set by what the physics of the ocean model needs (sills, troughs) at hundreds of metres; the vertical reference must be ellipsoidal or geoidal with the reference stated, since tides and the geoid under ice shelves are poorly known; currency is irrelevant for rock and decadal for sediment; and **uncertainty maps are indispensable**, because much of the "bathymetry" is interpolation, and BedMachine-class products ship a per-cell error estimate for exactly that reason.

## 2.11 Defence and security

Navies were the largest funders of bathymetry through the twentieth century, and still are in many countries. **Submarine navigation** relies on charted bathymetry in a way no surface ship does, because a submerged submarine cannot see; on 8 January 2005 the USS *San Francisco* ⟨H⟩, running at speed and depth about 560 km southeast of Guam, struck an uncharted seamount whose summit rose to within about 30 m of the surface in an area the chart showed as thousands of metres deep; one sailor died and the bow was destroyed. The feature was visible as a discoloured patch on satellite imagery and implied by altimetry data that had not been incorporated into the chart in use ([Chapter 56](ch56-case-files.md)). **Anti-submarine warfare** depends on acoustic-propagation models whose bottom boundary — depth, slope, sediment type — governs ray paths, convergence zones, and reverberation; a propagation model is only as good as the bathymetry under it. **Mine countermeasures** need object detection at the 0.5 m scale and repeat surveys that can detect new objects by change detection against a baseline ("route survey"). Amphibious operations need the beach gradient from the surf zone to the dune.

Assumptions: conservative surfaces for navigation and unbiased surfaces for acoustics (the two coexist in different products); very high feature detection and change detection for mine warfare; datums that match the chart; currency against the known migration of objects; and classification regimes that keep the best data out of civilian products and keep the civilian products from being updated ([Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)).

> **Case file.** The post-grounding investigation of the USS *San Francisco* found that the chart in use was based on a 1989 edition derived from older surveys with soundings tens of kilometres apart, and that other charts and data (including satellite-derived indications of a shoal) had not been reconciled with it. The lesson is not that the chart was wrong — large areas of the open Pacific are charted from sparse lines — but that a product's *uncertainty* was not visible to the user in the moment of use. CATZOC and S-102's uncertainty grid exist to make it visible ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)).

## 2.12 Search, rescue, salvage, and archaeology

When something is lost at sea, bathymetry decides how it is searched for. The search for Malaysia Airlines flight MH370 (2014–2017) began with a bathymetric survey of about 279,000 km² of the southeast Indian Ocean because the existing depth data — mostly altimetry-predicted at ~10 km resolution — were inadequate to plan deep-tow sonar lines safely in terrain of 2,000–6,000 m with slopes exceeding 30°; the survey, released publicly by Geoscience Australia, mapped seamounts, ridges, and landslides unknown before (Picard et al. 2018). Wreck location for salvage, environmental risk (oil in sunken tankers), and underwater archaeology use multibeam and side-scan at the object scale, with the wreck's least depth entering charts as a navigation hazard. Search and rescue for people in the water uses bathymetry indirectly, through the current models of §2.5.

Assumptions: object-scale resolution (metres to decimetres) on top of reconnaissance-scale coverage; full feature detection and a clear statement of the smallest detectable object at each depth; unbiased depths plus a conservative least depth for charting; horizontal accuracy good enough to return to a target (tens of metres for a ship survey, metres with USBL on a vehicle); and archiving, because the MH370 example shows that search data become science data.

## 2.13 Exploration and the unmapped majority

Only a minority of the ocean floor has been measured by ship. The **General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans (GEBCO)** ⟨H⟩ began in 1903 under Prince Albert I of Monaco as a series of printed charts and became, through the IHO and IOC, the global compilation grid used by everyone from Google Maps to climate modellers (Weatherall et al. 2015). Its resolution is 15″ (about 450 m at the equator) but most of its cells are *interpolated* or *altimetry-predicted*, not measured; the GEBCO grid ships a Type Identifier (TID) grid that tells you which. **Seabed 2030**, launched by the Nippon Foundation and GEBCO in June 2017, aims to have the entire ocean floor mapped at a depth-dependent resolution — 100 m cells in 0–1,500 m, 200 m in 1,500–3,000 m, 400 m in 3,000–5,750 m, 800 m deeper — by 2030; the fraction of the ocean mapped to those standards rose from about 6 % in 2017 to about 25–27 % by 2024 (Mayer et al. 2018; Wölfl et al. 2019; GEBCO annual figures, approximate). The remainder is filled by **altimetry-predicted** bathymetry (Smith and Sandwell 1997; SRTM15+, Tozer et al. 2019), which resolves features only down to about 10–15 km wavelength and has depth uncertainties of hundreds of metres in places ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)). **Crowdsourced bathymetry** — depths logged by volunteer vessels' echo sounders and GNSS, under the IHO's B-12 guidance and the IHO Data Centre for Digital Bathymetry — fills coastal and shelf gaps at low cost but with uncertain draft, sound speed, and tide ([Chapter 28](ch28-reducing-cost.md)).

What exploration-scale products assume is that the user will *look at the TID*: that a cell is understood as measured, interpolated, or predicted, and that the resolution is read as the Seabed 2030 depth-dependent target, not as the 15″ post spacing. The vertical datum is nominally MSL, the currency is irrelevant, and the uncertainty is category-based rather than numerical.

| Exploration / GEBCO (§2.13) | compiled; TID-labelled | Seabed 2030: 100–800 m by depth | n/a | category-based | MSL (nominal) | n/a |

> **Rule of thumb.** The footprint of a sonar beam on a flat seabed is approximately $w \approx d \cdot \theta$ with $\theta$ the beam width in radians and $d$ the depth: a 1° (0.0175 rad) beam gives 0.9 m at 50 m, 17 m at 1,000 m, and 70 m at 4,000 m. No gridding at finer cells than the footprint adds information; it only interpolates. The rule fails for AUVs (depth below the vehicle, not the surface, is what counts), for lidar (footprint set by beam divergence and water-column scattering, typically 1–3 m), and for SDB (footprint set by the image pixel).

## Then & now

- **Lead lines (antiquity–1920s).** Depth was a weight on a marked line, one point per cast, with tallow in the lead's cup to bring up a sediment sample. HMS *Challenger* (1872–76) made a few hundred deep soundings across the world ocean in three and a half years, each taking hours; the deep ocean was known as a scatter of points joined by imagination.
- **Wire drag (1900s–1980s).** To *prove* an area clear to a depth, US Coast and Geodetic Survey launches towed a horizontal wire set at the clearance depth between them; anything the wire snagged was a hazard. It was slow, but it was the only method that guaranteed detection — the ancestor of today's feature-detection requirement.
- **Echo sounder (1913–).** ⟨H⟩ Reginald Fessenden's oscillator, patented in 1913 and tested against an iceberg in 1914, and the German *Meteor* expedition of 1925–27, which ran continuous echo-sounding profiles across the South Atlantic, turned depth into a line rather than a point and revealed the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as a continuous feature.
- **Multibeam (1962–).** ⟨H⟩ The multibeam concept (the US Navy's SASS, patented 1962) became the commercial SeaBeam in 1977 and the Atlas Hydrosweep in 1989; depth became a swath, and the seabed became something that could be *imaged*. Swath width of several times water depth and feature detection in a single pass made the modern S-44 orders possible.
- **Satellite-predicted bathymetry (1997–).** ⟨H⟩ Smith and Sandwell (1997) combined declassified Geosat and ERS-1 altimetry with ship soundings to predict depth from the marine gravity field's correlation with topography, giving the first uniform global picture at ~10–15 km resolution and filling the gaps that GEBCO's contours had always interpolated.
- **Topobathymetric lidar and SDB (1990s–).** Airborne lidar at 532 nm and multispectral satellite-derived bathymetry extended mapping across the land–water seam and into shallow, clear, or remote water where ships cannot work efficiently ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md), [Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)).
- **Seabed 2030 (2017–).** The goal shifted from "chart the routes" to "map the planet," with crowdsourced bathymetry, uncrewed surface vessels, and data rescue of archived surveys as the means (Mayer et al. 2018).

The arc mirrors the land arc of [Chapter 1](ch01-uses-on-land.md) — from a chart you read to a surface you compute with — but with one difference: the navigation chart never went away, and its conservative philosophy coexists with the unbiased science surface, so the hydrographer must produce both.

## Validation & uncertainty

Bathymetric error has more sources than topographic error because the measurement passes through water, the platform moves in six degrees of freedom, and the datum moves with the tide. The hydrographic community's response has been to make the uncertainty budget a formal, per-sounding computation — the **total propagated uncertainty (TPU)** model (Hare 1995; Hare, Eakins and Amante 2011) — and to require products to carry it.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Typical 1σ contributions to the depth of a single multibeam sounding at 30 m depth, outer beam at 60° from nadir, survey-grade system (approximate magnitudes; see [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md) for derivation):
>
> | Component | Typical 1σ | Scales with |
> |---|---|---|
> | Range measurement (bottom detection) | 0.02–0.05 m | pulse length, bottom type |
> | Sound-speed profile error (refraction) | 0.05–0.15 m at outer beams | beam angle, depth, SSP age |
> | Roll error (0.02°) | ≈ 0.02 m at 60° in 30 m | $d \tan\theta \,\delta\theta$ |
> | Heave / induced heave | 0.03–0.05 m | sea state, lever arms |
> | Vessel draft and settlement/squat | 0.02–0.10 m | speed, loading |
> | Tide or ERS (water level) reduction | 0.05–0.20 m | distance from gauge, model quality |
> | Datum separation (CD ↔ ellipsoid) | 0.05–0.15 m | separation-model quality |
> | **Combined (root sum of squares)** | **≈ 0.10–0.30 m** | — |
>
> Compare with S-44 Special Order TVU at 30 m: $\sqrt{0.25^2 + (0.0075 \times 30)^2}$ = 0.34 m at 95 %, i.e. about 0.17 m at 1σ. The budget passes only if sound speed, water level, and datum are well controlled — the three terms that survey design, not sonar hardware, governs.

**Which error matters for which use.** Navigation cares about the *largest plausible* error at the shoalest point, which is why TVU is stated at 95 % and gridding is shoal-biased. Dredging and coastal volumes care about *bias between surveys*: sound speed, draft, and tide errors are systematic within a survey and do not average out over a channel. Habitat and geoscience care about *artefacts* (refraction smiles, heave ripples, nadir ridges, seam steps) that masquerade as morphology. Hazard modelling cares about *structural* errors — the presence and depth of channels and crests — more than about any statistic. Everything that crosses the shoreline cares about the **datum transformation** between tidal and orthometric/ellipsoidal references, whose uncertainty in VDatum ranges from a few centimetres in well-gauged estuaries to 0.2 m or more on open coasts and in areas with few gauges ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)).

**How to test.**

1. *Crosslines.* Run survey lines perpendicular to the main lines and compare depths at the crossings; the distribution of differences (reported as a percentage of depth and tested against the order's TVU) is the hydrographer's primary internal check. It detects sound-speed, roll, and heave problems; it does *not* detect a common tide or datum error, which cancels at the crossing.
2. *Reference surfaces and patch tests.* Survey a known flat or well-characterised area (a dock face, a patch-test site) at the start of each project to calibrate roll, pitch, yaw, and latency offsets ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)); re-survey a reference surface between projects to detect drift.
3. *Independent checks of water level.* Compare the tide applied with an independent gauge or with GNSS-derived ellipsoid heights of the vessel; this is the only test that catches datum errors.
4. *Object-detection trials.* Deploy or use known targets (a 1 m cube, a known wreck) and confirm they appear in the data at the required coverage; completeness cannot be inferred from TVU.
5. *Seam inspection.* Difference overlapping surveys and overlapping land and water datasets; plot the difference along the seam; any mean offset is a datum or bias problem, and any trend across the swath is refraction or roll.
6. *Volume difference closure.* For dredging, compute the pre–post volume with and without a 0.05 m shift in one survey; if the contract's tolerance is smaller than the resulting change, the surveys are not fit for the payment purpose regardless of their individual TVU.

> **Worked example.** A channel reach 1,000 m long and 150 m wide is surveyed before and after dredging, and the computed removed volume is 48,000 m³. Each survey has an independent random depth uncertainty of σ = 0.10 m per 1 m cell (150,000 cells), and an unknown systematic bias between surveys of up to $b$ = 0.05 m (tide reduction from a gauge 12 km away).
> *Random contribution to volume:* $\sigma_{V,rand} = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma\,\sqrt{n}\,A_{cell} = 1.414 \times 0.10 \times \sqrt{150{,}000} \times 1\ \text{m}^2 \approx 55\ \text{m}^3$ — negligible.
> *Bias contribution:* $\sigma_{V,bias} = b \times A = 0.05 \times 150{,}000 = 7{,}500\ \text{m}^3$ — about 16 % of the billed volume.
> The bias term dominates by two orders of magnitude; the path to a better volume is a nearer tide gauge or ellipsoidally referenced surveys, not a better sonar.

**What to report.** The S-44 order claimed and the evidence (crossline statistics, object-detection trials); the TPU model and per-node uncertainty grid; the sound-speed sampling plan; the water-level method and its uncertainty; the vertical datum and separation model with version; the gridding method and whether it is shoal-biased; the survey dates; and, for compiled products, a source or TID layer. [Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md) and [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) give templates.

> **Try it.** Inspect what GEBCO actually *measured* in your area of interest using the Type Identifier grid. With GMT 6:
>
> ```bash
> # Download the GEBCO_2024 grid and TID grid (netCDF) from gebco.net first.
> # Fraction of cells in a region that are direct measurements (TID 10-17)
> gmt grdcut GEBCO_2024_TID.nc -R-30/-20/40/50 -Gtid_sub.nc
> gmt grdmath tid_sub.nc 10 GE tid_sub.nc 17 LE MUL = measured_mask.nc
> gmt grdinfo -L2 measured_mask.nc | grep mean
> # Plot the mask over a hillshade of the bathymetry
> gmt grdcut GEBCO_2024.nc -R-30/-20/40/50 -Gbathy_sub.nc
> gmt grdimage bathy_sub.nc -I+d -JM15c -Baf -png gebco_region
> gmt grdimage measured_mask.nc -Ctid.cpt -JM15c -Q -png gebco_tid
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: in most open-ocean regions the mean of the mask (the measured fraction) is well below 0.5; the hillshade shows ship tracks as ribbons of texture crossing smooth altimetry-predicted terrain. This is the picture to keep in mind whenever a "global bathymetry" is used at face value.

## Pitfalls

- **Averaging soundings for a navigation product.** Mean gridding buries shoals by a fraction of the local relief within the cell. Detect by comparing the grid's minimum to the designated soundings; avoid by shoal-biased gridding or CUBE with shoal override for navigation surfaces.
- **Mixing chart datum with orthometric land data at the coast.** The separation is 0.5–3 m and varies spatially; the merged surface has a step at the shoreline that models interpret as a cliff or a channel. Detect with cross-shore profiles; avoid with VDatum or a documented separation model and carry its uncertainty.
- **Assuming altimetry-predicted bathymetry resolves features under ~10–15 km.** It cannot, and seamounts shorter than about 1–1.5 km are largely missing. Detect by checking the TID; avoid by treating predicted cells as hypotheses.
- **Reading an old chart as current.** Soundings may be nineteenth-century lead line; CATZOC tells you. Detect by reading the source diagram or ZOC; avoid by never computing with charted soundings without their provenance.
- **Using a conservative navigation surface for volumes or science.** Shoal bias becomes a systematic error of decimetres. Detect by metadata; avoid by requesting the unbiased surface from the same soundings.
- **Trusting crossline agreement as proof of accuracy.** Crosslines cancel common tide and datum errors. Detect with an independent water-level check; avoid by designing at least one external check into every survey.
- **Ignoring refraction in outer beams.** Sound-speed error curls the swath edges by decimetres; the artefact looks like a sand ridge parallel to the track. Detect by comparing overlapping swaths; avoid by frequent SSP casts and surface sound-speed monitoring.
- **Gridding finer than the footprint.** A 1 m grid from a 4,000 m survey is an interpolation, and texture in it is noise. Detect by computing the footprint; avoid with depth-dependent cell size.
- **Confusing object detection with depth accuracy.** A survey can meet TVU and miss a 1 m rock. Detect with target trials and coverage analysis; avoid by specifying detection explicitly.
- **Forgetting that fresh water has its own datums.** IGLD, river stage, reservoir pool — none is NAVD 88 or MSL. Detect by asking; avoid by documenting the baseline survey's datum before computing sedimentation.
- **Under-estimating the vertical error of SDB and topobathy lidar in turbid or deep water.** Both degrade with depth and turbidity in ways that a single accuracy number hides. Detect with depth-stratified checks; avoid by reporting accuracy by depth band.
- **Treating the GEBCO or SRTM15+ post spacing as resolution.** Measured density varies by orders of magnitude between cells. Detect with the TID; avoid by stating the Seabed 2030 depth-dependent target instead.

## Key takeaways

- Bathymetric resolution is inherently depth-dependent: the footprint grows with range, so an appropriate cell size is a function of depth, not a single number.
- Navigation products are conservative by design and science products unbiased by design — two different surfaces from the same soundings; know which one you hold.
- Object detection is a separate requirement from depth accuracy; S-44 specifies both, and a survey can pass TVU while missing a hazard.
- The land–water seam is where most datum errors live: chart datum, orthometric, and ellipsoidal references differ by metres, vary spatially, and must be reconciled with a documented separation model whose uncertainty is carried forward.
- Volume and change uses are controlled by bias between surveys (sound speed, draft, tide, datum), which crosslines do not detect; design an independent water-level or ellipsoidal check into every survey.
- Most of the global seabed grid is predicted or interpolated, not measured; read the Type Identifier before computing anything.
- Uncertainty must travel with the product — per-node in BAG/S-102, CATZOC in ENCs, TID in GEBCO — because the user in the moment of use cannot otherwise know what they are trusting.
- Fresh water has its own datums (IGLD, stage, pool) and its own seam problem with floodplain DTMs.
