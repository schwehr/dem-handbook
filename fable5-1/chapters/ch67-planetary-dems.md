# Chapter 67 — Planetary DEMs: mapping without ground truth

> **Part XIV — Domain deep dives.** The last domain chapter and the book's stress test: every method of the preceding sixty-six chapters, applied to worlds with no GNSS, no benchmarks, no survey crews, and at most a handful of landers whose positions are themselves inferred from the maps.

**In this chapter.** Planetary topography is made from the same physics as terrestrial topography—laser altimetry, stereo photogrammetry, radar, shape-from-shading, gravity—but without the comforts of Earth: no independent check points, few repeat passes, illumination you cannot choose, and reference surfaces that must be defined before they can be used. This chapter explains how the planetary community defines body-fixed frames, reference radii, and the areoid or selenoid that stand in for sea level; how laser altimetry became the control network for everything else; how stereo DEMs from HRSC, CTX, HiRISE, and LROC are tied to that network by bundle adjustment and crossover analysis; and what "accuracy" can honestly mean when the only tests are self-consistency, overlap, and inter-method comparison. You will learn to read the resolution and uncertainty claims of the main products, to avoid the latitude and datum confusions peculiar to Mars, to run the open pipelines (ISIS, Ames Stereo Pipeline) with planetary CRS codes in PROJ/GDAL, and to carry the lessons—crossover adjustment, archival discipline, honest uncertainty—back to Earth.

## 67.1 Reference systems off Earth

### 67.1.1 Body-fixed frames and the IAU working group

Every planetary coordinate begins with a decision by the **IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements (WGCCRE)**, whose triennial reports (most recently Archinal et al. 2018, with corrections) define for each body the orientation of its spin axis, the rotation rate, the location of the prime meridian (fixed to a crater or a feature—Airy-0 on Mars, the mean Earth direction on the Moon), and the recommended reference surface: a sphere, a biaxial ellipsoid, or, for irregular bodies, a triaxial ellipsoid or a shape model. The **body-fixed frame** rotates with the body and is realized through the **SPICE** system (NAIF, JPL): planetary constants kernels (PCK) carry the WGCCRE values, and spacecraft ephemeris, attitude, and instrument kernels (SPK, CK, IK, FK) place each observation in that frame. There is no GNSS and no ITRF; the frame is realized by the ephemerides, by the orientation model, and—on bodies with altimetry—by the altimetric datasets themselves.

The reference surfaces are deliberately simple. Mars uses a biaxial ellipsoid with equatorial radius 3,396.19 km and polar radius 3,376.20 km (flattening ≈ 1/169.8), or a 3,396.19 km sphere for many map products; the Moon a sphere of radius 1,737.4 km; Mercury a 2,439.7 km sphere; Venus a 6,051.8 km sphere. The choice of sphere versus ellipsoid for a map projection is one of the oldest sources of interoperability trouble in planetary GIS: a product projected onto a Mars sphere and one projected onto the ellipsoid differ in latitude by up to about 0.34° (≈ 20 km) at mid-latitudes if the latitudes are interpreted inconsistently, and the IAU codes in PROJ now exist precisely to make the choice explicit ([Section 67.7](#677-formats-and-archives)).

### 67.1.2 Planetocentric versus planetographic latitude

The second classic confusion is latitude itself. **Planetocentric latitude** $\phi_c$ is the angle between the equatorial plane and the vector from the body's center to the point; **planetographic latitude** $\phi_g$ is the angle between the equatorial plane and the normal to the reference ellipsoid at the point (the direct analogue of geodetic latitude on Earth). On a sphere they coincide; on an ellipsoid they differ by $\tan\phi_c = (b^2/a^2)\tan\phi_g$ ([Mathematics](#mathematics)), with a maximum difference of about 0.34° on Mars (and about 0.19° on Earth, which is why no terrestrial practitioner confuses them: Earth uses geodetic latitude universally). Planetary practice was historically split: Viking-era Mars maps used planetographic latitude and *west*-positive longitude; the MOLA team and the IAU (since 2000) recommend planetocentric latitude and *east*-positive longitude for Mars, and essentially all products since MGS follow that; but legacy products, some mission teams, and some rover operations still use the planetographic/west convention, and a dataset whose metadata says only "latitude" cannot be placed to better than 20 km near 45° without finding out which. Planetographic longitude is also, by IAU convention, measured positive in the direction opposite to rotation—west on Mars—so the two systems differ in longitude sign as well.

### 67.1.3 Sea-level surrogates: areoid and selenoid

Heights need a zero. On Earth it is the geoid, realized through mean sea level and a gravity model ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)). Mars has no sea, but it has a gravity field determined from the Doppler tracking of orbiters (radio science), and the **areoid** is defined as the equipotential surface of that field (gravitational plus rotational) whose mean radius at the equator equals the adopted mean equatorial radius of 3,396,000 m (Smith et al. 2001; the areoid in the MOLA products is computed from a Goddard Mars Model gravity field truncated to degree and order 50). MOLA elevations are **planetary radius minus areoid radius**: the analogue of orthometric height, and the datum of every Mars product since. The alternative—radius minus ellipsoid radius, the analogue of ellipsoidal height—differs from areoid height by the Martian "geoid undulation," which reaches roughly ±1 km over Tharsis and the Hellas basin (approximately; it is far larger relative to the planet than Earth's ±100 m). Mixing the two is the planetary version of mixing h and H, and because the areoid heights are what MOLA distributes while raw radii are what stereo pipelines compute, the mix-up is easy; a stereo DEM reported "relative to the MOLA areoid" must have had the areoid actually subtracted, and a DEM reported "relative to the ellipsoid" must name which ellipsoid.

The Moon's equivalent, the **selenoid**, is derived from the GRAIL gravity field; but the lunar convention for topographic products (LOLA, SLDEM, LROC) is simpler: elevation is radius minus the 1,737.4 km reference sphere, with no gravity-defined zero, so lunar heights are "spherical" heights and the selenoid matters only when a level surface is needed. Mercury's MESSENGER DEMs use a 2,439.4 km sphere, Venus (Magellan) a 6,051.0 km sphere for its radius products, and small bodies use the center of mass and the shape model itself, with "height" defined as radial distance, as distance from a best-fit ellipsoid, or as **geopotential height** from a constant-density gravity model of the shape (the asteroid equivalent of the geoid, needed to know which way regolith would slide).

> **Definitions that bite.** *"Elevation" on Mars.* The MOLA MEGDR gives elevation above the areoid; a HiRISE DTM from the USGS gives elevation above the areoid (after a correction step); a CTX DEM from an Ames Stereo Pipeline run gives, unless the user requests otherwise, *radius minus a datum the user specified*, which by default may be the ellipsoid or a sphere; a rover's "elevation" in its site frame is relative to a landing-site origin. A floor elevation of about −2,600 m at Jezero crater (areoid-referenced) and a value hundreds of metres different for the same point relative to the ellipsoid or a 3,396.19 km sphere can both be correct in their own frames. Any Mars height without a stated datum (areoid model and radius, or ellipsoid/sphere radius) and a stated latitude convention is a number without a meaning.

## 67.2 Sensors

### 67.2.1 Laser altimetry

Planetary laser altimeters are the backbone of planetary geodesy because they give an absolute range from a spacecraft whose orbit is known from radio tracking, independent of illumination or texture ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md); [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)). **MOLA** (Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter, on Mars Global Surveyor, 1997–2001) fired a 1,064 nm laser at 10 Hz from a ~400 km orbit, with a footprint of roughly 170 m and ~300 m along-track spacing, returning about 640 million valid ranges (Smith et al. 2001) with ~0.4 m range precision and, after orbit and crossover adjustment, absolute vertical accuracy of about 1 m and horizontal accuracy of about 100 m. **LOLA** (Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, 2009–) uses five beams at 28 Hz, giving five ~5 m spots per shot with ~10 cm range precision and billions of returns; with LRO's precise orbits (GRAIL gravity and laser ranging) it yields a lunar geodetic framework at the metre level. **MLA** on MESSENGER mapped Mercury's northern hemisphere (the eccentric orbit left the south to stereo). Earlier systems—Apollo 15–17's altimeter (1971–1972), Clementine's lidar (1994), NEAR's NLR at Eros—established the method; Chandrayaan-1's LLRI and Kaguya's LALT added lunar coverage; OSIRIS-REx's **OLA** scanned Bennu at centimetre scale. Altimetry is *sparse in plan* (tracks hundreds of metres to kilometres apart, converging at the poles) and *precise in height*; its role is control, not imaging.

### 67.2.2 Stereo photogrammetry

Everything finer than the altimetry comes from stereo ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)). **HRSC** (High Resolution Stereo Camera, Mars Express, 2004–) is a nine-line pushbroom camera acquiring nadir, forward, and backward stereo plus color in one pass at 12.5–25 m/pixel; its DTMs are produced at 50–100 m posting (Gwinner et al. 2016), cover most of Mars, and are adjusted to MOLA. **CTX** (Context Camera, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter) at ~6 m/pixel yields stereo DEMs at ~18–24 m posting from pairs acquired on different orbits. **HiRISE** (MRO) at 0.25–0.5 m/pixel yields DTMs at 1–2 m posting (Kirk et al. 2008), each from a targeted stereo pair, produced with BAE SOCET SET/GXP at USGS and the University of Arizona and increasingly with the Ames Stereo Pipeline; several thousand exist, covering a very small fraction of the planet. On the Moon, **LROC NAC** (0.5–2 m/pixel) stereo yields DTMs at 2–5 m posting (Henriksen et al. 2017), tied to LOLA; Kaguya's Terrain Camera (10 m/pixel) gave global 60 m stereo coverage merged with LOLA into **SLDEM2015** (Barker et al. 2016). Cassini's ISS and radar gave Titan and the icy satellites; Dawn's framing camera gave Vesta and Ceres; OSIRIS-REx and Hayabusa2 gave Bennu and Ryugu.

Planetary stereo differs from terrestrial in ways that matter for error. The two images of a pair are usually from *different orbits*, days to years apart, with different illumination; the pushbroom geometry carries spacecraft **jitter** (high-frequency attitude oscillation) into the line geometry; the lack of GCPs means the exterior orientation rests entirely on SPICE and on adjustment to altimetry; and the surfaces are often texture-poor (dust-mantled plains, smooth ice), producing matching failures that are filled by interpolation.

### 67.2.3 Radar, photoclinometry, gravity, and in situ sensors

**Magellan** (Venus, 1990–1994) combined SAR imaging at ~100–250 m resolution with a nadir radar altimeter whose footprint was 10–30 km, producing the global topography of Venus at 1–3 km effective resolution (Ford and Pettengill 1992) and stereo from overlapping SAR cycles at higher resolution locally; VERITAS and EnVision will improve it in the 2030s. **Cassini's radar** mapped ~half of Titan in SAR strips and produced "SARTopo" elevation from overlapping beams plus a sparse altimeter dataset; Titan's hydrocarbon lakes are the only extraterrestrial case of a "sea level" with liquid in it. **MARSIS** (1.3–5.5 MHz) and **SHARAD** (20 MHz, ~15 m vertical resolution in ice) are orbital ice- and ground-penetrating radars that map subsurface interfaces—the base of the polar layered deposits, buried glaciers—and so give a second "DEM" beneath the surface, with the same penetration-depth-to-dielectric ambiguities as terrestrial radio-echo sounding ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)). **Photoclinometry** and **shape-from-shading (SfS)** turn a single image's brightness into slopes and integrate them to heights; they achieve pixel-scale relief where stereo cannot, at the cost of ill-posedness ([Mathematics](#mathematics)) and sensitivity to albedo variation. **Radio science** gives the gravity field (GMM for Mars, GRAIL's GRGM for the Moon, MESSENGER's HgM for Mercury) and thereby the areoid/selenoid, the crustal thickness models, and a long-wavelength consistency check on topography. **Rovers and landers** carry stereo cameras (Mastcam-Z, Navcam) that produce local DEMs at centimetre scale within tens of metres, and the Mars helicopter Ingenuity carried a navigation camera and altimeter; their *positions* in the orbital map frame are among the few "ground-truth" ties available ([Section 67.3](#673-control)). **Sample return** adds a special requirement: the sample's provenance must be located on the orbital map to metres, forever, which is what the Bennu, Ryugu, and Mars 2020 mapping programs were built to guarantee.

## 67.3 Control

### 67.3.1 Altimetry as the control network

On Earth, a DEM is controlled by GNSS-surveyed points and checked against independent ones ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md); [Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)). On Mars and the Moon, the control network *is* the altimetry. MOLA's absolute accuracy comes from the MGS orbit (~1 m radial after reconstruction), from laser pointing (which maps into ~100 m horizontal error), and from **crossover analysis**: at each of the millions of points where two ground tracks intersect, the two heights should agree; their differences are minimized by solving for small corrections to each orbit's radial position and timing, which reduced the RMS crossover residual from several metres to about 1 m (Neumann et al. 2001). The adjusted shots then serve as the "truth" to which everything else is tied: HRSC DTMs are adjusted to fit MOLA in a least-squares sense; HiRISE and CTX DEMs are produced from images whose exterior orientation has been adjusted to MOLA shots or to a MOLA-controlled HRSC DTM; LOLA plays the same role on the Moon, with LRO's superior orbits.

### 67.3.2 Bundle adjustment to altimetry

A stereo pair's **bundle adjustment** ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) solves for the camera positions and pointing (and, for pushbroom sensors, their time-varying corrections) that minimize image-space residuals at tie points. Without ground control the solution has a free **datum**: the model can translate, rotate, and (weakly) scale while fitting the tie points equally well. The planetary remedy is to add altimetric observations as constraints—either as pseudo-GCPs (altimeter footprints identified in the images, rare) or, more commonly, as a surface constraint: the adjusted stereo DEM must agree with the altimetry profiles crossing it. The Ames Stereo Pipeline's `pc_align` and `bundle_adjust` tools implement both the post-hoc alignment (ICP-style registration of a DEM to MOLA/LOLA shots) and the in-adjustment constraint; USGS ISIS's `jigsaw` performs the photogrammetric bundle adjustment with optional height constraints and is the engine behind the USGS control networks. The result is a DEM whose *absolute* position is as good as the altimetry (metres vertically, tens to hundreds of metres horizontally for MOLA; better for LOLA) and whose *relative* geometry is as good as the stereo (sub-pixel to pixel in height over short distances).

### 67.3.3 Control networks and the few hard ties

Before altimetry, planetary control was built like terrestrial triangulation: **control networks** of identified craters measured in many images and adjusted together—the RAND lunar and Martian networks of the Mariner and Viking eras, the USGS Mars Digital Image Model networks, and the **Unified Lunar Control Network 2005** (Archinal et al. 2006). Modern networks (Laura et al. 2017) use automated tie-point extraction and `jigsaw`, with LOLA/MOLA as the height constraint. The few **independent** ties are landers and rovers whose positions are known in an inertial frame from direct-to-Earth radio tracking (Viking, Pathfinder, Spirit, Opportunity, Phoenix, InSight, Curiosity, Perseverance; the Apollo retroreflectors, ranged from Earth to centimetres), together with their identification in orbital imagery. These give a handful of points per body at metres to tens of metres—an independent check that the altimetry-defined and image-defined frames agree at that level. Beyond them there are no checkpoints; what replaces them is **self-consistency**—crossovers, overlapping-strip comparisons, inter-sensor agreement, and topography–gravity agreement at long wavelengths.

> **Worked example.** *How well do we know a HiRISE DTM's absolute height?* A HiRISE DTM at 1 m posting over a candidate landing site is produced from a stereo pair with 0.3 m/pixel images and a convergence angle of 20°. Expected relative vertical precision from matching: $\sigma_z \approx \text{GSD} \cdot \sigma_{\text{match}} / \tan(\theta) \approx 0.3 \times 0.3 / 0.36 \approx 0.25$ m (with $\sigma_{\text{match}}$ ≈ 0.3 pixel). That is the number often quoted. The absolute height, however, is fixed by adjusting the DTM to MOLA shots within the ~5 × 20 km footprint: perhaps 40 shots, each with ~1 m vertical and ~100 m horizontal uncertainty, on terrain with local slopes of 2° (0.035). Each shot's effective vertical uncertainty is $\sqrt{1^2 + (100 \times 0.035)^2} \approx 3.6$ m; the mean of 40 shots has an uncertainty of about 0.6 m if the shot errors are independent, but MOLA orbit errors are correlated along track, so the effective number is closer to the number of distinct tracks (say 6): $\approx 3.6/\sqrt{6} \approx 1.5$ m. Add the uncertainty of the MOLA frame itself (~1 m) and the residual tilt the adjustment can leave over 20 km (a 0.01° tilt is 3.5 m end to end). The honest statement is: relative precision ~0.25 m over hundreds of metres, absolute vertical accuracy ~1–2 m, horizontal accuracy ~tens of metres (limited by the MOLA tie), and possible long-wavelength tilt of metres across the DTM—**not** "1 m accuracy." Kirk et al. (2008) report exactly this structure of precision versus accuracy for the Phoenix and MSL landing-site DTMs.

Two product families deserve a comment on method. **Small-body shape models** are built either by **stereophotogrammetry (SPG)**—classic multi-image bundle adjustment and dense matching, as practiced by DLR for Vesta, Ceres, and 67P—or by **stereophotoclinometry (SPC)**—Gaskell's method that fits, for each small surface patch ("maplet"), both its slope field (from the shading in many images under many illuminations) and its height (from the stereo parallax across images), and stitches maplets into a global model; SPC achieves finer relief than SPG where images are numerous and illumination diverse, at the cost of a more complex error structure. The Bennu program ran both and compared them against OLA lidar (Barnouin et al. 2020)—one of the few planetary cases where an independent sensor could test a photogrammetric product on the same body at the same time. **Gravity-derived "geoids"** (GMM for Mars, GRAIL models for the Moon) are not DEMs but are needed to interpret them: a river valley on Mars "flows" according to areoid slope, not radius slope, and the mismatch between topography and gravity at long wavelengths is a check on both.

## 67.5 Quality without ground truth

### 67.5.1 Precision is not accuracy

The planetary literature is careful, more often than the terrestrial, to separate **precision** (repeatability; the scatter of a surface about its own trend, estimated from matching statistics, overlapping strips, or flat-area roughness) from **accuracy** (agreement with the true surface, which cannot be measured directly). A HiRISE DTM's "expected vertical precision" of 0.2–0.3 m is a matching statistic; its accuracy is bounded only by the MOLA tie (metres) and the self-consistency tests. The honest planetary product report states both and names the tie. Users who need absolute heights (for a lander's altimeter, for a thermal model) must use the accuracy figure; users who need slopes or local relief (for hazard assessment) can use the precision figure, with a caveat about correlated error—the smooth, long-wavelength errors that pass through every stereo pipeline and are invisible in precision statistics.

### 67.5.2 Inter-method comparison

The substitute for check points is comparison between methods with different error physics. HiRISE DTMs are compared against CTX DEMs of the same area, both against HRSC, and all against MOLA profiles; LROC NAC DTMs against LOLA tracks and against SLDEM; SPC against SPG against lidar on Bennu. Agreement proves little about accuracy (shared control makes shared errors), but *disagreement* localizes problems: a HiRISE-versus-CTX difference map with a stripe pattern reveals jitter in one of them; a HiRISE-versus-MOLA difference that grows linearly across the DTM reveals a tilt; a residual bowl reveals a focal-length or distortion error, the planetary cousin of the SfM dome of [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md).

### 67.5.3 Artefacts to know

**Illumination and SfS.** Shape-from-shading hallucinates relief wherever albedo varies: a dark dust streak becomes a slope facing away from the Sun, a bright frost patch a slope facing toward it. Under oblique illumination the method is most sensitive and most fooled; a DTM "sharpened" with SfS should be presented with its stereo-only version and tested on areas of known albedo contrast (Kirk et al. 2003 discuss the method's limits). **Jitter** in pushbroom imagers (HiRISE and CTX notably) produces along-track ripples of metres amplitude at wavelengths of hundreds of metres in uncorrected DTMs; mitigation requires attitude reconstruction from the image itself or from multiple detectors, and residual ripple is a standard item in DTM quality reports. **CCD seams**: HiRISE's focal plane is an array of staggered CCDs, and mis-modelled offsets between them produce steps in the DTM at the seam positions. **Matching failures** on bland terrain are filled by interpolation and should be flagged (good products ship a "figure of merit" or confidence layer). **Radar penetration**: Magellan altimetry and SHARAD/MARSIS surfaces depend on the dielectric properties of regolith and ice; the "surface" is a volume-scattering centroid, and polar-cap radar surfaces differ from optical surfaces by metres to tens of metres in places.

### 67.5.4 Time variability

Other worlds are not static either ([Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md)). Mars has **dust devils** and regional dust storms that change albedo (and so SfS results) within days; migrating **dunes** and ripples (metres per year at some sites, measured from repeat HiRISE DTMs); seasonal **CO₂ frost** that raises polar surfaces by up to a metre or two each winter (MOLA measured the seasonal cycle directly); recurring slope lineae and gullies; and new impact craters appearing between images. Titan's **lakes** change level and extent; **Io** resurfaces by volcanism on timescales of years; comet 67P changed shape visibly through perihelion. A planetary DEM therefore has an epoch, and a stereo pair whose images are years apart is a DEM of a surface that may have moved between them—the same aliasing problem as mobile bedforms in [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md).

### 67.5.5 Landing-site certification

The highest-stakes use of a planetary DEM is a landing. For **Mars 2020**, the Lander Vision System (LVS) performed **terrain-relative navigation (TRN)** by matching descent images against an onboard orthomosaic and DEM derived from HiRISE and CTX, with the map-relative position requirement at the level of tens of metres so that the lander could divert from hazards identified in a pre-built **hazard map** (Johnson et al. 2022 report the flight performance, which met requirements with margin). The hazard map itself was built from HiRISE DTMs: slopes above threshold (e.g., > 25–30° at lander scale), rocks above a height threshold (from shadow measurement and rock-abundance models, since 0.5 m rocks are below DTM resolution), and scarps. This use made explicit what every DEM user needs: the **slope statistics for engineering**—slope at the lander footprint baseline (a few metres), its distribution over the ellipse, and its uncertainty—and the fact that slope error is amplified by DEM noise at short baselines ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)). A DTM with 0.25 m random error at 1 m posting produces slope noise of order $\arctan(0.25\sqrt{2}/1) \approx 20°$ at the pixel baseline, which is why hazard slopes are computed at multi-metre baselines and why correlated-versus-random error structure matters so much.

> **Case file.** *The Mars Polar Lander (1999) and the value of terrain knowledge.* MPL was lost on descent in December 1999; the most probable cause identified by the review board was a software fault that interpreted the leg-deployment transient as touchdown, not a terrain problem. But the mission predated MOLA's full mapping, its landing-site topography was known only at kilometre scales, and the subsequent decade—MOLA, HRSC, HiRISE, TRN—was shaped by the recognition that landing safely requires metre-scale terrain knowledge of the site. Phoenix (2008) and MSL (2012) were the first missions certified with HiRISE DTMs (Kirk et al. 2008), and Mars 2020 was the first to use them onboard. The lesson for Earth is unglamorous: certification is a *requirements* exercise—baseline, slope threshold, rock height, position accuracy—and the DEM is evaluated against those, not against a generic accuracy statement.

## 67.6 Lessons back to Earth

Planetary mapping is forced to practice what terrestrial mapping often only preaches. **Self-consistency metrics**—crossover residuals, overlap statistics between adjacent strips, inter-sensor difference maps—are first-class products published alongside the DEMs, because they are all there is; on Earth they are cheap to compute and routinely omitted in favor of a single RMSE against a few dozen check points. **Crossover adjustment** of the kind that made MOLA a geodetic framework applies directly to airborne lidar strip adjustment, ICESat-2, and multibeam surveys. **Honest uncertainty in the absence of truth** means stating precision and accuracy separately, naming the tie, and refusing to let a precision number masquerade as accuracy—the discipline [Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md) asks of anyone evaluating a DEM whose provenance they cannot fully check. **Archival discipline**: every planetary observation is archived in the PDS with raw data, calibration, geometry, and documentation in a validated, versioned format, and derived products are peer-reviewed before archive; terrestrial elevation data is archived, when it is, with a fraction of that rigor ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)). **Open pipelines**: ISIS and the Ames Stereo Pipeline are open source and used by the production centers themselves, so any user can reproduce a USGS or NASA DTM from archived images; the terrestrial equivalent is only now being built from PDAL, GDAL, MicMac, and the open photogrammetry stacks.

## 67.7 Formats and archives

### 67.7.1 PDS3, PDS4, and ISIS cubes

NASA's **Planetary Data System** archived data for three decades under **PDS3**—binary files with ODL (Object Description Language) labels describing dimensions, data types, and map projection—and since 2013 under **PDS4**, an XML-schema-based standard with a formal information model, required for new NASA missions and adopted by ESA's PSA and others. PDS4 labels describe array structure, cartographic parameters (via the Cartography discipline dictionary), geometry, and provenance; the data files are plain binary arrays, deliberately simple so they remain readable for decades. **ISIS cubes** (`.cub`) are the working format of the USGS Integrated Software for Imagers and Spectrometers: a PVL-labelled multiband image with camera-model and SPICE information attached, so every pixel can be mapped to the body surface. GDAL reads PDS3, PDS4, and ISIS3 natively and writes PDS4 and ISIS3 with some limitations; the planetary-specific metadata (areoid model, latitude type, longitude direction) often survives only as free text, which is the **interoperability gap**: a GeoTIFF exported from an ISIS cube may carry a spherical Mars datum, planetocentric latitude, and east longitude without saying so in any way a terrestrial GIS user would notice, and older PDS3 products carry projection keywords (`POSITIVE_LONGITUDE_DIRECTION`, `COORDINATE_SYSTEM_TYPE`) that no terrestrial tool reads.

### 67.7.2 Planetary CRS in PROJ and GDAL

Since PROJ 8.2 (2021), PROJ ships the **IAU_2015** authority with CRS codes for every body in the WGCCRE report, in a systematic scheme: the NAIF body number times 100 plus a variant digit—**IAU_2015:49900** is Mars (2015) sphere/planetocentric, **49901** Mars ellipsoid/planetographic, **49902** Mars ellipsoid/planetocentric, with projected variants in subsequent codes; **IAU_2015:30100** is the Moon (2015) sphere; 19900 Mercury; 29900 Venus. GDAL, QGIS, and pyproj accept them as `IAU_2015:49900`, and this is now the recommended way to make the sphere/ellipsoid and latitude-type choices explicit in a GeoTIFF. **SPICE kernels** remain the authority for geometry: a DEM's frame is only as good as the PCK it was made with, and WGCCRE values change slightly between reports (Mars's rotation model, for example), so a product should name its kernel set.

> **Try it.** Produce a Mars stereo DEM from two CTX images with ISIS and the Ames Stereo Pipeline, align it to MOLA, and export a GeoTIFF with an explicit planetary CRS. Requires ISIS (with the MRO/CTX kernels installed via `downloadIsisData`) and ASP ≥ 3.x; the two CTX image IDs are the stereo pair used in the ASP documentation (near 2°N, 4°E; check their availability in the PDS).
>
> ```bash

>     --bundle-adjust-prefix ba/run --stereo-algorithm asp_mgm --subpixel-mode 9 st/run

## Then & now

Planetary topography began with a shadow. In 1609–1610 Galileo measured the heights of lunar mountains from shadow lengths near the terminator, arriving at several kilometres—the first extraterrestrial elevation measurement, by exactly the photoclinometric reasoning SfS formalizes. Telescopic shadow heights and limb profiles were the only method for 350 years. The **Apollo 15, 16, and 17** service modules (1971–1972) carried a metric mapping camera, a panoramic camera, and a laser altimeter—the first planetary altimeter—and the resulting photogrammetry and control networks founded lunar cartography for three decades. **Mariner 9** (1971) and the **Viking** orbiters (1976–1980) gave Mars its first photogrammetric control and kilometre-scale topography, with absolute heights later found to be off by kilometres in places. **MOLA** (1997–2001) changed everything on Mars: a global, metre-accurate, self-consistent geodetic framework that revealed the hemispheric dichotomy, the northern lowland flatness, and the polar-cap volumes, and became the datum for all subsequent mapping. **HRSC** (2004–) brought systematic stereo; **HiRISE DTMs** (first produced in 2008 for Phoenix landing-site certification) brought metre-scale relief; **LOLA and LROC** (2009–) did for the Moon what MOLA and HiRISE did for Mars, with GRAIL (2012) adding the gravity field. **Rover TRN** (Mars 2020, February 2021) made the orbital DEM an onboard navigation chart, and the **sample-return** missions (Hayabusa2 at Ryugu, OSIRIS-REx at Bennu, Mars 2020 caching) made centimetre-scale shape models and metre-scale provenance maps mission requirements. The software history parallels it: USGS's lineage runs from the 1970s Flagstaff systems through PICS to **ISIS** (ISIS 2 in the 1990s, ISIS 3 from the mid-2000s, open source), and the **Ames Stereo Pipeline** ⟨H⟩—whose lineage runs back to the Mars Pathfinder stereo tools of the mid-1990s and which was released as open source in October 2009—became the community's reproducible DEM engine (Beyer, Alexandrov, and McMichael 2018).

## Mathematics

**Planetocentric ↔ planetographic latitude.** For a biaxial ellipsoid with equatorial radius $a$ and polar radius $b$,

$$\tan\phi_c = \frac{b^2}{a^2}\tan\phi_g = (1-e^2)\tan\phi_g, \qquad e^2 = 1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}.$$

The difference $\phi_g - \phi_c$ is maximal near 45° and equals approximately $\tfrac{1}{2}e^2\sin 2\phi$ in radians to first order: for Mars ($a$ = 3,396.19 km, $b$ = 3,376.20 km, $e^2 \approx 0.01174$) about 0.336° ≈ 0.00587 rad, i.e., roughly 20 km of ground distance; for Earth about 0.19°. Planetographic longitude increases westward on bodies with prograde rotation (Mars, Earth's convention excepted), planetocentric longitude eastward; the IAU recommends planetocentric/east for Mars.

**Areoid from a spherical-harmonic gravity field.** With the gravitational potential expanded as

$$U(r,\phi,\lambda) = \frac{GM}{r}\left[1 + \sum_{n=2}^{N}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^n \bar P_{nm}(\sin\phi)\left(\bar C_{nm}\cos m\lambda + \bar S_{nm}\sin m\lambda\right)\right] + \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 r^2\cos^2\phi ,$$

the areoid is the surface $r_a(\phi,\lambda)$ on which $U = U_0$, with $U_0$ chosen so that the mean of $r_a$ around the equator equals the adopted equatorial radius (3,396,000 m for MOLA). The areoid radius is found by Newton iteration on $r$ at each $(\phi,\lambda)$; topographic height is then $h = r_{\text{surface}} - r_a$. Truncation at degree $N$ = 50 (MOLA practice) leaves out areoid wavelengths shorter than ~400 km, which is appropriate because shorter-wavelength gravity is dominated by the topography itself.

**Photoclinometry.** For a Lambertian surface under a point source at incidence angle $i$, image brightness $I = A\cos i$, with $A$ the albedo; more realistic planetary photometric functions (Minnaert, lunar-Lambert, Hapke) add emission-angle and phase dependence. Along a profile in the direction of the Sun's azimuth, the slope $\partial z/\partial x$ is related to $\cos i$ through the surface normal, and heights follow by integration: $z(x) = z_0 + \int_0^x \tan\theta(x')\,dx'$. The problem is ill-posed in three ways: (1) albedo and slope are confounded—one equation, two unknowns per pixel; (2) only the slope component toward the Sun is constrained, so relief perpendicular to the illumination is unobserved and must be regularized; (3) integration accumulates error, so long-wavelength heights are poorly determined and must be constrained by stereo or altimetry. Modern SfS (e.g., ASP's `sfs`) solves a regularized least-squares problem for the DEM that reproduces several images under different illumination, initialized by and softly constrained to a stereo or altimetric DEM, with the albedo either assumed constant or co-estimated with strong smoothness priors.

**Crossover adjustment.** For two altimetric tracks $i$ and $j$ crossing at a point, with observed heights $h_i, h_j$ and unknown per-track corrections parameterized (e.g.) as a radial bias and a linear drift in time, $\delta h_i(t) = a_i + b_i (t - t_{0,i})$, the crossover residual is

$$r_{ij} = \left[h_i + \delta h_i(t_{ij})\right] - \left[h_j + \delta h_j(t_{ij})\right] ,$$

and the adjustment minimizes $\sum_{(i,j)} w_{ij} r_{ij}^2 + \lambda \sum_i \|\mathbf{p}_i\|^2$ over all crossovers, with a weak prior $\lambda$ to fix the global datum (which crossovers alone cannot: adding the same constant to every track leaves all residuals unchanged). With horizontal position errors, the crossover height difference also includes a slope-times-offset term, $\mathbf{s}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{x}$, so the adjustment can estimate along-track timing or cross-track offsets as well where terrain is sloped; on flat terrain they are unobservable. Neumann et al. (2001) apply this to MOLA with orbit-arc parameters; the same formulation serves airborne lidar strip adjustment and multibeam crossline analysis.

**Bundle adjustment constrained to altimetry.** The standard bundle adjustment minimizes $\sum \|\mathbf{x}_{\text{obs}} - \pi(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{X})\|^2$ over camera parameters $\mathbf{P}$ and ground points $\mathbf{X}$. Adding altimetry as a surface constraint appends terms $\sum_k w_k \left[ h_{\text{alt},k} - h_{\text{DEM}}(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{X}; \mathbf{x}_k) \right]^2$, where $h_{\text{DEM}}$ is the height of the adjusted stereo surface at the altimeter footprint; or, more simply, appends tie points whose ground coordinates are constrained to lie on the altimetry-derived surface with a vertical weight reflecting altimeter accuracy and a horizontal weight reflecting footprint uncertainty. ASP's `bundle_adjust --heights-from-dem` and `pc_align` implement the two common variants: the former in the adjustment, the latter as a rigid (or affine) post-alignment that minimizes point-to-point or point-to-surface distances between the DEM and the altimeter shots.

## Validation & uncertainty

The planetary validation toolkit is the terrestrial one with the check points removed. What remains, in order of strength:

**1. Independent-sensor comparison at the same site.** The gold standard, available only occasionally: OLA lidar versus SPC/SPG on Bennu; LOLA versus LROC NAC DTMs; MOLA versus HiRISE/CTX/HRSC. Report mean difference (bias), standard deviation, and the spatial pattern of the residuals (tilt, bowl, stripes). Remember that if the DEM was adjusted *to* the altimetry, the comparison is a fit statistic, not an independent test; hold out a subset of tracks.

**2. Overlap and crossover statistics.** Adjacent HRSC strips, overlapping CTX pairs, LROC NAC DTMs of the same site from different pairs: difference them, after co-registration on the common area, and report statistics. For altimetry, the crossover residual RMS *after* adjustment (≈ 1 m for MOLA) is the precision of the framework; before adjustment it measures orbit error.

**3. Internal precision.** Matching-based expected vertical precision; roughness over areas assumed flat (crater floors, lava plains); strip-to-strip consistency within a product. These bound random error only.

**4. Lander and rover ties.** Where a lander's inertial position is known from radio tracking and the lander is identified in orbital imagery, the difference is an absolute horizontal check at the metre-to-decametre level, and the lander's stereo DEM against the orbital DTM is a local relative check. Report these for every site that has one.

**5. Physical plausibility.** Topography–gravity admittance at long wavelengths; drainage consistency of valley networks on the areoid-referenced surface; agreement of crater depth–diameter statistics with established scaling; absence of relief correlated with albedo boundaries (the SfS test).

**6. Artefact screening.** Difference maps against a smoother reference (MOLA, HRSC) for jitter stripes and seams; hillshades under several azimuths for interpolation artefacts; slope histograms for the noise floor.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Indicative vertical error structure for a HiRISE DTM at 1 m posting over a flat-lying landing site (1σ; the numbers are illustrative of the structure reported by Kirk et al. 2008 and in USGS DTM metadata, not specifications).
>
> | Component | Magnitude | Wavelength | How assessed |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Matching noise | 0.2–0.3 m | 1–5 pixels | Expected precision; flat-area roughness |
> | Jitter residual | 0.2–1 m | 100–1,000 m along track | Difference vs CTX/MOLA; periodogram |
> | CCD seam steps | 0–0.5 m | At seams | Profiles across seams |
> | Interpolated voids (bland terrain) | undefined | Patch scale | Figure-of-merit layer |
> | Tilt / low-order warp after MOLA fit | 1–3 m across 20 km | > 5 km | Held-out MOLA tracks |
> | Absolute datum (MOLA frame) | ~1 m vertical; ~100 m horizontal | Global | MOLA crossover statistics; lander ties |
>
> The user computing 3 m-baseline slopes for hazard assessment is exposed to the first three rows; the user computing a crater's depth relative to the regional surface is exposed to the fourth and fifth; the user feeding a lander altimeter needs the sixth. "Accuracy 1 m" describes none of them.

Report, for any planetary DEM: body, frame and PCK version; reference surface (sphere/ellipsoid radii or areoid/selenoid model); latitude type and longitude direction; source images and their dates (the epoch); pipeline and version; control (what altimetry, how many shots, held-out residual statistics); expected precision; estimated absolute accuracy with its basis; known artefacts and a confidence layer. The USGS Astrogeology DTM metadata template does essentially this and is a good model for terrestrial products as well.

## Software

**Open source:** **USGS ISIS** (ingestion, calibration, camera models, SPICE attachment, `jigsaw` bundle adjustment, map projection for dozens of planetary instruments) — caveat: large kernel downloads and a learning curve; camera models are mission-specific. **NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP)** ⟨H⟩ (stereo correlation, `bundle_adjust`, `pc_align`, `point2dem`, `sfs`, `geodiff`; works with ISIS cubes and with terrestrial frame/linescan imagery including DigitalGlobe/Maxar) — caveat: defaults need tuning per sensor; read the per-instrument examples. **SPICE Toolkit / SpiceyPy** (geometry; kernels from NAIF). **GDAL/PROJ** (PDS3/PDS4/ISIS3 drivers; IAU_2015 CRS codes; `gdalwarp` between bodies' projections) — caveat: planetary metadata beyond the CRS is carried as free-form metadata. **QGIS** with planetary CRS support and plugins (e.g., for PDS imagery and MOLA/LOLA layers). **Cosmographia** (NAIF's mission-geometry visualizer; free). **Knoten / Ale / CSM** (USGS community sensor models for photogrammetry outside ISIS). **MarsSI**, **PDS Geosciences Node tools**, and **OpenPlanetary** community resources for discovery and processing.

**Free but closed:** **JMARS** (Arizona State University; free Java GIS for Mars, Moon, and other bodies with built-in access to MOLA, HRSC, CTX, HiRISE, THEMIS layers) — caveat: analysis export is limited; it is a viewer and planning tool. **NASA Solar System Treks** (web portals with DEM layers and simple measurement tools). **Quickmap** (LROC's web viewer).

**Commercial:** **BAE SOCET SET / SOCET GXP** (the production photogrammetric suite behind most USGS and University of Arizona HiRISE and LROC DTMs; rigorous sensor models and interactive editing) — caveat: expensive and closed, which is why ASP exists; results from the two are routinely compared. **Hexagon ERDAS IMAGINE** (planetary sensor models in some versions). **Agisoft Metashape** (used for rover and lander image sets and for some small-body work; no SPICE integration).

## Standards & guides

- **IAU WGCCRE reports** (Archinal et al. 2018, *Celestial Mechanics and Dynamical Astronomy* 130:22, with corrigendum; earlier reports 2009, 2011) — rotational elements, reference radii, prime meridians, and coordinate conventions for all bodies.
- **PDS4 Standards Reference, Information Model, and Data Provider's Handbook** (NASA PDS; current version, updated roughly twice yearly) — archive structure, labels, and the Cartography discipline dictionary.
- **PDS3 Standards Reference (v3.8, 2009)** — the legacy label format of most pre-2013 products.
- **USGS Astrogeology Science Center DTM production and metadata guidelines** (HiRISE and LROC DTM "README"/metadata templates; Kirk et al. 2008 as the methods reference) — content of a planetary DTM deliverable.
- **NASA PDS Cartography and Imaging Sciences Node (IMG) guidance** — map-projection and product conventions for imaging data.
- **IAU/USGS planetary CRS codes in PROJ (IAU_2015 authority)** — the explicit encoding of body, radii, latitude type, and longitude direction.
- **NAIF SPICE documentation (required reading: "Frames," "PCK," "Geometry Finder")** — authoritative geometry.
- **Mars 2020 / MSL landing-site engineering constraints documents** (slope, rock abundance, map accuracy requirements; published in the landing-site workshop literature) — an example of DEM requirements written for a safety-critical use.
- **MAPSIT (Mapping and Planetary Spatial Infrastructure Team) and Planetary Spatial Data Infrastructure roadmaps** (Laura et al. 2017) — community guidance on foundational data products and control.

## Pitfalls

- **Mixing areoid- and ellipsoid-referenced Martian heights** → MOLA products are areoid-referenced, raw stereo outputs are radius-based → state and verify the datum; difference a new DEM against MOLA MEGDR and look for a smooth kilometre-scale offset.
- **Treating HiRISE "1 m posts" as 1 m accuracy** → precision quoted as accuracy → separate matching precision (~0.25 m) from absolute accuracy (~1–2 m vertical, tens of metres horizontal) and from long-wavelength tilt; read the metadata.
- **Shape-from-shading hallucinating relief** → albedo variation under oblique light read as slope → compare SfS against stereo-only over albedo boundaries; distrust relief aligned with brightness patterns.
- **Planetographic/planetocentric confusion** → legacy or mission-specific conventions → check the latitude type and longitude direction in the label; a 20 km mismatch near 45° on Mars is the signature.
- **Sphere versus ellipsoid projection mismatch** → products on a 3,396.19 km sphere overlaid on ellipsoid-based products → use the IAU_2015 codes; never let a tool "assume" the datum.
- **PDS products without modern CRS metadata** → PDS3 labels with keywords no GIS reads → convert through GDAL/ISIS and assert the CRS explicitly; record the assertion.
- **Wrong or outdated SPICE kernels** → geometry off by image-scale amounts → `spiceinit` with the mission's reconstructed kernels, not predicts; record kernel versions.
- **Jitter stripes mistaken for landforms** → periodic along-track ripples in pushbroom DTMs → difference against a coarser DEM; periodogram the residual.
- **Stereo pairs years apart over mobile surfaces** → dunes, frost, or new craters moved between images → check image dates; expect matching failures and apparent relief over dunes.
- **Fitting the DEM to all altimetry and then "validating" against it** → circular statistics → hold out tracks; report held-out residuals.
- **Slopes at pixel baseline for engineering** → noise amplification turns 0.25 m noise into ~20° slope noise → compute slopes at the engineering baseline (lander footprint) and state it.
- **Assuming a rover's local frame is the orbital frame** → site frames are relative to landing or to a local origin → use the published localization (e.g., the Mars 2020 localization products) to transform.
- **Interpolated voids presented as data** → bland terrain fails to match → ship and read the figure-of-merit/confidence layer.

## Key takeaways

- Off Earth, the reference system is a decision (IAU frames, radii, latitude type, longitude direction, areoid or sphere); state every element of it, and use IAU_2015 CRS codes to make it machine-readable.
- Laser altimetry, adjusted by crossovers, is the control network; every finer product inherits its absolute accuracy (metres vertical, ~100 m horizontal for MOLA; better for LOLA) and adds its own relative precision.
- Precision (matching statistics) and accuracy (tie to altimetry, held-out residuals, lander ties) are different numbers; planetary reports separate them, and terrestrial reports should too.
- Without check points, validation is self-consistency: crossovers, overlaps, inter-sensor difference maps, physical plausibility, and artefact screening—make these first-class deliverables.
- Know the artefacts by their signatures: jitter stripes, CCD seams, SfS relief aligned with albedo, long-wavelength tilt and bowls, interpolated voids, radar penetration surfaces.
- Planetary surfaces have epochs too; a stereo pair spans time, and dunes, frost, and new craters move between images.
- Landing-site certification is the model of requirement-driven DEM evaluation: baseline, slope threshold, rock height, map-relative position—test against those.
- The open pipelines (ISIS, ASP) and the PDS archive are a reproducibility standard that terrestrial elevation data has yet to meet.
