# Chapter 73 — Open problems and the next decade

> **Part XVI — Standards, software, and history.** The last chapter looks forward: what is unsolved in elevation measurement and validation, what is arriving, and what a practitioner should do now so that the data made today remain usable, testable, and honest in 2035.

**In this chapter.** You will be able to separate the problems the next decade will probably solve from those that will merely change shape, and to plan accordingly. We take reference frames first — the NSRS modernization, fully kinematic global frames, and geoid-based heights at the centimetre — and the decade of mixed-datum data that will follow. We then survey the sensor pipeline (SWOT, NISAR, Biomass, ICESat-2/GEDI continuity, single-photon and Geiger-mode lidar, LEO-PNT, quantum sensors, autonomous surface and underwater fleets, smallsat stereo, consumer lidar), the product changes they enable (uncertainty rasters by default, per-pixel time stamps, multi-surface bundles, living compilations with supersession), and the methods questions that matter most for trust: foundation models, physics-informed and probabilistic terrain modelling, 4D change as the native product, and standardized intercomparison. Semantics, data infrastructure, policy, and education each get a section, followed by a grand-challenges list with measurable targets and a short list of things to do now. The book's validation discipline is the thread: the next decade's gains will come as much from metadata, uncertainty, and openness as from sensors.

## 73.1 Reference frames

The most consequential change of the decade for anyone working in North America is administrative rather than instrumental. The US National Geodetic Survey's **NSRS modernization** replaces NAD83 and NAVD88 with four plate-fixed terrestrial reference frames — **NATRF2022** (North American), PATRF2022 (Pacific), CATRF2022 (Caribbean), and MATRF2022 (Mariana) — and a single geopotential datum, **NAPGD2022**, in which orthometric heights are obtained from a gravimetric geoid model (GEOID2022) rather than from levelling (NGS Blueprints, NOAA Technical Reports NOS NGS 62, 64, 67). Coordinates will carry an epoch and an intra-frame velocity model; heights will be defined by $H = h - N$ with $N$ from a geoid whose accuracy, after the GRAV-D airborne gravity campaign, has an NGS goal of about 1 cm where airborne gravity coverage allows and about 2 cm elsewhere; the achieved figure will be known only from the final GEOID2022 assessment. The scheduled release has slipped repeatedly; when this was written NGS had announced a beta release of the modernized NSRS for 2026, with formal adoption still pending.

The physics is sound and the benefit is real — NAVD88 is tilted by about a metre across the continent and has subsided out from under its own bench marks in the Gulf Coast — but [Chapter 72](ch72-history.md) predicts the consequence: a decade of mixed-datum data. Expect lidar deliverables labelled NAVD88 that were actually processed with GEOID18 against NATRF2022 base stations; expect floodplain maps on NAVD88 compared against new surveys on NAPGD2022 with 0.3–1.0 m of apparent change; expect "NAD83" to persist in software defaults until 2035. The international picture is similar in kind if not in schedule: ITRF2020 is fully kinematic with post-seismic deformation models; ETRS89 realizations are being reconciled with the ITRF through ETRF2020; Australia's ATRF is a time-dependent frame alongside the static GDA2020; the IAG's International Height Reference System (IHRS, 2015 resolution) and its realization, the IHRF, define heights by geopotential number globally, with $W_0 = 62\,636\,853.4\ \mathrm{m^2 s^{-2}}$.

The open problems are not geodetic but operational. First, **GNSS-only heights at the centimetre** require geoid models good to 1 cm in the places people build — coastal plains, river deltas, mountain valleys — and those are exactly where airborne gravity, terrain corrections, and local density anomalies are hardest. Second, **time-dependent coordinates** require every file format, database, and CRS registry to carry epoch and a deformation model; EPSG and PROJ added point-motion operations and time-dependent transformations in the late 2010s, but most GIS software still treats a CRS as static, and almost no DEM format carries a per-pixel epoch ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md), [Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)). Third, there is no agreed mechanism to **re-reference an archive**: a 2015 lidar DTM on NAVD88 can be transformed to NAPGD2022 by a smooth model, but its control was NAVD88 bench marks of uncertain motion, so the transformed product has an additional, unquantified uncertainty that the model cannot supply.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Orthometric height" will mean two different things on either side of the NSRS transition: a Helmert orthometric height from levelling plus a hybrid geoid (NAVD88), and a height derived as $h - N$ from a gravimetric geoid in a geopotential datum (NAPGD2022). Both will be called "elevation above sea level" in metadata. Record the datum *and* the geoid model name; record the epoch; and record whether the height came from levelling, GNSS + geoid, or a transformation.

## 73.2 Sensors

The sensor pipeline for the next decade is unusually rich, and almost none of it is a direct replacement for airborne lidar over land or multibeam at sea; each new instrument fills a specific gap, and each brings a new error structure that will take several years of independent validation to characterize.

**Water surfaces.** The **Surface Water and Ocean Topography** mission (SWOT, launched December 2022) carries the Ka-band Radar Interferometer (KaRIn), which measures water-surface elevation across two 50 km swaths at roughly 10 cm height precision after averaging over 1 km² of water, with a 21-day repeat. Its targets are rivers wider than about 100 m and lakes larger than about 250 m × 250 m (Biancamaria et al. 2016; Fu et al. 2024). SWOT does not measure terrain, but it measures the water-surface slopes and stages that [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md) and [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md) currently infer from DEMs, which turns hydro-flattening from an assumption into a testable quantity. Its layover over steep banks and its dependence on a reference DEM for geolocation are the first open validation questions.

**Radar.** **NISAR**, the NASA–ISRO L- and S-band SAR launched on 30 July 2025, provides 12-day repeat interferometry over nearly all land, with L-band's canopy penetration improving coherence over vegetation relative to Sentinel-1's C-band; its primary products are deformation and biomass, but the global, consistent InSAR time series it will produce are the data from which vertical-land-motion models at the millimetre-per-year level — and hence the time-dependent corrections of §73.1 — will be built ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)). ESA's **Biomass** (launched April 2025) ⟨H⟩ is the first civilian **P-band** SAR; its 70 cm wavelength penetrates dense canopy, and its tomographic phase promises, as a by-product, a sub-canopy terrain estimate across the tropics where every optical and C/X-band DEM is biased upward by canopy (Quegan et al. 2019). Whether a P-band DTM can reach the 2–4 m accuracy needed to replace FABDEM-style corrections is an open question, and ionospheric phase distortion at P-band is the dominant error.

**Spaceborne lidar.** ICESat-2 (2018) and GEDI (2018–; paused on the ISS in 2023 and reinstalled in 2024) have become the de facto global reference for DEM validation ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)). Neither has a confirmed successor as this is written; both exceed their design lives. The validation community has built its practice on them — ATL08 terrain photons, GEDI L2A ground elevations — and continuity is an infrastructure problem the Decadal Survey (NASEM 2018) flagged under "surface topography and vegetation." The engineering frontier is **single-photon** and **Geiger-mode** lidar at scale: airborne systems (Leica SPL100, Harris/L3 IntelliEarth) collect at higher altitude and wider swath than linear-mode systems, trading per-return SNR for density, and their noise and vegetation-penetration behaviour differ enough that the USGS Lidar Base Specification treats them separately. A satellite topo-bathymetric lidar — a green-wavelength ICESat-2 successor optimized for the nearshore — has been studied but not funded; ICESat-2's incidental bathymetry (to about 40 m in clear water) has already changed expectations ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md), [Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)).

**Optical.** Commercial smallsat stereo constellations — Maxar's WorldView Legion (2024) ⟨H⟩, Airbus Pléiades Neo (2021) ⟨H⟩, Planet's Pelican — bring 30–50 cm imagery with daily-to-weekly revisit, which makes **on-demand DSMs** from satellite stereo a routine product at 1–2 m vertical accuracy where ground control or ICESat-2 tie points exist. Hyperspectral missions (EMIT 2022 ⟨H⟩, PACE 2024 ⟨H⟩, EnMAP, PRISMA) improve satellite-derived bathymetry by resolving bottom type from water column, the chief ambiguity in Lyzenga- and Stumpf-style inversions.

**Positioning and timing.** The positioning foundation is changing underneath the sensors. **Chip-scale atomic clocks** (commercial since about 2011) and holdover oscillators make GNSS-denied timing survivable; **LEO-PNT** constellations (Xona, ESA's LEO-PNT demonstrator, and opportunistic use of Starlink and OneWeb signals) promise stronger, faster-converging, harder-to-jam positioning; and GNSS jamming and spoofing — routine in the Baltic, Black Sea, and eastern Mediterranean since 2022 — have made resilience a survey-planning concern rather than a military one ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), [Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)). **Quantum sensors** — cold-atom gravimeters and gradiometers, quantum accelerometers — have been demonstrated on ships and in aircraft; a navigation-grade quantum IMU would end the drift that limits all GNSS-denied mapping, and a satellite quantum gradiometer would improve the geoid at the short wavelengths that limit §73.1, but neither is expected to be routine before the 2030s.

**Platforms.** Seabed 2030 cannot be met by crewed survey ships at current rates; the arithmetic in Mayer et al. (2018) implies on the order of hundreds of ship-years. **Uncrewed surface vessels** (Saildrone Surveyor, XOCEAN, Ocean Infinity's Armada) and AUV swarms lower the cost per square kilometre by removing crew, and the open problems are now positioning (USBL and INS drift for AUVs), sound-speed sampling by vehicles that cannot cast a CTD, and the quality-control workload of data arriving faster than hydrographers can review it ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md)). On land, **consumer lidar** — in phones since 2020, in cars at scale from the mid-2020s — is a potential crowdsourced source of street-level geometry whose calibration, consent, and provenance problems are unsolved ([Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)).

## 73.3 Products

What a DEM *is* as a product is changing faster than how it is measured. Five shifts are under way.

**Uncertainty rasters by default.** The hydrographic world made per-node uncertainty mandatory in BAG (2006) and S-102; the topographic world is following. Copernicus DEM ships height-error masks, TanDEM-X ships a height-error map, and the 2024 USGS Lidar Base Specification requires accuracy reporting by land-cover class, but no major national lidar programme yet delivers a per-pixel uncertainty raster with its DTM. The obstacle is not computation — propagating lidar TPU through classification and gridding is a solved problem in principle ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)) — but the lack of a standard encoding and the fear that a visible uncertainty layer invites liability. The open technical problem is harder than it looks: a per-pixel σ is only useful with a spatial correlation model, since volume and slope uncertainties depend on correlation length as much as on σ ([Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md)); no mainstream format carries one.

**Per-pixel time stamps.** A national DTM compiled from flights spanning 2010–2023 is a mosaic of epochs; users treat it as a single surface. STAC and the Lidar Base Specification carry acquisition dates per tile or swath, but a per-pixel date band — cheap to compute, since every lidar return has a GPS time — is rare. With vertical land motion of 5–10 mm/yr in deltas and cities, a 13-year spread is 6–13 cm of unmodelled height change, comparable to the product's accuracy.

**Multi-surface products.** The DSM/DTM dichotomy is dissolving into layered bundles: ground, canopy top, canopy height, building, water surface, bridge deck, each with its own inclusion rules and uncertainty ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md), [Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md)). Shipping them together, with a shared point-cloud provenance, resolves the "which surface is this" ambiguity of [Chapter 4](ch04-names-and-definitions.md), but requires a surface ontology (§73.5) that does not yet exist.

**Continuous update instead of decadal campaigns.** The Netherlands (AHN, now on a rolling cycle), Denmark, Switzerland, and some US states have moved from one-off national campaigns to continuous or short-cycle updates; satellite stereo and InSAR fill between. The resulting product is a time series with supersession rather than a version, and the archive, catalog, and format implications ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)) are mostly unmet.

**Living compilations with supersession.** NOAA's **National Bathymetric Source** (NBS) and its public **BlueTopo** product are the clearest model: a continuously maintained best-available bathymetric surface in which each cell carries its source survey, uncertainty, and a supersession rule by which newer or better data replace older ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)). Extending this to land — a national "best available DTM" with per-cell source and date — is technically straightforward and institutionally novel. ML-corrected global DTMs (FABDEM, DeltaDTM, GEDTM30) are the global analogue; their open problem is that their uncertainty is inherited from ML models trained where lidar exists and applied where it does not ([Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md)).

> **Case file.** FABDEM (Hawker et al. 2022) removed forest and building bias from Copernicus GLO-30 with a random-forest model trained on lidar in a small number of countries; against reference lidar its mean absolute error in forested test areas fell from about 5.2 m to about 2.9 m and in built-up areas from about 1.6 m to 1.1 m, and validation in those test regions was strongly positive. Independent tests in regions outside the training domain — tropical peatlands, dense informal settlements, high-relief mountains — have reported both larger errors and cases where the "correction" introduced spurious terrain. The lesson is not that FABDEM is poor but that a product's accuracy statement is valid only on the distribution it was tested on, and that global ML products must publish their training-domain mask alongside the heights.

## 73.4 Methods

**Foundation models for point clouds and terrain.** Large self-supervised models pretrained on imagery (Clay 1.0, 2024 ⟨H⟩; Prithvi; SatMAE) and, more recently, on point clouds promise a single backbone that classifies ground, extracts buildings, fills voids, and super-resolves DEMs after light fine-tuning. The promise is real where labelled data are scarce; the risks are specific. A model that has seen the world's lidar has seen it where lidar exists — temperate, wealthy, flat-to-rolling — and its priors about what terrain "looks like" are those places' priors. Void filling and super-resolution with such a model produce plausible terrain that is indistinguishable, cell by cell, from measured terrain ([Chapter 45](ch45-super-resolution.md)); the Tharp–Heezen problem of [Chapter 72](ch72-history.md) returns at global scale with a better pencil. Three requirements follow: a per-cell provenance flag distinguishing measured from inferred, uncertainty that is calibrated (a stated 95 % interval contains the truth 95 % of the time) rather than merely produced, and evaluation on held-out *regions*, not held-out *pixels*.

**Physics-informed and probabilistic modelling.** Two older ideas are returning with modern compute. Physics-informed ML constrains the learned surface with hydraulic or geomorphic laws — water flows downhill, channels are continuous, slopes have angle-of-repose limits — which regularizes exactly the failures (spurious pits, broken drainage) that hydrologists spend the most effort fixing ([Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). **Gaussian-process terrain models**, long confined to small areas by $O(n^3)$ cost, now scale through sparse, hierarchical, and GPU implementations to millions of points, and they deliver the one thing deterministic interpolators cannot: a posterior variance that reflects data density and local roughness, which is the uncertainty raster of §73.3 obtained honestly ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)).

**4D change as the native product.** With annual or better revisit from InSAR, satellite stereo, and rolling lidar, the natural product is not a DEM but an elevation time series per cell with a fitted trend, seasonal term, and step changes, plus their uncertainties — what the glaciology community already does with ASTER and ArcticDEM stacks (Hugonnet et al. 2021). The open problems are co-registration across sensors with different surface definitions (a lidar DTM and a stereo DSM do not measure the same thing in forest), the spatially correlated noise that makes naive per-pixel regression overconfident, and a format for the result ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)).

**Automatic QA/QC with human audit.** Classification, void detection, artefact detection, and checkpoint comparison can be automated; deciding what to do about a borderline result cannot, yet. The practical architecture emerging in national programmes is an automated screen producing a ranked list of anomalies, a human reviewing the top of the list, and the human's decisions becoming training data. The risk is automation bias — the reviewer accepting the screen's framing — and the mitigation is to seed the queue with known errors and measure the reviewer's detection rate.

**Standardized intercomparison.** The **DEMIX** initiative (Guth et al. 2021; Bielski et al. 2024) established a method — tiles, criteria, rankings, statistical tests — for comparing global DEMs on equal terms, and showed, among other things, that Copernicus DEM ranks first among open 1″ products across most criteria. Making this a community habit, as the atmospheric and climate communities did with model intercomparison projects, requires persistent benchmark datasets ([Appendix H](../appendices/appendix-h-datasets.md)), agreed metrics (NMAD and LE95 by land cover and slope, not a single RMSE), and the willingness of producers to be ranked.

**Formal verification of pipelines.** Elevation pipelines are long chains of floating-point transformations in which a half-cell shift, a unit mismatch, or a datum default can pass silently ([Chapter 29](ch29-processing-pipelines.md)). Property-based testing (round-trip transforms, invariants such as "slope never exceeds 90°" or "no cell below the local chart datum on land"), unit-carrying types, and reproducible builds are available now; formal verification of geodetic libraries remains research.

> **Rule of thumb.** Any method that produces elevation where none was measured — void fill, super-resolution, ML correction, kriging beyond the data — should ship three layers: the value, a calibrated uncertainty, and a flag. If it ships only the value, treat its uncertainty as unknown and its accuracy claims as applying to the training domain only.

## 73.5 Semantics

The deepest open problem in this book is not instrumental: it is that "ground," "building," and "water surface" have no agreed cross-domain definitions, so two correct DTMs of the same place differ by metres at bridges, overpasses, piers, dams, retaining walls, solar farms, and greenhouses ([Chapter 4](ch04-names-and-definitions.md), [Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md)). Hydrologists want bridges removed and culverts cut; aviation wants every obstacle kept; cadastral surveyors want the engineered surface; flood insurers want the surface water would see. Each is right, and a single "bare earth" cannot serve them all.

What is needed is a **surface ontology**: a machine-readable vocabulary of surface types (natural ground, engineered ground, structure deck, vegetation top, water surface at stated datum, ice surface, bed under water) and of **inclusion/exclusion rules** stated as predicates on object classes, so that a product can declare "DTM-H: bridges removed, culverts enforced, dams retained, buildings removed, vegetation removed" in a form that software can test. Pieces exist — ASPRS LAS classification codes, CityGML LoD concepts, INSPIRE Elevation's surface types, the Lidar Base Specification's hydro-flattening rules — but no common model. The hardest sub-problem is the **multi-valued surface**: a cell under a bridge has two legitimate heights, and under an overhanging cliff or a multi-level interchange it may have three; grids cannot hold them, meshes and voxels can, and no national product does ([Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md)). Integration of **indoor and underground** models (IndoorGML, BIM) with outdoor DEMs ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)) raises the same question in three dimensions and adds the problem of vertical datums for floors.

## 73.6 Data infrastructure

The direction of infrastructure is set: **cloud-native everything**. COG for grids, COPC and Entwine Point Tiles for point clouds, Zarr (with GeoZarr and Icechunk 2024 ⟨H⟩ for transactional, versioned arrays) for multidimensional stacks, GeoParquet for vector and STAC-GeoParquet (2024) ⟨H⟩ for catalogs, and STAC 1.1 (2024) ⟨H⟩ as the discovery layer ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md), [Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md)). The open problems are above the format layer.

**Quality-first catalogs.** No major catalog lets you search by *tested* accuracy — "DTMs in this basin with NVA ≤ 10 cm verified against ≥ 30 independent checkpoints since 2018." STAC has the extension mechanism and ISO 19157 has the vocabulary; what is missing is the habit of producers publishing checkpoints and the convention for encoding results. This is the single most practical improvement available, and it needs no new technology.

**Persistent provenance.** Content-addressed storage (hashes as identifiers, as in Icechunk and in the broader data-versioning tools) makes it possible to state exactly which bytes a product was derived from. Linking that to W3C PROV-style lineage so that a cell in a national compilation can be traced to a swath, a flight, a calibration, and a processing-software version is the goal; the obstacle is that lineage is generated by a dozen tools that do not share a model ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)).

**Raw archives as infrastructure.** [Chapter 72](ch72-history.md)'s pattern — the archive is always under-funded — continues. Full-waveform lidar, raw multibeam water-column data, and SAR single-look complex data are routinely discarded or kept on media that will not be readable in a decade. A raw lidar archive costs roughly one to a few percent of the acquisition cost per decade at current object-storage prices (an order-of-magnitude figure; price it against your own storage provider); re-acquisition costs 100 % and cannot recover the past epoch.

**Energy and cost accounting.** Training foundation models and re-processing global archives has an energy footprint that is rarely reported; a cost-per-product-quality metric that includes compute would make the trade-off between re-flying and re-processing visible ([Chapter 28](ch28-reducing-cost.md)).

## 73.7 Policy

**Openness versus security.** The equilibrium has moved toward openness for two decades — SRTM, Landsat, Copernicus, 3DEP, the EU High-Value Datasets regulation (in force 2023) that makes national elevation data free by law — while the counter-pressure has sharpened: high-resolution DEMs and 3D city models are dual-use, GNSS interference is routine, and some states restrict elevation data at resolutions better than 30 m or require licensing of surveys ([Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)). The next decade's open question is whether openness survives the first widely publicized misuse of an open 1 m DTM, and whether the response is targeted (masking of specific sites, as in imagery) or blanket.

**Privacy in 3D.** A 5 cm point cloud of a street shows what is in the yards; a continuously updated one shows when it changed. Aerial DSMs at 10–25 cm already resolve swimming pools, extensions, and vehicles, and the GDPR's personal-data test applies when a dataset can be linked to a person. There is no accepted technique for "privacy-preserving 3D" analogous to face blurring; generalization destroys the product. The open problem is a policy one — what resolution, revisit, and attribute combination constitutes personal data — and the technical community has not yet given policymakers a vocabulary to decide it ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)).

**Licence contamination.** ML models trained on datasets with incompatible licences (share-alike, non-commercial, government-restricted) produce outputs whose licence status is unsettled. For elevation this is acute: the best training data are national lidar programmes with varying terms, and global ML DTMs trained on them are distributed under licences their inputs may not permit. A resolution — through licence metadata on training sets (OGC TrainingDML-AI), model documentation, or legislation — is needed before the first lawsuit decides it.

**International bathymetric data sharing.** Most of the unmapped ocean lies in exclusive economic zones whose coastal states have, under UNCLOS Part XIII, consent rights over marine scientific research and legitimate security concerns about seabed data. Seabed 2030's progress depends on the IHO's Crowdsourced Bathymetry (B-12) framework, on data-sharing agreements that let a state contribute a 400 m grid while withholding full resolution, and on **capacity building** so that coastal states map their own waters. Closing the **lidar deserts** — most of Africa, much of South America and Central Asia, and the small island states that need coastal elevation most — is a development problem as much as a technical one, and **data-sovereignty tooling** (processing in-country, exporting only derived products under the state's terms) is becoming a condition of access.

## 73.8 Education and workforce

The field's bottleneck is people who can hold geodesy, hydrology, and machine learning in one head. Curricula have added deep learning faster than they have retained error budgets; it is now possible to graduate with a geospatial degree able to fine-tune a segmentation model but unable to say what a geoid is, what NAVD88 is tied to, or why RMSE is not a 95 % bound. The remedy this book argues for is sequencing: error budgets, datums, and sampling before learning methods, so that the learner knows what the model's output means ([Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md), [Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

Certification lags practice. The IHO/FIG/ICA Category A and B hydrographic standards (S-5A/S-5B) and the ASPRS Certified Photogrammetrist, Mapping Scientist, and Lidar Technologist programmes were written for crewed, human-reviewed surveys; neither yet examines the supervision of autonomous platforms, the auditing of ML classification, or the propagation of uncertainty through learned pipelines. Updating them is the most direct lever on professional practice.

> **Worked example.** A planning exercise for a mixed team. A delta city with 6 mm/yr subsidence has a 2016 lidar DTM on NAVD88 (GEOID12B), NVA 8 cm. It plans a 2027 re-flight, which will be delivered on NAPGD2022. The geodesist expects a datum offset of about 0.6 m locally (an illustrative value; the real offset will come from the NGS transformation tool), with ±3 cm transformation uncertainty, and 11 yr × 6 mm/yr = 6.6 cm of real subsidence (±2 cm from the InSAR model). The hydrologist wants a DoD for flood-storage change; the ML engineer proposes a learned correction between the two. The correct sequence: transform both to one datum and epoch; estimate LoD₉₅ = 1.96 × √(0.08² + 0.08² + 0.03²) ≈ 0.23 m for independent errors; recognize that the 6.6 cm subsidence signal is below detection in any single cell and recoverable only by spatial averaging over areas large enough that correlated errors cancel; and only then consider whether a learned model adds anything the budget does not already explain.

## 73.10 What to do now

None of the challenges above requires waiting. A practitioner, a programme, or a company can act this year on six things.

**Adopt uncertainty-bearing formats.** Deliver BAG or S-102 for bathymetry; deliver a σ band (and, if you can, a correlation length) with every DTM; use COG/COPC/Zarr so the uncertainty travels with the data at no cost to users who ignore it. **Publish checkpoints.** Release the independent checkpoints you used for accuracy assessment — coordinates, dates, land-cover class, method — as open data alongside the product, so that others can retest it and so that catalogs can one day index by tested accuracy ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)). **Archive raw data.** Keep the point clouds, waveforms, water-column data, and trajectories with their calibration, in open formats, with checksums; budget it as a percentage of acquisition. **Participate in intercomparisons.** Submit products to DEMIX-style exercises and publish the results even when they are unflattering. **Write limitations sections.** Every product page should state what the data cannot do — surfaces not represented, land covers not tested, datum and epoch caveats — in plain language, as this book's chapters try to. **Keep an open-source path.** Ensure that every product you deliver can be read, transformed, and validated with open tools, so that its usability does not depend on a vendor's licence term or an API's survival ([Chapter 71](ch71-software-landscape.md)).

## Then & now

In 1958 Miller and Laflamme's digital terrain model was a grid of numbers on punched cards whose meaning — the ground, at some date, in some local datum, to some accuracy the engineers knew and did not write down — lived in the heads of the people who made it. In 2025 the grid is a cloud-native array that any laptop can open, and the meaning still mostly lives in a PDF, a tile-level metadata record, or nowhere. The sentence this book has been trying to make routine is: *the elevation of this place at this time, with this uncertainty, for this purpose, relative to this surface*. Every term in it is now technically expressible — epochs in WKT2 and STAC, uncertainty in BAG and S-102 and an extra band, purpose in product specifications, surface in classification codes — and none is yet habitual. The next decade's work is to make them so, and the measure of success is not a better sensor but a user, in 2035, who can answer those five questions about a dataset without opening a support ticket.

## Validation & uncertainty

The open problems of this chapter are, almost without exception, validation problems in disguise, and the next decade will make validation harder in three specific ways before it makes it easier.

**The reference is moving.** Validation has always assumed a reference 3–10 times better than the product, independent of it, and on the same datum ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)). Time-dependent frames break the third assumption unless epochs are reconciled; ML products trained on the same lidar that would otherwise serve as a reference break the second; and ICESat-2 and GEDI — the only global references — have per-footprint uncertainties (roughly 0.1–0.5 m over flat open ground, metres on slopes and under canopy) that are not 3× better than the products they are used to test in difficult terrain. The honest procedure is the one in [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md): stratify by slope and land cover, report the reference's own uncertainty, and subtract it in quadrature only when it is independent and known.

**Plausibility is cheaper than measurement.** Foundation-model fill, super-resolution, and learned corrections generate surfaces that pass every visual and most statistical checks while being, in places, invention. Standard checkpoint statistics do not detect this when checkpoints fall in the measured majority of cells. Validation must therefore (1) test inferred cells separately, using the provenance flag of §73.4; (2) test on held-out *regions*, because a model that has seen a neighbouring tile has effectively seen the test tile; and (3) check calibration, not just accuracy — a product that states a per-pixel 95 % interval should contain the reference 95 % of the time, and reliability diagrams or the probability integral transform ([Appendix B](../appendices/appendix-b-math-reference.md) §B.12) are the tools.

**Change is the product.** When the deliverable is a time series, the quantity to validate is a rate or a step, and its uncertainty depends on the spatial correlation of errors in each epoch more than on their per-pixel σ. The level of detection for a mean change over an area $A$ with correlation length $\ell$ shrinks not as $1/\sqrt{n}$ but as roughly $1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ with $n_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx A/(\pi \ell^2)$; ignoring this is the commonest overconfidence in published change maps ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)).

> **Uncertainty budget.** What the decade changes in a typical airborne-lidar DTM error budget (open flat terrain; 1σ; approximate).
>
> | Component | 2015 practice | 2030 target | What has to happen |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | GNSS/INS trajectory | 3–5 cm | 2–3 cm | PPP-AR, LEO-PNT augmentation, better IMUs |
> | Boresight/lever arm | 1–3 cm | 1 cm | Automated strip adjustment, continuous calibration |
> | Range/scanner | 1–2 cm | 1 cm | Single-photon noise models; waveform processing |
> | Ground classification (open) | 2–5 cm | 2 cm | Learned classifiers with audited error rates |
> | Geoid model | 2–5 cm (hybrid) | 1–2 cm (gravimetric) | GEOID2022-class models; airborne gravity |
> | Datum epoch / VLM | often ignored (0–10 cm) | modelled (1–2 cm) | Time-dependent frames; InSAR VLM models |
> | Reference uncertainty in test | 1–3 cm (GNSS RTK) | same | Independent checkpoints, published |
> | **Combined NVA (1.96σ)** | **≈ 10–15 cm** | **≈ 6–8 cm** | — |
>
> The largest single gain is not instrumental: it is treating the datum epoch and VLM as a modelled term instead of a silent one.

**What to report.** By 2030 an accuracy statement for an elevation product should include, beyond the ASPRS/NSSDA statistics: the datum realization, geoid model, and epoch; a per-pixel or per-class uncertainty with its correlation model; the fraction of cells inferred rather than measured, with their separate accuracy; the test regions and whether they overlap any training domain; the reference's own uncertainty; and a calibration statement for any stated interval. [Appendix G](../appendices/appendix-g-checklists.md) provides a template.

## Software

**Open source:** PROJ ≥ 7 (time-dependent transformations, point-motion operations, deformation-model grids — the infrastructure for §73.1, if the grids are installed); GDAL/rasterio and PDAL (COG, COPC, multi-band uncertainty layers; the Try-it box); xdem (DEM co-registration, spatially correlated uncertainty, and the correlation-length-aware error propagation of §Validation — the most direct open implementation of this chapter's statistics); GPyTorch and GPflow (scalable Gaussian-process terrain models); TorchGeo (2021) ⟨H⟩ and Clay (2024) ⟨H⟩ for foundation-model experiments on raster and point data, with the caveat that their geospatial splits default to random rather than regional held-out; Icechunk (2024) ⟨H⟩ and Zarr v3 for versioned, content-addressed array stores; MB-System and the NBS open tooling for supersession-style bathymetric compilation. Caveat for all ML tooling: calibration diagnostics are rarely built in; add them.

**Free but closed:** NGS NCAT/HTDP and the forthcoming NSRS modernization tools (datum and epoch transformations); NOAA BlueTopo/NBS data services; Google Earth Engine (large-scale intercomparison against ICESat-2/GEDI, with the caveat that its export limits and licence constrain reproducibility).

**Commercial:** TerraSolid, Trimble Inpho, and Esri ArcGIS Pro for production lidar and photogrammetry — all now include ML classification, none yet ships calibrated per-pixel uncertainty by default; CARIS HIPS/SIPS and QPS Qimera for hydrographic CUBE/BAG workflows, which remain the model for uncertainty-bearing production.

## Standards & guides

- **NGS, *Blueprint for the Modernized NSRS*, Parts 1–3 (NOAA TR NOS NGS 62, 64, 67; 2017–2021).** Geometric frames, geopotential datum, and the working-in-the-modernized-NSRS procedures; the definitive statement of §73.1.
- **IAG Resolution 1 (2015) on the International Height Reference System; IHRF conventions (ongoing).** Geopotential-number heights and $W_0$ — the international counterpart of NAPGD2022.
- **IHO S-100 Ed. 5.x (2022–) and S-102 Ed. 3.0.0 (2024).** Uncertainty-bearing gridded bathymetry and the product-specification framework into which living compilations fit.
- **IHO B-12 Guidance on Crowdsourced Bathymetry (Ed. 3.0.0, 2023).** The framework for volunteer and uncrewed-platform contributions to Seabed 2030.
- **USGS Lidar Base Specification 2024 (rev. A).** Current US lidar deliverable requirements, including accuracy by land-cover class and handling of single-photon/Geiger-mode data.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Ed. 2 (2023).** RMSE$_V$/RMSE$_H$ accuracy classes and checkpoint requirements; the baseline that per-pixel uncertainty reporting must extend.
- **ISO 19157-1:2023 (data quality) and ISO 19115-1 (metadata).** Vocabulary for encoding tested accuracy in catalogs (§73.6).
- **OGC TrainingDML-AI Part 1 (2023).** Metadata for ML training data, including licence and provenance — the hook for licence-contamination resolution (§73.7).
- **STAC 1.1 (2024) ⟨H⟩ and extensions (raster, projection, scientific).** Catalog encoding for epoch, CRS, and quality fields.
- **EU Regulation 2023/138 (High-Value Datasets) and INSPIRE 2007/2/EC.** Open elevation data as legal obligation in the EU.
- **NASEM 2018, *Thriving on Our Changing Planet* (Decadal Survey).** Surface topography and vegetation structure as a designated observable; the policy basis for ICESat-2/GEDI continuity.

## Pitfalls

- **Betting on a single sensor or model** → each new instrument fills one gap and brings one new error structure → design programmes around complementary sources and independent validation, not around the newest mission.
- **Assuming new frames will be adopted quickly** → every datum transition took a decade → plan for mixed-datum inputs through the 2030s and build datum/epoch tests into intake.
- **Shipping ML products without truth** → training-domain accuracy is reported as global accuracy → test on held-out regions, publish the training mask, and ship a provenance flag.
- **Treating a produced σ as a calibrated σ** → models emit uncertainty whether or not it is meaningful → check coverage (does the 95 % interval contain the reference 95 % of the time?) before publishing it.
- **Neglecting archives while chasing novelty** → raw data cost a few percent to keep and 100 % to re-acquire → budget archiving as a fixed fraction of acquisition.
- **Validating a time series as if epochs were independent** → correlated errors make rates overconfident → use effective sample size and co-registration residuals, not per-pixel σ.
- **Calling an inferred surface a DEM** → void fill and super-resolution are indistinguishable from measurement after gridding → the three-layer rule: value, calibrated uncertainty, flag.
- **Waiting for the surface ontology** → "bare earth" will remain ambiguous for years → state your inclusion/exclusion rules explicitly in metadata now.
- **Assuming openness is settled policy** → security and privacy pressures are rising → document the public benefit of each open release and design for targeted rather than blanket restriction.
- **Teaching deep learning before datums** → graduates cannot interpret their own outputs → sequence curricula: error budgets and reference systems first.
- **Trusting a vendor's cloud API as the archive** → services end → keep an open-format, open-tool path for every product.

## Key takeaways

- The next decade's gains will come as much from metadata, uncertainty, and openness as from sensors; most grand challenges are high on technical readiness and low on institutional readiness.
- NSRS modernization and kinematic global frames fix real problems and will produce a decade of mixed-datum data; record realization, geoid model, and epoch on everything.
- SWOT, NISAR, Biomass, smallsat stereo, and autonomous platforms each close a specific gap; none replaces airborne lidar or multibeam, and each needs years of independent validation.
- Products are becoming layered, time-stamped, uncertainty-bearing, and continuously updated; the hydrographic NBS/BlueTopo model of supersession is the template for land.
- Any method that produces elevation where none was measured must ship value, calibrated uncertainty, and a provenance flag; test it on held-out regions and check calibration.
- The surface ontology — agreed, machine-readable definitions of ground, building, and water with inclusion rules — is the deepest unsolved problem and needs no new hardware.
- Quality-first catalogs, published checkpoints, and persistent provenance are achievable now with existing standards.
- The validation discipline — independent reference, stratified statistics, honest reporting of what the data cannot do — is the part of this handbook least likely to be automated away.
