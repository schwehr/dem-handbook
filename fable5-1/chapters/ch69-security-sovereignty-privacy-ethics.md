# Chapter 69 — National security, sovereignty, privacy, and ethics

> **Part XV — Law, policy, security, privacy, ethics.** The companion to [Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md): not what the law requires, but who may map what, who may see it, who is helped or harmed, and how the field should behave when the law is silent.

**In this chapter.** Elevation data are powerful and therefore political. This chapter traces the security history of terrain and seafloor data — deliberately distorted Soviet maps, GPS Selective Availability, the staged release of SRTM, classified bathymetry versus Seabed 2030 — and the present mix of openness and new restriction (data localization, offset coordinate systems, drone rules, GNSS jamming). It then turns to privacy: what a 10 cm DSM or a mobile-mapping point cloud reveals about backyards, courtyards, and people; how inference (unpermitted construction detected from DSM change) blurs civic good and surveillance; which legal frameworks apply; and which de-identification and redaction techniques exist, with the artefacts each leaves behind. It covers equity ("lidar deserts," the unmapped seafloor, capacity building, Indigenous data sovereignty), the ethics of representation and inference in ML products, the environmental and animal-welfare footprint of surveying, and governance proposals. After reading it you should be able to run a release review for an elevation product — threats, harms, mitigations, residual risk — and document the trade-off you chose rather than stumbling into one.

## 69.1 Security: history and present

### 69.1.1 Secrecy, distortion, and the civilian release of precision

National mapping agencies began as military organizations — the Ordnance Survey, the Survey of India, the US Coast Survey's wartime roles — and for most of the twentieth century detailed topography was treated as a military asset. The Soviet Union went furthest: from the late 1930s, publicly available maps were deliberately distorted — features shifted, rivers moved, grids falsified — while the General Staff produced accurate 1:50,000 and larger-scale mapping of the USSR and much of the world, which surfaced in the West only after 1991 (Postnikov 2002; Davies and Kent 2017). The lesson that survives is that distortion, unlike omission, poisons every downstream use: a shifted river on a public map corrupted every civilian engineering project that trusted it.

Positioning was gated the same way. GPS **Selective Availability** (SA) degraded civilian accuracy to roughly 100 m horizontal (95 %) until it was switched off on 2 May 2000 (local time; 1 May in Washington) ⟨H⟩, after which civilian accuracy improved by an order of magnitude overnight ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)). The United States has since stated that SA capability was removed from GPS III satellites. The **Shuttle Radar Topography Mission** (SRTM, February 2000) was flown jointly by NASA and the National Imagery and Mapping Agency (now NGA); its 1″ (≈30 m) product was released for the United States in 2003 but held at 3″ (≈90 m) for the rest of the world until the US government announced the global 1″ release in September 2014, completed region by region through 2015. Meanwhile the **Kyl–Bingaman Amendment** ⟨H⟩ ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)) capped US commercial imagery of Israel at 2 m until the 0.4 m relaxation of 2020, a rare case of a resolution limit written into statute and then explicitly revisited against the "available from foreign sources" test.

### 69.1.2 Bathymetry: the last classified terrain

Seafloor depth remains the most restricted elevation surface. Detailed bathymetry supports submarine navigation, terrain-aided underwater positioning ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)), acoustic propagation modelling, and mine warfare, so navies classify their high-resolution multibeam, and several coastal states treat near-shore bathymetry as sensitive regardless of who collected it. Against this stands the **Nippon Foundation–GEBCO Seabed 2030** project (Mayer et al. 2018), which aims at a complete, publicly available bathymetric map of the ocean at target resolutions from 100 m in shallow water to 800 m in the abyss; the fraction of the seafloor mapped at those resolutions rose from about 6 % in 2017 to 26.1 % at the GEBCO_2024 release. A large part of the remainder is not unmapped but *unshared* — held by navies, oil companies, cable surveyors, and fishing fleets. The practical compromise emerging is **resolution-tiered release**: contributing a 400 m or 100 m grid while retaining the full-resolution data, which is defensible because the capabilities that make bathymetry sensitive depend on sub-100 m detail.

### 69.1.3 Restricted high-resolution DEMs and resolution caps

The TanDEM-X mission's global 12 m DEM is commercial; the **TREx** (TanDEM-X High Resolution Elevation Data Exchange) program, led by NGA and Germany's BKG with partner nations, produced an edited 12 m terrain product whose full version is restricted to participating governments, though a lower-resolution public edition has been released for parts of the coverage (verify). National airborne lidar policies range from fully open (US 3DEP, Netherlands AHN, Denmark, Finland, England) through registered-user access (parts of Switzerland, several Australian states) to unpublished. Some countries cap the resolution or accuracy that may be released or exported: India's 2021 thresholds (1 m horizontal, 3 m vertical) are explicit; China's restrictions are absolute for high-resolution data; several others operate by unpublished practice. Elevation data about *other* countries can also be restricted at the source — a national mapping agency may hold foreign DEMs under bilateral agreements that prohibit release — which is why a global product's licence can differ tile by tile.

### 69.1.4 Jamming, spoofing, and anti-drone rules

Since 2022 GNSS interference has become a routine operational hazard for surveys. Jamming and spoofing in the Baltic (notably around Kaliningrad and the Gulf of Finland), the Black Sea, the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Red Sea have caused aircraft to lose GNSS for extended periods, ships' AIS to report positions on land, and — directly relevant here — RTK and PPK trajectories to degrade or fail. Finnair suspended flights to Tartu, Estonia, for a month in 2024 after repeated GPS loss on approach. For elevation surveys the consequences are failed ambiguity fixes, trajectory excursions that survive into the point cloud as swath-to-swath offsets, and, under spoofing, *plausible but wrong* positions that pass naive QC ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), [Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md)). Mitigations are multi-constellation, multi-frequency receivers with interference monitoring, tightly coupled INS with long coasting ability, independent checks against control, and — above all — recording raw observables so that the event is visible in post-processing. Anti-drone regulation is the other operational constraint: no-fly geofences around airports, prisons, military sites, and critical infrastructure (FAA UAS Facility Maps and TFRs; EU geographical zones under Regulation 2019/947), plus national bans on foreign-manufactured drones for government work, define where a UAS survey may happen at all.

## 69.2 Sovereignty and national mapping control

The legal mechanisms of [Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md) — survey licensing, data localization, coordinate obfuscation — are expressions of a political claim: that the map of a territory belongs to the state that governs it. **India**, **China**, **Russia**, and most **Gulf states** require that surveys by foreign entities be licensed or conducted with national partners and that data remain in-country. China's **GCJ-02** system, which applies a non-linear, officially secret distortion of 50–500 m to WGS 84 coordinates, and Baidu's **BD-09** on top of it, mean that any elevation product combined with Chinese web-map geometry must be de-warped by reverse-engineered algorithms whose use is itself legally questionable. States also assert control over how foreign satellites image their territory: the UN Principles on Remote Sensing (1986) favour open access to data about one's own territory, but shutter control and resolution caps remain in national licensing regimes.

At sea, UNCLOS Part XIII requires a coastal state's consent for **marine scientific research** (MSR) in its EEZ and on its continental shelf (Articles 245–246), with consent to be granted "in normal circumstances." Whether **hydrographic surveying** is MSR is contested: the United States and others hold that hydrographic and military surveys are high-seas freedoms not subject to consent, while China, India, and others treat them as MSR requiring permission — the 2001 *Bowditch* and 2009 *Impeccable* incidents in the South China Sea arose from exactly this disagreement. A university or commercial multibeam survey in a foreign EEZ should obtain consent through diplomatic channels months in advance; data from unconsented surveys have been impounded and the vessels detained. Who "owns" the resulting map of an EEZ is unsettled in law and sharply felt in practice: coastal states expect copies of the data (Article 249 requires sharing with the coastal state), and may restrict publication.

Names and lines are the third sovereignty question. An elevation product that labels features or draws boundaries takes a position whether it intends to or not: Google Maps shows different borders in Kashmir to users in India, Pakistan, and elsewhere; the Persian/Arabian Gulf naming dispute has produced lawsuits and bans; Crimea's status differs by map domain. A DEM has no labels, but its tiling schemes, EEZ masks, and country-coded metadata do, and a mosaic that uses a disputed boundary to choose between two national datasets ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)) encodes a claim. The defensible practice is to document the source of every boundary used, to prefer neutral lines (UN cartographic section, Natural Earth with disputed-area flags) for non-political purposes, and to avoid implying that a technical seam is a border.

## 69.3 Dual use

Terrain data were military before they were civilian, and the uses have not diverged. **TERCOM** (terrain contour matching), fielded on US cruise missiles from the 1980s, navigates by correlating radar-altimeter profiles with stored DTED — the archetype of terrain-aided navigation ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)); digital scene matching, line-of-sight and radio-propagation analysis for communications and surveillance, **trafficability** (where vehicles can move), helicopter landing-zone selection, inundation modelling for dam-breach targeting, and tunnel detection from microgravity and subsidence all consume the same DEMs that civil engineers use. The resolution–security trade-off debate therefore has a real core: a 1 m DSM of a port is more useful to an attacker than a 30 m DTM. It also has a weak core, because by the 2020s global 30 m DEMs, sub-metre commercial imagery, and open street-level 3D exist for most of the world, so the marginal advantage conferred by restricting a specific national dataset is small while the civil cost — in flood mapping, infrastructure planning, disaster response — is large. Schumann and Bates (2018) made the case for a global open high-accuracy DEM on precisely these grounds.

Disaster response is where the trade-off is tested under time pressure. The **International Charter "Space and Major Disasters"** (operational since 2000) and the **Copernicus Emergency Management Service** (2012) release imagery and derived products within hours of activation; pre-event DEMs determine the quality of flood-depth, landslide-volume, and debris-flow products ([Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md), [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). After the 2010 Haiti earthquake, the 2015 Nepal earthquake, and the 2023 Türkiye–Syria earthquakes, the limiting factor for damage assessment was less imagery than the absence of recent high-resolution elevation to compare against. A policy that restricts a national DEM in peacetime should state how it will be released in a declared emergency, to whom, and through which channel — in advance, not during.

> **Case file.** *Strava Global Heatmap, January 2018.* Strava published an updated heatmap of about 1 billion GPS-tracked activities. Within days, analysts (Nathan Ruser and others) showed that it revealed the layout of military bases and patrol routes in Afghanistan, Syria, and elsewhere, where soldiers' fitness trackers were the only people recording activities (Hern 2018). None of the data were classified or individually sensitive; the aggregate was. The incident is the canonical **mosaic effect**: harmless pieces combine into a sensitive whole. Its elevation analogue is a DSM time series that, cell by cell, reveals nothing, but whose change layer shows new earthworks, a widened runway, or a dug-in position. Release reviews should ask what the *derived* products will show, not only the base layer.

## 69.4 Privacy

### 69.4.1 What 3D data reveal

Privacy debates about geospatial data were built around imagery — faces, licence plates, a person at a window — and the norms that resulted (blurring, opt-out, camera-height limits) address imagery. Elevation data raise a different set of exposures. A 10 cm airborne DSM or an oblique 3D city model shows what is behind the fence: pools, extensions, sheds, solar panels, vehicles, a trampoline, the layout of a garden — the **elevated-view "peeking"** problem that courtyard and roof-terrace cultures (Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, East Asian cities) feel acutely and that a street-level camera never had. Mobile-mapping and terrestrial point clouds capture people as recognizable silhouettes with gait, height, and clothing shape even when no image is stored; at 2–5 mm spacing a face in a terrestrial scan is identifiable. Indoor SLAM scans ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)) of homes, offices, and hospitals record floor plans, furniture, and contents — a burglar's survey or a landlord's inspection, depending on who holds it. And because elevation is *measured*, it supports inference that imagery only suggests: a DSM difference between two epochs detects a new 2.8 m extension with a volume, a date range, and an address attached ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md), [Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

That last capability is the sharpest ethical edge, because its civic and surveillance uses are the same computation. Greek authorities used satellite imagery in 2010 to find undeclared swimming pools in Athens suburbs (press reports at the time cited 16,974 pools found against 324 declared; the figure is widely repeated but we have not traced it to a primary source); France's tax authority announced in 2022 that an AI pipeline on aerial imagery had found more than 20,000 undeclared pools, yielding about €10 million in tax. The same methods find unpermitted construction in informal settlements, where "enforcement" means demolition. Whether a DSM-change product that outs private construction is a public good depends entirely on who is on the receiving end, and a producer cannot control that after release.

### 69.4.2 Legal frameworks, briefly

The EU **GDPR** (2016/679) treats any information relating to an identifiable natural person as personal data, including location and 3D representations; it requires a lawful basis, purpose limitation, **data minimization** (Article 5(1)(c)), storage limitation, and data-protection impact assessments for systematic monitoring of public areas. The California **CCPA/CPRA** (2018/2020) and Canada's **PIPEDA** (2000) are narrower but cover precise geolocation. US constitutional law on aerial observation is permissive — *California v. Ciraolo* (1986) and *Florida v. Riley* (1989) allowed warrantless observation of a backyard from 1,000 ft and 400 ft respectively, and *Dow Chemical v. United States* (1986) allowed aerial mapping-camera photography of an industrial plant — but *Kyllo v. United States* (2001) drew a line at technology "not in general public use" that reveals details of the home interior, and *Carpenter v. United States* (2018) recognized a privacy interest in aggregated location history. Whether a persistent 5 cm DSM or a terrestrial scan through windows is "in general public use" is the open question; the Fourth Circuit's 2021 *Leaders of a Beautiful Struggle v. Baltimore Police Department* decision, which held persistent wide-area aerial surveillance unconstitutional, suggests the trend. The practical standard for a non-governmental producer is the GDPR one even outside Europe, because it is the most demanding and the most operationalized.

### 69.4.3 De-identification: methods and the artefacts they leave

Every redaction method changes the data in ways that a careful user — or adversary — can detect. Choose with the artefact in mind.

| Method | Applied to | What it removes | Artefact left behind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blur faces/plates | imagery, textured meshes | identity of people/vehicles | visible blur patches; location of people still evident |
| Remove moving objects | point clouds (multi-pass, temporal filtering) | people, vehicles | holes/occlusion shadows behind removed objects; lower density |
| Clip or flatten polygons | DSM/DTM, point clouds | sensitive sites, private parcels | sharp edges, flat plateaus; the mask itself advertises the site |
| Coarsen (resample to 5–30 m) | DSM/DTM | small structures, detail | loss for all users; still shows large features |
| Add noise (Gaussian, ±σ) | DSM/DTM | fine detail | spatially white noise is trivially filtered; correlated noise biases slopes |
| Generalize to land-cover height classes | DSM | individual objects | step edges, class boundaries |
| Aggregate (statistics per block) | derived products | per-parcel inference | fine for reporting, useless for most elevation uses |
| Differential privacy on aggregates | counts, change statistics | re-identification from totals | calibrated noise; not applicable to the raster itself |

Three warnings. First, **masking announces**: the blurred rectangle on Google Earth over a Dutch military site in the 2000s became a famous index of sensitive locations, and a flattened patch in a lidar DTM is just as conspicuous — if the goal is to hide a *location*, redaction must be applied to a decoy set as well, or the whole class of feature coarsened. Second, **removed is not gone** if other epochs, other sensors, or other publishers show the same place; the mosaic effect works against redaction. Third, **hydrology and derivatives break**: flattening a parcel in a DTM creates a sink or a dam ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)); coarsening changes slope statistics; noise destroys curvature. Document every redaction in metadata ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)) as a quality element — users cannot interpret a product whose edits are secret.

### 69.4.4 Minimization and retention

The cheapest privacy control is to not collect or not keep. Fly at the resolution the use requires ([Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md)); a flood study does not need 5 cm. Record the sensor's imagery only where it is needed for colourization or classification, and discard it after use if retention is not required. Set retention periods for raw mobile-mapping data in which individuals are identifiable (many European operators delete raw imagery within months and keep only blurred derivatives). Separate the identifiable layer (imagery, dense point cloud) from the published layer (DTM, generalized DSM) with different access controls. And decide *before* collection who may request the raw data — including law enforcement, which will ask.

## 69.5 Equity and access

The distribution of elevation data is as unequal as the distribution of wealth. The United States, most of Europe, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand have national or near-national airborne lidar; most of Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America rely on SRTM-era 30 m global DEMs with vertical errors of several metres in exactly the vegetated, low-relief floodplains where flood risk is highest. Schumann and Bates (2018) called these **lidar deserts** and argued that the gap in hazard mapping is now a gap in elevation data more than in models; Hawker et al. (2018) quantified how much flood-inundation estimates in the Global South depend on DEM choice. Offshore, Seabed 2030's unmapped majority is concentrated in the Southern Ocean, the Pacific, and the EEZs of small island states that cannot afford multibeam ships. Cost barriers compound: a commercial 12 m global DEM licence or a national lidar campaign is out of reach of many agencies, which is why open releases (Copernicus DEM, 3DEP, EU high-value datasets) have outsized value in poorer regions even though they were funded for richer ones.

Capacity building is the structural response. The IHO **Capacity Building Sub-Committee** funds training and equipment for developing hydrographic offices; the Nippon Foundation–GEBCO training program at the University of New Hampshire has trained scholars from 46 countries since 2004 (per Seabed 2030, 2024); OpenTopography, the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team, and regional lidar consortia lower processing and access barriers. **Indigenous data sovereignty** — the CARE principles and Canada's OCAP ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)) — asks that communities govern data about their lands and waters and share in the benefits of surveys over them: copies of the data, training, employment, and a say in what is published, especially the locations of cultural sites that lidar reveals under canopy. A benefit-sharing clause in a survey plan is cheap; its absence has stopped projects.

## 69.6 Ethics of representation and inference

Machine-learning elevation products — bare-earth DTMs inferred from DSMs, super-resolved grids, SDB from imagery, building heights from single images ([Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md), [Chapter 45](ch45-super-resolution.md)) — look exactly like measured ones, and users treat them as truth. Three ethical obligations follow. **Disclose the inference**: metadata and the product itself should distinguish measured, interpolated, and predicted cells ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)), because a predicted floodplain elevation is a hypothesis. **Report bias by geography**: models trained on North American and European lidar underperform in tropical forests, informal settlements, and arid badlands, and a published accuracy that averages over the training domain overstates performance where the product will do the most harm. **Beware downstream decisions**: an overstated DEM accuracy flows into flood maps, into insurance pricing, into mortgage availability — a form of algorithmic redlining in which the cheapest data describe the poorest neighbourhoods worst. Publishing uncertainty is therefore an ethical duty, not a technical nicety ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)), and the uncertainty must be spatially explicit, not a single national RMSE.

Dual-use review belongs in methods papers too. A technique that estimates building-level occupancy or structural vulnerability from a DSM is a disaster-preparedness tool and a targeting tool; the authors cannot prevent the second use, but they can state it, can choose what to release (method versus trained model versus global product), and can avoid publishing the most sensitive derived layers. Several journals and funders now request such statements.

## 69.7 Environmental and animal-welfare ethics of surveying

Surveying is not passive. Mid-frequency multibeam sonars (12 kHz systems used in deep water, in particular) emit at levels and frequencies that overlap marine-mammal hearing, and an International Whaling Commission expert panel concluded in 2013 that a 12 kHz multibeam survey was the most plausible trigger for the 2008 mass stranding of melon-headed whales in Madagascar (Southall et al. 2013). Most shallow-water multibeams (200–400 kHz) are above the hearing range of most species, but sub-bottom profilers, sparkers, and seismic sources are not. Permitting regimes — the US Marine Mammal Protection Act incidental-harassment authorizations, the UK JNCC guidelines for geophysical surveys (2017), Australia's EPBC Act referrals — require marine-mammal observers, soft starts, exclusion zones, and shutdowns, and surveyors should plan them in rather than treat them as a nuisance. Drones disturb wildlife: nesting seabirds, raptors, marine mammals on haul-outs, and ungulates in winter all show measurable stress responses at altitudes commonly flown for mapping (Mulero-Pázmány et al. 2017), so survey windows and minimum altitudes over sensitive habitat belong in the flight plan. Finally, the energy and carbon footprint of large campaigns and of ML training is non-trivial — a national lidar campaign flies thousands of aircraft hours, and training a large model emits on the order of tonnes of CO₂ (Strubell et al. 2019) — and belongs in the same cost–benefit that decides whether to fly at 8 or 2 points per square metre ([Chapter 28](ch28-reducing-cost.md)).

## 69.8 Governance proposals

The field needs institutions, not just intuitions. Six proposals recur in the literature and in agency practice and deserve adoption. (1) **Tiered access with declassification schedules**: publish a coarse product now, a medium product to vetted users, and commit to a date at which the full product becomes open unless a renewed justification is filed — the US practice of automatic declassification after a fixed period is the model. (2) **Open metadata even when data are closed**: the existence, extent, resolution, date, and accuracy of a restricted survey should be discoverable ([Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md)), so that users can seek access and funders avoid duplicate collection. (3) **Independent quality audits of restricted DEMs**: a restricted product's accuracy claims cannot be checked by the community, so an independent body should test them against checkpoints and publish the results — "restricted" must not mean "unvalidated." (4) **Responsible-use statements for products**: a short, plain-language section in product documentation listing intended uses, known limitations, and uses the producer considers inappropriate (building-level exposure of individuals, for example). (5) **Community review in survey planning**: for surveys over inhabited or Indigenous lands, a documented consultation step with the authority to change the plan, modelled on human-subjects review. (6) **Release review as a standard pipeline stage**: before publication, a checklist covering privacy, sensitive sites, dual use, licensing, and redaction artefacts, with the outcome recorded ([Appendix G](../appendices/appendix-g-checklists.md)).

## Then & now

National surveys began secret ⟨H⟩ — military departments producing maps that civilians could buy only in degraded form, if at all — and the Soviet case made degradation deliberate. The **civilian GPS** decision (1983, after KAL 007) and the **removal of Selective Availability** (2000) ⟨H⟩ moved precise positioning into the public domain; **SRTM** (2000, global 1″ by 2015) did the same for terrain, and the open release of 3DEP, Copernicus DEM, and the EU's high-value datasets turned openness into the default for land. The present is mixed: Seabed 2030 pushes bathymetry toward openness while navies hold the detail; India and China write thresholds and localization into law; drone rules and GNSS interference constrain collection in ways that did not exist in 2015. Privacy moved from imagery (Street View blurring, 2008–2012) to 3D and to inference (pool detection by AI, 2022; DSM change products), and from "can you see a face" to "what can be computed about a household." The ethics conversation moved from critical cartography's critique of the map as power (Harley 1989; Pickles 1995) to operational questions — release reviews, responsible-use statements, CARE — that producers can actually implement.

## Validation & uncertainty

Security, privacy, and ethics decisions are decisions under uncertainty about *capability* — what a dataset enables — and the same quantitative tools that validate a DEM also bound what it can reveal. Four quantities should be computed and recorded in a release review.

**Detectability of features.** Whether a DSM reveals a 0.4 m-high garden wall, a 2 m shed, or a 0.3 m earthwork is a question about effective resolution and vertical noise ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)). A feature of height $h_f$ and footprint of $n$ cells is detectable against noise $\sigma_z$ roughly when $h_f > k\,\sigma_z/\sqrt{n}$ for a detection threshold $k$ of 2–3, provided the footprint exceeds the effective resolution. A 1 m-posting DSM with $\sigma_z$ = 0.15 m detects a 2 m × 2 m shed 1.5 m high trivially ($n$ = 4, threshold ≈ 0.2 m) and a 0.3 m earthwork over 10 m × 10 m with margin ($n$ = 100, threshold ≈ 0.04 m). Coarsening to 10 m posting removes the shed from the resolvable set but *not* the earthwork. Redaction by coarsening therefore protects against some inferences and not others; compute which.

**Minimum detectable change.** For inference from DSM differences, the quantity is the **level of detection** LoD$_{95}$ = $1.96\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$ of [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md). With two epochs at $\sigma_z$ = 0.15 m, LoD$_{95}$ ≈ 0.42 m per cell, and far less when averaged over a structure's footprint; a 2.8 m extension is unambiguous. If a producer wishes a published change product *not* to support parcel-level construction inference, the noise or aggregation applied must raise the footprint-averaged LoD above the heights of interest — which, for a 2.5 m single-storey addition, means degradation so severe that the product is useless for its legitimate purposes. The honest conclusion is usually that the inference cannot be prevented by data treatment and must be governed by access and use terms instead.

**Residual identifiability after redaction.** For point clouds, measure what remains: after removing clusters, compute the fraction of ground area under removed objects left as holes, and whether the hole geometry (a 0.5 m × 1.8 m void) still indicates a person's position. For imagery-derived meshes, run a face/plate detector over the *textures* after blurring and report the miss rate on a labelled sample; blur pipelines that are tuned on street-level imagery miss oblique and small faces.

**Spatial accuracy disclosure as an ethical statistic.** Report accuracy stratified by land cover and by region, with per-stratum sample sizes, not as a single number. A product with national RMSE$_z$ = 0.5 m that is 0.2 m on open ground in the capital and 2 m under forest in the periphery is misreported by its mean. Where checkpoints are unavailable in a region, say so; the absence of validation is itself a finding that users in that region need ([Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md)).

> **Worked example.** *Does adding noise protect a parcel from construction inference?* A city proposes releasing a 0.5 m DSM with Gaussian noise of σ = 0.3 m added to each cell to "protect privacy." A 4 m × 6 m single-storey extension (2.7 m high) covers $n$ = 96 cells. The footprint-mean of the noise has σ = $0.3/\sqrt{96}$ = 0.031 m, so the extension is detected at a signal-to-noise ratio of 2.7/0.031 ≈ 88. Even σ = 3 m of added white noise leaves SNR ≈ 9. White noise fails because it averages out; only *spatially correlated* noise with a correlation length comparable to the structure (several metres) or outright aggregation to 10–20 m blocks defeats the inference — and both destroy the DSM's legitimate uses (solar potential, drainage, line-of-sight). The release decision therefore cannot be made by data treatment alone; it must be made by deciding whether parcel-level inference is acceptable and, if not, by licence terms and access tiers.

> | Construction inference | DSM change | high | medium (varies by neighbourhood) | responsible-use terms; no public change layer |
> | Sensitive-site disclosure | detail at listed sites | medium | high | coarsen class of sites, not individual sites |
> | Person identifiability | MMS point clouds | medium | high | cluster removal; raw data retention 6 months |
> | Mosaic with other data | combination with cadastre/imagery | high | medium | document; cannot be prevented |

## Software

**Open source:** PDAL (filters.cluster, filters.hag_nn, expression filters) for auditable object removal from point clouds; CloudCompare for interactive review of redaction results; open face- and plate-detection models (e.g., RetinaFace, YOLO-family detectors trained on open datasets; the `understand-ai anonymizer` and similar) for blurring textures — caveat: trained on street-level imagery, weak on oblique/aerial views; GDAL (`gdalwarp -r average`, `gdal_rasterize` masks) for coarsening and polygon masking with a logged command line; OpenDP and Google's differential-privacy libraries for aggregate statistics; STAC/pystac for publishing open metadata about closed data; the RTKLIB observables and `gnss-interference` monitoring scripts for detecting jamming in raw logs. **Free but closed:** national geofence/no-fly map services (FAA B4UFLY/LAANC providers; EU national UAS zone portals); NOAA/NGA public release summaries. **Commercial:** Cyclomedia, Google, and Apple blurring pipelines (not purchasable separately but define norms); Brighter AI, Celantur for imagery anonymization at scale; Esri and Hexagon tools for masked publishing; restricted government distribution systems (NGA, national defence geospatial portals) — caveat: closed pipelines cannot be audited by the data subject or the user, so demand published miss rates.

## Pitfalls

- **Collecting high-resolution 3D data in a jurisdiction that requires permits or local partners.** Teams assume survey rules are like aviation rules → check national geospatial regulations (India, China, Russia, Gulf states) before mobilizing; budget for impounded data.
- **Running a multibeam survey in a foreign EEZ without MSR consent.** "Hydrography is not MSR" is a contested position → request consent months ahead; expect to share data with the coastal state.
- **Publishing a DSM change layer that outs private construction without a harm review.** The computation is easy and the harm asymmetric → run a release review; consider use terms rather than a public change product.
- **Treating white-noise addition as privacy protection.** It averages out over any footprint → compute footprint SNR; use aggregation or access control instead.
- **Redacting individual sensitive sites with visible masks.** The mask becomes the index → coarsen the whole class of features or apply decoys; document the policy, not the list.
- **Assuming an export-controlled IMU or GNSS/INS can travel in checked baggage.** It is a controlled item regardless of packaging → classify equipment and obtain licences or exceptions.
- **Taking a restricted DEM's stated accuracy on faith.** Nobody outside can test it → insist on an independent audit or treat the claim as unverified.
- **Trusting a GNSS trajectory in an interference zone because the solution "fixed."** Spoofed solutions look healthy → log raw observables, monitor C/N₀ and AGC, and check against control.
- **Using a single national RMSE in the metadata of an ML-inferred product.** Averages hide regional failure → stratify by land cover and region; flag unvalidated areas.
- **Ignoring wildlife and marine-mammal mitigation in survey planning.** Permits take months and shutdowns cost days → plan observers, soft starts, and seasonal windows from the start.
- **Forgetting that aggregation in other people's hands reverses your redaction.** The mosaic effect is not under your control → state residual risk honestly; do not promise anonymity.
- **Skipping community consultation on Indigenous or inhabited lands.** Projects have been stopped and data withdrawn → build CARE/OCAP-consistent agreements into the plan.

## Key takeaways

- Elevation data are political: the history of terrain and bathymetry is a history of what states chose to hide, distort, or release, and that history is still being written.
- Know the regime before you fly, sail, or publish — survey licensing, data localization, MSR consent, drone zones, export control — and assume GNSS interference is possible in conflict-adjacent regions.
- Openness has large, measurable civil benefits and usually small marginal security costs once global 30 m DEMs and sub-metre imagery exist; where restriction is justified, tier it, schedule its end, and keep metadata open.
- Privacy exposure from 3D data is about elevated views and inference, not faces; the same DSM-change computation is civic enforcement and surveillance, and data treatment cannot separate the two — governance must.
- Redaction leaves artefacts: masks announce, white noise averages out, flattening breaks hydrology; compute what each method actually prevents and document it in metadata.
- Publishing spatially explicit uncertainty is an ethical duty: a single national RMSE misrepresents exactly the places where elevation data do the most harm.
- Equity gaps (lidar deserts, the unmapped seafloor) are now the limiting factor in hazard mapping for much of the world; open data and capacity building are the remedies.
- Build a release review into the pipeline: threats, harms, mitigations, residual risk, responsible-use statement — recorded, not improvised.
