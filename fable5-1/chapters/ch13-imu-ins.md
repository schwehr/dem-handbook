# Chapter 13 — IMU/INS, motion sensing, and GNSS-aided navigation

> **Part IV — Positioning and orientation.** GNSS ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) tells a platform where its antenna is a few times per second; this chapter covers the inertial sensors that tell a scanning sensor where it is *pointing* and where it is *between* GNSS fixes — the orientation and short-term motion that every lidar, sonar, and camera on a moving platform needs.

**In this chapter.** A lidar pulse or a sonar beam leaves the sensor at a known angle in the sensor's frame; turning that into a ground coordinate requires the sensor's position *and* its attitude at the instant of the measurement, to a few thousandths of a degree. Inertial measurement units supply the attitude and fill the gaps between GNSS epochs, and the Kalman-filtered combination of the two — the GNSS/INS trajectory — is the single largest determinant of accuracy for airborne lidar, multibeam bathymetry, and mobile mapping. This chapter covers the sensors and their error model (bias, scale factor, noise, random walk, Allan variance), strapdown mechanization and attitude representations, heading sources, Kalman filtering and smoothing, lever arms and boresight, the timing effects that turn milliseconds into decimetres, marine motion (roll, pitch, heave, induced heave) and aircraft and vehicle dynamics as elevation error sources, dead reckoning under water, and how to judge a trajectory from its own statistics and from the mapped data. You should finish able to compute the elevation error from an attitude error at a given range, write a lever-arm and boresight budget, and read an SBET quality report critically.

## 13.1 Sensors, grades, and the error model

An **inertial measurement unit (IMU)** contains three gyroscopes measuring angular rate about orthogonal axes and three accelerometers measuring **specific force** — acceleration minus gravity, which is why a stationary IMU on a bench reads 9.81 m s⁻² upward. An **inertial navigation system (INS)** is an IMU plus the computer that integrates its outputs into attitude, velocity, and position. The sensor technologies differ by three orders of magnitude in performance and price:

| Grade | Gyro technology | Gyro bias stability | Angle random walk | Accel bias | Approx. cost | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer / automotive MEMS | Vibrating-structure MEMS | 10–100 °/h | 0.1–1 °/√h | 1–10 mg | US$1–100 | Phones, drones' flight controllers, cars |
| Industrial / tactical MEMS | High-end MEMS | 0.5–10 °/h | 0.05–0.3 °/√h | 0.1–1 mg | US$0.5–5 k | Small drones, handheld scanners, low-cost mobile mapping |
| Tactical FOG / RLG | Fibre-optic or ring-laser gyro | 0.1–1 °/h | 0.01–0.1 °/√h | 0.05–0.5 mg | US$10–50 k | Airborne lidar, MBES motion units, mobile mapping |
| Navigation grade | RLG, high-end FOG, hemispherical resonator | 0.001–0.01 °/h | ≤ 0.002 °/√h | 10–50 µg | US$50–200 k+ | Aircraft, ships, submarines, AUV dead reckoning |

Values are indicative ranges compiled from manufacturer specifications and Groves (2013); grades blur at the boundaries and "tactical" in particular covers a wide span. The relevant number for a mapping sensor is not the gyro grade in isolation but the attitude accuracy achieved *after* GNSS aiding: 0.005–0.01° roll and pitch and 0.01–0.03° heading for a tactical-grade airborne or marine unit, 0.02–0.05° and 0.1–0.3° for industrial MEMS, with heading always the weakest ([§13.2](#132-strapdown-mechanization-attitude-and-heading)).

**Ring-laser gyros (RLG)** and **fibre-optic gyros (FOG)** measure rotation through the Sagnac effect — counter-propagating light in a rotating loop has a path difference proportional to the rotation rate — and have no moving parts, excellent scale-factor linearity, and bias stability limited by lock-in (RLG, mitigated by dither) or by fibre thermal effects (FOG). **MEMS gyros** sense Coriolis force on a vibrating proof mass; their biases are temperature-dependent and vary from turn-on to turn-on by more than the in-run stability, so the filter must estimate them continuously. Accelerometers are pendulous force-rebalance (navigation and tactical grades) or MEMS capacitive (lower grades).

**The error model.** For each axis, the measured rate $\tilde\omega$ and specific force $\tilde f$ are related to the true values by

$$\tilde\omega = (1 + s_g)\,\omega + b_g + n_g, \qquad \tilde f = (1 + s_a)\,f + b_a + n_a,$$

with **bias** $b$ (a slowly varying offset, split into a turn-on bias and an in-run instability modelled as a random walk or first-order Gauss–Markov process), **scale factor error** $s$ (parts per million to percent; relevant in turns and vibration), misalignment and non-orthogonality (a small matrix, calibrated at manufacture), and **noise** $n$, which when integrated produces **angle random walk (ARW)** for gyros and **velocity random walk (VRW)** for accelerometers — the attitude or velocity uncertainty grows as $\sigma \cdot \sqrt{t}$. ARW is quoted in °/√h, so a 0.1 °/√h gyro integrated for 1 h without aiding accumulates 0.1° (1σ) of random attitude error, for 1 min $0.1\sqrt{1/60} = 0.013°$, and for 1 s 0.0017°. The in-run **bias instability** is the floor of the Allan deviation plot (Mathematics), the point at which averaging longer stops helping and slow drift takes over; it sets how well the filter can know the bias between GNSS updates. **Allan variance** analysis (IEEE Std 952; El-Sheimy, Hou & Niu 2008) of a few hours of static data identifies these terms from the slope of the log–log plot: −½ for white noise (ARW/VRW), 0 for bias instability, +½ for rate random walk. It is the first test to run on any IMU whose datasheet you do not trust.

> **Rule of thumb.** Attitude error scales with range. An angular error $\delta\theta$ (radians) at slant range $R$ produces a positional error of $R\,\delta\theta$ perpendicular to the beam. $0.01° = 1.75 \times 10^{-4}$ rad gives 1.7 cm at 100 m, 17 cm at 1 km, and 52 cm at 3 km. For a nadir-looking sensor, roll and pitch errors map mostly into horizontal error at nadir and increasingly into vertical error off-nadir: the vertical component is $R\,\delta\theta\,\sin\phi$ for scan angle $\phi$, so 0.01° roll at 1 km range and 20° off nadir gives 6 cm of height error at the swath edge and essentially none at nadir — which is why attitude errors appear as swath-edge mismatches and "roll" between overlapping lines ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md), [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)).

## 13.2 Strapdown mechanization, attitude, and heading

In a **strapdown** system the IMU is rigidly attached to the vehicle and the attitude is maintained numerically rather than by a stabilized platform. The mechanization integrates in a chosen navigation frame (usually local-level north-east-down, or Earth-centred Earth-fixed): the gyro rates, corrected for Earth rotation and for the rotation of the local-level frame as the vehicle moves (transport rate), update the attitude; the attitude rotates the measured specific force into the navigation frame; gravity (from a model, [Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)) and Coriolis terms are added; the result is integrated to velocity and again to position. Update rates are 100–1 000 Hz, and the coning and sculling corrections that handle rotation and acceleration within a sample interval are where low-rate or poorly implemented systems lose accuracy under vibration.

**Attitude representations.** Three are in use and all appear in mapping software. **Euler angles** (roll $\phi$, pitch $\theta$, heading/yaw $\psi$) are what humans read and what SBET files store; they are intuitive but singular at ±90° pitch and their *order* (which rotation first) and *sign conventions* (roll positive port-up or starboard-up; heading from north clockwise) differ between vendors — a documented source of entire-swath errors when a trajectory from one package is used in another. The **direction cosine matrix (DCM)** $R^n_b$, a 3 × 3 orthonormal rotation from body to navigation frame, is unambiguous and is what the lever-arm and boresight equations use. **Quaternions** (four parameters, unit norm) are the integration representation of choice because they have no singularity and renormalize cheaply. Convert between them with care and test with a known rotation.

**Heading** is the hard axis. Roll and pitch are observable from the accelerometers whenever the vehicle is not accelerating (gravity defines down), but heading has no gravitational reference. Four sources exist. **Gyrocompassing** — sensing the horizontal component of Earth's rotation (15 °/h × cos latitude) — requires gyros with bias well under 0.01 °/h and is the preserve of navigation-grade units; it fails near the poles and takes minutes to tens of minutes to settle. **GNSS-derived heading** comes either from the velocity vector (course over ground, which equals heading only when there is no crab, sideslip, or current — rarely true for a survey launch or an aircraft in crosswind) or from a **dual-antenna** baseline, which gives true heading to about $0.1°/L$ for a baseline of $L$ metres (roughly 0.1° at 1 m, 0.05° at 2 m) independent of motion, and is standard on survey vessels and increasingly on drones and mobile mappers. **Magnetometers** give magnetic heading, which must be corrected for declination from the World Magnetic Model or IGRF (declination varies from a few degrees to over 20° and changes by up to tenths of a degree per year) and is corrupted by any nearby ferrous or current-carrying object — a steel vessel, a drone's motors, a car body — by many degrees; magnetometers are acceptable for consumer drones and useless for survey-grade attitude. Finally, **dynamic alignment** in the Kalman filter makes heading observable from horizontal accelerations: when the vehicle turns or accelerates, a heading error produces a velocity error that GNSS detects. This is why airborne and marine systems need a few S-turns or figure-eights after take-off or departure to converge heading, why heading accuracy degrades during long straight lines, and why a mobile mapping van parked at a traffic light for two minutes loses heading observability.

## 13.3 GNSS/INS integration, Kalman filtering, and smoothing

The INS is accurate over seconds and drifts over minutes; GNSS is accurate over minutes and noisy (or absent) over seconds. The **Kalman filter** (Kálmán 1960) combines them as a recursive least-squares estimator: a **predict** step propagates the state and its covariance forward with the INS mechanization and a process-noise model derived from the IMU's Allan parameters, and an **update** step corrects the state when a GNSS (or other) measurement arrives, weighting the correction by the ratio of predicted to measured uncertainty (Mathematics). Mapping systems use the **error-state** (indirect) formulation: the filter estimates the *errors* of the INS solution — position, velocity, and attitude errors, plus gyro and accelerometer biases and scale factors, typically 15–21 states — rather than the state itself, which keeps the filter linear and lets the INS run at full rate while the filter updates at GNSS rate.

Three coupling architectures differ in what the GNSS contributes. **Loosely coupled** integration feeds GNSS *positions and velocities* to the filter; it is simple and modular but needs at least four satellites for any update and discards the information in a three-satellite epoch. **Tightly coupled** integration feeds GNSS *pseudoranges, carrier phases, and Dopplers* as measurements and includes the receiver clock in the state, so that even one or two satellites constrain the solution during partial outages, cycle slips can be detected against the INS prediction, and ambiguities can be re-fixed faster after an outage because the INS provides a precise position prior. **Deeply (ultra-tightly) coupled** integration closes the loop into the receiver's tracking loops, using the INS to aid signal tracking under jamming and high dynamics; it is a military and research technique with limited presence in mapping. Tightly coupled processing is the norm in POSPac, Inertial Explorer, and Qinertia for airborne and marine work because the environments (banked turns, bridges, canopy edges) produce exactly the partial outages where it helps.

A filter is causal: at each epoch it knows only the past. After the survey, an **optimal smoother** — the Rauch–Tung–Striebel (RTS) algorithm, or an equivalent forward–backward combination — runs backward through the stored filter states and covariances to produce an estimate at each epoch conditioned on *all* the data, which halves the error during outages (the drift is constrained from both ends) and removes the sawtooth at each GNSS update. The output is the **smoothed best estimate of trajectory (SBET)** — position, velocity, attitude, and their standard deviations at 100–200 Hz in a vendor binary format — which is the trajectory used to georeference the lidar or sonar data. The companion file of smoothed standard deviations (Applanix's SMRMSG) is the first place to look when judging a trajectory (§13.8).

## 13.4 Marine motion: roll, pitch, heave, and the reference point

A survey vessel moves in six degrees of freedom; the sonar needs all six at every ping. **Roll** (rotation about the fore–aft axis), **pitch** (about the athwartships axis), and **yaw/heading** come from the integrated INS; **heave** — the vertical displacement of the vessel — is the one translation that GNSS cannot supply with enough bandwidth and precision alone on a boat that rises and falls a metre in six seconds. A **motion reference unit (MRU)** such as the Applanix POS MV, iXblue Octans/Hydrins/Phins, SBG Ekinox/Apogee/Navsight, or Kongsberg Seatex MRU is a GNSS-aided INS packaged for vessels with a heave output.

**Heave** is obtained by double-integrating the vertical accelerometer and high-pass filtering the result to remove the drift that double integration produces; the filter's corner period (typically 10–20 s, configurable) is a compromise between following long-period swell and rejecting drift. Two consequences follow. First, heave is *relative*: it is a band-limited vertical displacement about a zero that is the vessel's mean vertical position over the filter's window, so it cannot by itself place the sonar in the tidal datum — tides, squat, settlement, and loading changes must come from elsewhere. Second, the high-pass filter has a **settling time** of several filter periods after any change in the vessel's motion statistics; after a turn, after a speed change, or after a quartering sea becomes a head sea, the heave output is biased for one to two minutes, and the bias appears in the data as a vertical offset at the start of each survey line (the standard mitigation is to steady up early and discard the first minute of each line, or to use **delayed heave**, computed in post-processing with a non-causal filter and typically good to a few centimetres). Long-period swell (periods beyond the filter corner) passes partly or wholly into the data as a wavy seafloor — the classic "heave artefact" with the swell's wavelength along track.

**Induced heave** is the vertical motion of a point *away* from the IMU caused by roll and pitch about the IMU: a transducer 3 m below and 2 m to port of the IMU rises and falls by $2\sin\phi$ metres as the vessel rolls through angle $\phi$ — 0.17 m at 5° roll — and this motion must be computed from the attitude and the lever arm and applied *at the transducer*. Applying induced heave at the wrong point, or applying it twice (once in the MRU, once in the acquisition software), or not at all produces roll-correlated depth errors that look like a wobbly seafloor across track. Modern MRUs output heave at a configurable point; the vessel configuration must say which one.

**True heave / GNSS vertical.** With PPK or RTK GNSS the antenna's ellipsoidal height is known at a few centimetres, and the vessel's vertical position can be computed directly — **ellipsoidally referenced surveying (ERS)**, in which the depth is reduced to the ellipsoid through the lever arm from antenna to transducer without tide gauges, and then to chart datum by a separation model ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). ERS eliminates heave-filter settling, tide-zoning errors, and squat/settlement as separate corrections — they are all in the GNSS height — but transfers the whole burden to the GNSS vertical accuracy, the IMU attitude (for the lever arm), and the separation model.

**Squat and settlement.** A moving hull sits lower in the water (**settlement**, the bodily sinkage from the pressure field, 2–20 cm for launches at survey speed) and trims (**squat**, the change in pitch angle, often used loosely for both); in shallow water both increase sharply. Traditional tide-reduced surveys must apply a speed-dependent squat/settlement table measured for the vessel at several speeds (the NOAA FPM describes the procedure using RTK heights or levelling to a shore mark); ERS surveys see it directly in the GNSS height. Draft changes from fuel and water consumption (a few centimetres over a day for a launch, more for a ship) are a related slow term.

**The reference point.** "What is it we are really aligning?" (Hughes Clarke 2003) is the question that resolves most vessel-offset confusion. Define a single **vessel reference point** (RP) — often the IMU's sensing centre, or a marked point near the centre of motion — and measure every lever arm from it: GNSS antenna, transducer(s), MRU if separate, waterline, draft mark. The geometric survey of these offsets should be done by total station or laser tracker to 1 cm or better with the vessel on the hard or still in flat water ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)); the three mounting angles of each sensor relative to the RP are then calibrated dynamically (§13.5 for aircraft; the **patch test** for MBES, [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). Every vendor's software has its own sign convention for lever arms (x forward or starboard; z up or down) and its own choice of whether offsets are entered in the vessel or the sensor frame — writing the vessel configuration file is where a 0.6 m transducer-draft sign error becomes a 1.2 m depth bias.

> **Case file.** A 2.5 m survey launch with a dual-head multibeam, IMU in the cabin 1.8 m above and 0.4 m aft of the transducers, reported a persistent 5–8 cm across-track depth discrepancy between port and starboard halves of the swath that varied with sea state. The induced-heave point in the MRU configuration had been left at the IMU, while the acquisition software also applied lever-arm compensation to the transducers; the roll-induced vertical motion of the heads was therefore applied twice at the transducer position. The error was roll-correlated and vanished in flat water, which is why it survived the patch test (done on a calm day). Detected by comparing overlapping lines run in opposite directions in moderate seas and by inspecting the depth residuals against roll. (Composite of incidents of the type described in Hughes Clarke's vessel-configuration notes and NOAA FPM guidance; specific project details are not from a published report.)

## 13.5 Airborne: boresight, lever arms, timing, and calibration flights

An airborne lidar's point coordinate is (Mathematics)

$$\mathbf{r}^{m}_{P} = \mathbf{r}^{m}_{GNSS}(t) + R^{m}_{b}(t)\,\big[\,R^{b}_{s}\,\mathbf{r}^{s}_{P} - \mathbf{l}^{b}_{GNSS \to IMU} + \mathbf{l}^{b}_{IMU \to s}\,\big],$$

where $\mathbf{r}^{m}_{GNSS}$ is the antenna position in the mapping frame, $R^{m}_{b}$ the body (IMU) attitude from the SBET, $R^{b}_{s}$ the **boresight** rotation from sensor to IMU frame, $\mathbf{r}^{s}_{P}$ the range-and-angle vector to the point in the sensor frame, and $\mathbf{l}$ the lever arms. Each term carries a calibration.

**Lever arms** are measured by survey (total station or laser tracker to a few millimetres, or from the aircraft's engineering drawings plus a check) and entered with the correct sign convention; an error in the antenna-to-IMU lever arm is constant in the body frame but rotates with attitude, so it produces position errors that depend on heading — opposite on reciprocal lines — of the lever-arm error's magnitude. A 5 cm vertical lever-arm error is a 5 cm constant height bias; a 5 cm horizontal error appears as a 10 cm along-track split between reciprocal lines.

**Boresight** — the small rotation between the IMU axes and the lidar scanner axes, typically a few hundredths to a few tenths of a degree, fixed by the mounting but changed by any remount or hard landing — is not measurable mechanically to the required 0.001–0.005° and is calibrated from the data: fly a **calibration pattern** (reciprocal lines over flat terrain to find roll; lines in opposite directions over a pitched roof or slope to find pitch; crossing lines over distinct features at the swath edges for heading; and a flat, well-surveyed surface such as a runway or parking lot for range/scale and absolute height) and solve for the three angles that minimize the misfit between overlapping strips, either manually by inspecting cross-sections or by least-squares **strip adjustment** (Skaloud & Lichti 2006; Glennie 2007; Habib et al. 2010). The signature of each boresight angle in overlapping data is diagnostic: a **roll** error tilts the swath across track, so reciprocal lines show a height difference that grows linearly from zero at nadir to opposite extremes at the edges; a **pitch** error displaces points along track, invisible on flat ground but a height error on slopes facing along track, opposite on reciprocal lines; a **heading** error rotates the swath about nadir, displacing swath-edge points along track in opposite senses on the two sides. Boresight is re-verified every project and re-calibrated after any sensor removal; the USGS LBS requires a calibration report with the delivery.

**Timing.** The lidar's pulse times, the IMU's samples, and the GNSS epochs must be on one time base, usually GPS time distributed by the receiver's PPS pulse and serial time message ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)). The sensitivity is brutal: a timing offset $\delta t$ between attitude and ranging produces an attitude error of $\dot\theta\,\delta t$; at a 5 °/s roll rate (ordinary turbulence), 1 ms is 0.005°, which at 1 500 m is 13 cm. Along-track position error is $v\,\delta t$: 1 ms at 60 m/s is 6 cm. A latency that is *constant* is calibrated like a boresight (it looks like pitch that scales with speed); a latency that *varies* (software timestamps, buffered serial links, an unsynchronized logger clock drifting at tens of ppm) is noise that strip adjustment cannot remove. Verify PPS synchronization at every installation.

**Feedback from strip adjustment.** The overlapping strips of a lidar block are the most sensitive instrument available for trajectory and calibration errors, and modern production uses the overlap residuals both to solve for the calibration and to apply per-strip or time-dependent corrections to the trajectory (typically small rotations and translations per strip or splines in time) — which improves relative accuracy but can absorb genuine systematic errors into a smooth, wrong surface if no ground control constrains it. Relative accuracy between swaths of ≤ 6–8 cm RMS$_z$ (LBS QL1/QL2) is the acceptance test ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

> **Worked example.** Height error budget at the swath edge for an airborne lidar at 1 200 m above ground, 20° half scan angle (slant range at the edge $R = 1200/\cos 20° = 1277$ m), tactical-grade IMU after GNSS aiding. Contributions (1σ), converted to vertical at the swath edge: roll 0.005° → $R\,\delta\phi\,\sin 20° = 1277 \times 8.7\times10^{-5} \times 0.342 = 3.8$ cm; pitch 0.005° → on flat ground, negligible vertical (along-track 11 cm horizontal); heading 0.008° → horizontal only on flat ground; GNSS vertical (PPK, 25 km baseline) 3 cm; vertical lever arm 1 cm; range error (laser) 2 cm; timing 0.2 ms at 3 °/s → 0.0006° → 0.5 cm; atmospheric/refraction < 1 cm. Root-sum-square: $\sqrt{3.8^2 + 3^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 + 0.5^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{29.7} \approx 5.4$ cm at the edge, versus about 3.9 cm at nadir where the roll term vanishes. On a 20° slope, the pitch and heading horizontal errors (11 cm and $1277 \times 1.4\times10^{-4} \times 0.342 = 6$ cm) each add $\tan 20° \approx 0.36$ times their value — 4 cm and 2 cm — raising the edge budget to about 7 cm. This is why checkpoints on flat ground understate the error of a DEM in terrain.

## 13.6 Cars and GNSS outages

A mobile mapping van or a rail scanner operates where GNSS is worst: urban canyons, tree-lined streets, underpasses, tunnels. The INS bridges the gaps, and the quality of the bridge depends on the IMU grade, the length of the outage, and the aiding available. Position error during a pure inertial outage grows as (Mathematics) roughly $\tfrac12 b_a t^2$ from an accelerometer bias $b_a$ and $\tfrac16 g\,b_g t^3$ from a gyro bias $b_g$ tilting the gravity vector, plus random-walk terms; for a tactical-grade unit (1 °/h, 0.5 mg) this is about 2.5 cm after 10 s, 1 m after 60 s, and roughly 10 m after 3 min, while a MEMS unit (10 °/h, 5 mg) is ten times worse. Smoothing halves these. Two aiding measurements make the difference between a usable and an unusable urban trajectory. **Odometer / wheel-speed (DMI)** aiding constrains along-track velocity and, with the non-holonomic constraints that a car does not move sideways or vertically in its body frame, makes the bridged position error grow roughly linearly with distance (typically 0.1–0.5 % of distance travelled for tactical-grade systems with DMI) rather than cubically with time. **Zero-velocity updates (ZUPTs)** — the filter is told the vehicle is stationary whenever it is (at traffic lights, or by deliberate stops every few minutes in tunnels) — reset velocity error and make the gyro and accelerometer biases observable, which is the single most effective technique for pedestrian and vehicle INS and the reason survey procedures for tunnels specify periodic stops. Control points and scan-to-scan registration (effectively SLAM constraints, [Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)) supply the absolute correction that no inertial technique can.

The elevation consequence is that mobile-mapping DTMs of streets are relatively accurate (centimetres along a block) but can carry smooth absolute height errors of decimetres where GNSS was poor, and that the trajectory's own σ underestimates this where the filter was fed multipath-corrupted GNSS positions that it believed. Validation is by control points surveyed by total station from GNSS points in open areas and by comparison to airborne lidar where available ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md), §16.2).

## 13.7 Underwater: INS + DVL + acoustic fixes

An AUV or ROV beneath the surface has no GNSS. Its navigation is **dead reckoning** by INS aided by a **Doppler velocity log (DVL)** — a downward-looking four-beam sonar that measures the vehicle's velocity over the seafloor (bottom lock, within roughly 30–200 m of the bottom depending on frequency) to about 0.1–0.3 % of speed plus a few mm/s — corrected intermittently by acoustic fixes from USBL or LBL ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)). With a navigation-grade INS and DVL, horizontal drift is roughly 0.05–0.2 % of distance travelled (a few metres per 2–4 km) between fixes; tactical-grade systems drift 0.5–1 % or more. The dominant residual errors are heading (a 0.1° heading error puts the vehicle 1.7 m off per kilometre), DVL scale factor and misalignment (calibrated like a boresight by running reciprocal lines and comparing against acoustic fixes), and sound speed in the DVL's range measurement. The *vertical* is far better than the horizontal: a pressure sensor gives depth to about 0.01–0.1 % of range (centimetres to decimetres at 1 000 m), limited by the conversion from pressure to depth through the water density profile ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). The result for bathymetry is a map whose internal shape is excellent and whose absolute horizontal placement is as good as the acoustic positioning and the dead-reckoning chain allow — typically metres in deep water — and the budget must say so ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md), §14.1).

## 13.8 Judging a trajectory

Four sources of evidence tell you whether an SBET is good enough, in increasing order of credibility.

**Filter statistics.** The smoothed standard deviations (position north/east/down, velocity, roll/pitch/heading) over time: for airborne lidar, the LBS-era expectation is position σ under 5–10 cm and roll/pitch σ under 0.005–0.01° throughout the project, with heading under 0.01–0.02°; spikes mark outages or ambiguity resets. These are *formal* values from the filter's own model and are optimistic by a factor of two or more when the model is wrong; they will not show a wrong lever arm, a wrong boresight, or a biased base station at all.

**Internal consistency.** The **forward–backward separation** (difference between the forward and reverse GNSS or GNSS/INS solutions before combination): under 2–3 cm vertical for a healthy flight; sustained offsets of 5–10 cm indicate float ambiguities or a wrong fix in one direction. The **GNSS–INS innovation** (the difference between the INS-predicted and GNSS-measured position at each update): a histogram that is Gaussian with the filter's predicted width is healthy; fat tails or steps mean multipath or false fixes that the filter accepted. Number of satellites and PDOP over time; fix/float status over time; residuals per satellite in tightly coupled processing.

**Overlap statistics in the mapped data.** Swath-to-swath or line-to-line height differences in overlap regions, computed on flat, hard, open surfaces (roads, parking lots, bare fields), summarized as mean (bias) and RMS per overlap pair and plotted along track and across track. For airborne lidar, the USGS LBS QL1/QL2 requires inter-swath RMS$_z$ ≤ 8 cm and maximum differences ≤ 16 cm in non-vegetated areas; the pattern of the differences diagnoses the cause (§13.5). For MBES, crossline comparisons at the 95 % level against the S-44 order are the equivalent ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). A trajectory can pass its own statistics and fail here.

**Independent control.** Checkpoints and control surfaces surveyed by GNSS and total station ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.5; [Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)) compared to the mapped surface: the absolute test, which catches base-station, datum, lever-arm, and timing errors that all the internal checks miss.

> **Try it.** Compute inter-swath height differences on hard surfaces from two overlapping lidar strips with PDAL, then summarize the bias and RMS — the most informative single check of a trajectory.
>
> ```bash
> # 1. Rasterize each strip's ground/road returns (class 2 and 11) to 1 m mean-height grids
> for s in strip_A strip_B; do
>   pdal translate $s.laz $s.tif range \
>     --filters.range.limits="Classification[2:2],Classification[11:11]" \
>     --writers.gdal.resolution=1.0 --writers.gdal.output_type=mean \
>     --writers.gdal.data_type=float32
> done
> # 2. Difference where both exist; mask slopes > 5° to isolate trajectory error from horizontal error
> gdal_calc.py -A strip_A.tif -B strip_B.tif --outfile=dz.tif --calc="A-B" --NoDataValue=-9999
> gdaldem slope strip_A.tif slope.tif
> gdal_calc.py -A dz.tif -B slope.tif --outfile=dz_flat.tif --calc="A*(B<5)+(-9999)*(B>=5)" --NoDataValue=-9999
> # 3. Statistics
> gdalinfo -stats dz_flat.tif | grep STATISTICS
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: mean (bias) within ±2–3 cm and standard deviation under 5–8 cm for a well-calibrated tactical-grade system; a bias that changes sign between reciprocal lines indicates boresight roll or a lever-arm error, a bias that grows toward the swath edge indicates roll, and a bias that differs between flight days indicates GNSS base or troposphere problems. Plot `dz_flat.tif` as a map before trusting the numbers — the spatial pattern is the diagnosis.

## Then & now

- **Platforms to strapdown.** The first INS (V-2, 1942 ⟨H⟩) and the gimballed platforms of submarines and airliners (1950s–1970s) kept a physical stable element; strapdown systems, practical from the late 1970s with ring-laser gyros and fast computers, replaced gimbals with arithmetic. Every mapping IMU today is strapdown.
- **Kalman 1960 ⟨H⟩ to error-state tightly coupled filters.** The Apollo guidance computer ran a Kalman filter in 1969; GNSS/INS integration for mapping arrived with Applanix's POS systems in the mid-1990s (the airborne POS/AV and the marine POS MV), making direct georeferencing of lidar and multibeam possible without ground control for every strip.
- **Heading sources.** Gyrocompasses (Anschütz 1908, Sperry 1911) → GNSS velocity heading → dual-antenna GNSS heading (1990s) as the standard for vessels and now drones.
- **MEMS.** From automotive airbag sensors (1990s) to tactical-grade MEMS IMUs (2010s) that approach FOG performance for roll and pitch at a tenth of the price, enabling drone lidar and handheld SLAM scanners ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)).
- **Processing.** Forward-only filters in real time → RTS smoothing in post-processing (POSPac, Inertial Explorer, Qinertia) → tightly coupled multi-constellation processing with PPP-AR as an alternative to a base station; and, increasingly, trajectory refinement from the mapped data itself (strip adjustment, scan matching), blurring the line with SLAM.
- **Standards.** IEEE Std 647/952/1554 formalized gyro and accelerometer specification and test methods (Allan variance) in the 1990s–2000s; NOAA's Field Procedures Manual and the USGS Lidar Base Specification brought vessel-offset surveys, patch tests, boresight reports, and trajectory deliverables into routine contract requirements in the 2010s.

## Mathematics

**INS error growth.** With a constant accelerometer bias $b_a$ in the horizontal channel, velocity error $\delta v = b_a t$ and position error $\delta p = \tfrac12 b_a t^2$. A gyro bias $b_g$ tilts the computed level by $\delta\theta = b_g t$, which misresolves gravity into a horizontal acceleration error $g\,\delta\theta$, giving $\delta v = \tfrac12 g b_g t^2$ and $\delta p = \tfrac16 g b_g t^3$. Random walks add $\sigma_{\theta} = \mathrm{ARW}\sqrt{t}$ and $\sigma_p \approx \mathrm{VRW}\,t^{3/2}/\sqrt{3}$. For $t$ short compared to the Schuler period (84.4 min) these polynomial forms hold; over longer intervals the errors oscillate with the Schuler period and the $t^3$ term is bounded. Numbers: $b_g = 1\,°/\text{h} = 4.85\times10^{-6}$ rad/s, $g = 9.81$: $\delta p(60\,\text{s}) = \tfrac16 \times 9.81 \times 4.85\times10^{-6} \times 60^3 = 1.7$ m; $b_a = 0.5$ mg $= 4.9\times10^{-3}$ m/s²: $\delta p(60\,\text{s}) = 8.8$ m from the accelerometer term alone *if uncorrected* — in practice the filter has estimated most of the bias before the outage, and residual biases a tenth of these are typical.

**Lever arm.** For a sensor at body-frame offset $\mathbf{l}^b$ from the reference point whose mapping-frame position is $\mathbf{r}^m_{ref}$,

$$\mathbf{r}^m_{sensor} = \mathbf{r}^m_{ref} + R^m_b\,\mathbf{l}^b, \qquad \mathbf{v}^m_{sensor} = \mathbf{v}^m_{ref} + R^m_b\,(\boldsymbol\omega^b_{ib} \times \mathbf{l}^b),$$

the second equation giving the induced velocity (and, integrated, induced heave) at an offset point. A lever-arm error $\delta\mathbf{l}^b$ produces a position error $R^m_b\,\delta\mathbf{l}^b$ that rotates with heading: on reciprocal headings the horizontal components reverse sign, which is how it is detected.

**Boresight as a small rotation.** For small angles $(\delta\phi, \delta\theta, \delta\psi)$, $R^b_s \approx I + [\boldsymbol\delta\times]$ with $[\boldsymbol\delta\times]$ the skew-symmetric matrix; the point error is $\delta\mathbf{r} = R^m_b\,[\boldsymbol\delta\times]\,\mathbf{r}^s_P \approx R^m_b\,(\boldsymbol\delta \times \mathbf{r}^s_P)$, i.e., perpendicular to the beam and proportional to range. For a nadir-pointing beam of length $R$ at scan angle $\phi$ in the across-track plane, roll error $\delta\phi$ gives vertical error $R\,\delta\phi\,\sin\phi$ and horizontal error $R\,\delta\phi\,\cos\phi$.

**Kalman filter (error-state).** State $\delta\mathbf{x}$ (position, velocity, attitude errors; biases), transition $F$, process noise $Q$, measurement $\mathbf{z} = H\delta\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}$ with covariance $R$:

Predict: $\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k = \Phi_{k-1}\,\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}$, $\quad P^-_k = \Phi_{k-1} P_{k-1} \Phi^\mathsf{T}_{k-1} + Q_{k-1}$.

Update: $K_k = P^-_k H^\mathsf{T}_k (H_k P^-_k H^\mathsf{T}_k + R_k)^{-1}$, $\quad \delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k + K_k(\mathbf{z}_k - H_k\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k)$, $\quad P_k = (I - K_k H_k)P^-_k$.

The innovation $\mathbf{z}_k - H_k\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k$ should be zero-mean with covariance $H P^- H^\mathsf{T} + R$; its normalized squared value (NIS) is a χ² test used to reject outliers (multipath, false fixes). The RTS smoother runs $\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N} = \delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k + C_k(\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} - \delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_{k+1})$ with $C_k = P_k\Phi_k^\mathsf{T}(P^-_{k+1})^{-1}$ backward from $k = N$.

**Allan deviation.** For a rate series averaged over clusters of duration $\tau$ giving means $\bar\omega_i(\tau)$, $\sigma^2_A(\tau) = \tfrac12\langle(\bar\omega_{i+1} - \bar\omega_i)^2\rangle$. On a log–log plot of $\sigma_A$ against $\tau$: slope −½ is white noise, with ARW $= \sigma_A(\tau = 1\,\text{s})$ (convert units: °/s·√s → °/√h by multiplying by 60); slope 0 is bias instability, read as $\sigma_A/0.664$ at the minimum; slope +½ is rate random walk, read at $\tau = 3$ s. Several hours of static data are needed to reach the bias-instability floor of a tactical-grade unit.

**Timing sensitivity.** Attitude error from a latency $\delta t$ at angular rate $\dot\theta$: $\delta\theta = \dot\theta\,\delta t$; position error at range $R$: $R\dot\theta\,\delta t$. Along-track position error: $v\,\delta t$. Both are proportional to the dynamics, so they appear in turbulence and turns and vanish in smooth straight flight — a trajectory "artefact that comes and goes with the weather" is a timing problem until proven otherwise.

## Validation & uncertainty

Errors from the trajectory and its calibration dominate the error budget of nearly every moving-platform elevation product, and they have recognizable signatures.

**How they arise.** (1) Attitude error from the IMU and filter — scales with range, strongest at swath edges and in heading during straight lines; (2) position error from GNSS — see [Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), constant or slowly varying, with steps at ambiguity changes; (3) lever-arm error — constant in the body frame, heading-dependent in the map frame; (4) boresight error — range-proportional, with the roll/pitch/heading signatures of §13.5; (5) timing error — dynamics-proportional; (6) heave-filter and induced-heave error (marine) — correlated with vessel motion and with time since the last manoeuvre; (7) squat/settlement/draft — speed- and load-dependent constant offsets.

**How they propagate.** Through the georeferencing equation: position errors add directly to the point; attitude and boresight errors multiply the range; lever-arm errors rotate with attitude. Their effect on a *DEM* is then filtered by gridding ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)) — random point noise averages down, but systematic strip-wise errors survive as steps and tilts between strips, which are the dominant visible artefact in shaded-relief views of poorly adjusted lidar ([Chapter 57](ch57-visualizing-dems.md)).

**How to test.** In order: (a) the sensor-level tests — Allan variance on the IMU before purchase or after a shock; lever-arm survey with an independent check measurement; PPS verification with an oscilloscope or the vendor's timing diagnostic; (b) the calibration flight or patch test, repeated per project and after remounts, with the solved angles compared to previous values (a change of more than a few thousandths of a degree without a remount is a warning); (c) the filter statistics and forward–backward separation of every mission; (d) the overlap analysis of the mapped data on flat hard surfaces, separated into bias and spread, plotted spatially; (e) the comparison to independent control, separating flat and sloped checkpoints so that horizontal errors show up as vertical error on slopes. Any step that is skipped leaves a class of error undetected: the filter statistics cannot see lever arms, the overlap analysis cannot see a whole-project base-station offset, and flat checkpoints cannot see heading or timing.

**Heave-specific checks.** Compare real-time heave with delayed/post-processed heave and with GNSS vertical (where PPK is available) over a line that includes a turn; the difference after the turn shows the settling error. Inspect the seafloor along track for periodicities at swell periods × vessel speed.

**What to report.** IMU model and grade; GNSS processing mode and base details; lever arms with their survey method and estimated uncertainty; boresight/patch-test values and residuals, with date; filter σ summaries (median and 95th percentile for position and attitude); forward–backward separation statistics; overlap statistics (bias and RMS per overlap, with the spatial plot); heave filter settings and settling policy (marine); and the resulting estimated per-point uncertainty, which for lidar can be propagated analytically (Glennie 2007) and for MBES is the TPU model of CUBE/S-44 ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical error contributions (1σ) at the swath edge for three moving platforms, order-of-magnitude values from the sources cited; the trajectory terms are in bold.
>
> | Component | Airborne lidar, 1 200 m AGL, 20° | MBES launch, 30 m depth, 60° beam | Mobile mapping van, 30 m range, street |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **GNSS vertical (PPK)** | 3 cm | 3 cm (ERS) or tide 5–10 cm | 3 cm open / 10–30 cm bridged |
> | **Roll/pitch (aided IMU)** | 0.005° → 4 cm | 0.02° → 0.9 cm (× tan 60° on depth: 1.8 cm) | 0.01° → 0.3 cm |
> | **Heading (on slopes)** | 0.01° → 1–2 cm on 20° slope | 0.05° → < 1 cm | 0.05° → < 1 cm |
> | **Lever arm / boresight residual** | 1–2 cm | 1–2 cm | 1 cm |
> | **Timing (0.5 ms)** | 1 cm | < 1 cm | 1–2 cm |
> | **Heave / induced heave** | — | 2–5 cm (settled) | — |
> | Range, refraction, sound speed | 2–3 cm | 0.2–0.5 % depth → 6–15 cm at 60° | 1 cm |
> | Total (RSS) | ≈ 6 cm | ≈ 8–18 cm | ≈ 4 cm / 10–30 cm |

> The lesson is in the columns: for airborne lidar the trajectory *is* the budget; for MBES the outer-beam sound-speed term competes with it; for mobile mapping the GNSS-outage term dwarfs everything else.

## Software

**Open source:** **ROS 2 `robot_localization`** — EKF/UKF fusion of IMU, odometry, and GNSS for robots and vehicles; a good teaching tool, not a survey post-processor. **OpenVINS** — visual-inertial estimator with a rigorous error-state filter and IMU-intrinsics calibration. **Kalibr** — camera–IMU spatial and temporal calibration (lever arm, boresight, and latency between a camera and an IMU from a calibration sequence). **GTSAM** — factor-graph optimization with IMU preintegration (Forster et al. 2017), the foundation of many lidar-inertial systems ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)). **RTKLIB** — GNSS only, but the input to loosely coupled experiments. **allantools** (Python) — Allan deviation computation for IMU characterization. **Closed:** **Applanix POSPac MMS** — the industry standard for airborne and marine GNSS/INS post-processing (SBET, SMRMSG, IN-Fusion tightly coupled, PP-RTX PPP); caveat: its quality plots are formal statistics. **NovAtel Inertial Explorer** — tightly coupled GNSS/INS post-processing for any IMU, widely used with mobile mapping and drone systems. **SBG Qinertia** — post-processing for SBG and third-party IMUs with PPK/PPP. **iXblue (Exail) Delph INS** — post-processing for Phins/Hydrins marine units. **Kongsberg Seatex / NavLab** and **Sonardyne SPRINT** post-processing suites for underwater INS+DVL. Lidar strip-adjustment and calibration tools (TerraSolid TerraMatch, Riegl RiPRECISION, BayesMap StripAlign, open-source `OPALS` with academic license) close the loop from mapped data back to trajectory and boresight.

## Pitfalls

- **Vendor-specific lever-arm sign conventions** → one package wants x-forward/y-right/z-down, another y-forward/z-up, and some ask for the offset *from* the sensor *to* the IMU → write every lever arm in a diagram with the convention stated, enter it, and verify by a reciprocal-line test before production.
- **Heave filter settling after turns** → the high-pass filter needs one to two minutes to re-converge after a change in motion statistics; the first part of each line carries a vertical bias → steady up early, discard or flag line starts, use delayed heave or ERS, and check line-start depths against overlap.
- **Magnetometer heading near steel** → hull, car body, drone motors, and rebar bias magnetic heading by degrees → never use magnetometer heading for survey attitude; use dual-antenna GNSS, gyrocompassing, or dynamic alignment, and verify heading from overlap residuals.
- **Induced heave applied at the wrong point (or twice)** → MRU and acquisition software both offer lever-arm compensation → decide once where heave is referenced, document it in the vessel configuration, and test in moderate seas, not only on the calm patch-test day.
- **Trajectory and base station in different frames or epochs** → base in ITRF at observation epoch, project in NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00 → the SBET is offset by the frame difference (up to 1–2 m horizontal, centimetres vertical) → specify frame and epoch in the GNSS processing and in the deliverable metadata.
- **Boresight not re-verified after a remount or hard landing** → angles shift by hundredths of a degree → compare each project's calibration solution with the previous; investigate changes.
- **Timing verified once, assumed forever** → a PPS cable, a firmware update, or a logging-software change introduces latency → run the timing diagnostic at every installation and look for dynamics-correlated artefacts in overlap.
- **Trusting the SBET σ as the accuracy** → formal covariances assume correct models and no systematic errors → use overlap statistics and independent control for the reported accuracy.
- **Long straight lines without manoeuvres** → heading observability decays; heading error grows → insert turns or S-manoeuvres at intervals appropriate to the IMU grade (every 15–30 min for tactical FOG, more often for MEMS); dual-antenna heading removes the issue.
- **Checkpoints only on flat ground** → heading, pitch, and timing errors produce horizontal displacements that have no vertical signature on flat ground → include sloped and roof-edge checks, or horizontal checks on painted lines and building corners.
- **Squat/settlement table from a different load or speed** → settlement depends on speed squared and on draft → re-determine after significant changes; ERS makes it moot.
- **Assuming a MEMS IMU in a drone performs like its datasheet** → bias repeatability turn-on to turn-on and temperature sensitivity exceed in-run specs; vibration aliases into attitude → allow warm-up, isolate from vibration, and check calibration with every flight's overlap.

## Key takeaways

- Attitude error scales with range: 0.01° is 1.7 cm at 100 m and 17 cm at 1 km, so the IMU and its calibration, not the ranging sensor, usually set the accuracy of DEMs from moving platforms.
- The GNSS/INS trajectory is an error-state Kalman filter plus a smoother; its formal statistics are necessary checks and optimistic estimates — the overlap of the mapped data and independent control are the real tests.
- Heading is the weak attitude axis; it needs dual-antenna GNSS, gyrocompassing, or manoeuvres, never a magnetometer near metal.
- Lever arms, boresight, and timing are three calibrations with distinct signatures (heading-dependent, range-proportional, dynamics-proportional); learn to read them in reciprocal-line residuals.
- Heave is a band-limited relative measurement with a settling time; induced heave must be applied at the transducer exactly once; ellipsoidally referenced surveying replaces heave, tide, squat, and settlement with the GNSS vertical and its budget.
- Inertial error grows as $t^2$ (accelerometer bias) and $t^3$ (gyro bias) without aiding; odometer, ZUPT, and DVL aiding turn it into a slow function of distance travelled, and smoothing halves it.
- Under water, dead reckoning by INS + DVL gives excellent relative bathymetry and metre-level absolute horizontal placement; budget it explicitly.
- Deliver and archive the trajectory, its statistics, the calibration report, and the vessel or aircraft configuration with every dataset; a DEM without its trajectory lineage cannot be re-validated.
