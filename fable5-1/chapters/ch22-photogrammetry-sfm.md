# Chapter 22 — Photogrammetry, stereo, and structure from motion

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** This chapter covers the oldest remote elevation method — measuring parallax between overlapping images — from film cameras and aerial triangulation through drone structure from motion and satellite tri-stereo to neural radiance fields, and it pairs with [Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md) (radar) and [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md) (lidar) as the three ways to make a DSM from above.

**In this chapter.** Image-based 3D is cheap, scalable, and dangerously easy: modern software will produce a dense, beautiful surface from almost any set of overlapping photographs whether or not the geometry supports it. You will be able to write the collinearity equations and explain what interior and exterior orientation are; derive the height precision of a stereo pair from base-to-height ratio and matching precision; follow the structure-from-motion pipeline from feature detection through RANSAC, incremental reconstruction, and bundle adjustment to dense matching; explain why self-calibrated, nadir-only drone blocks dome and how oblique images and well-placed ground control cure it; design a ground-control and checkpoint scheme whose checkpoints are genuinely independent; read a satellite RPC model and understand bias compensation; recognise the failure modes of image DSMs — water, snow, shadow, repetitive texture, thin and moving objects — and state precisely what surface an image DSM represents; and place shape-from-shading, NeRF, and 3D Gaussian splatting in their proper roles as reconstruction and visualisation tools rather than metrology. The Validation section gives a GCP/checkpoint protocol and a worked error budget for a drone survey.

## 22.1 Camera model and calibration

A frame camera is modelled, to first order, as a **pinhole**: every object point $\mathbf{X}$, its image $\mathbf{x}$, and the **perspective centre** $\mathbf{O}$ lie on one straight line. Writing the object point in camera coordinates as $(X_c, Y_c, Z_c)$, the image coordinates are $x = f X_c / Z_c$, $y = f Y_c / Z_c$, where $f$ is the **principal distance** (loosely, focal length) and the image origin is the **principal point** $(x_0, y_0)$. The parameters that describe the camera itself — $f$, $(x_0, y_0)$, and the lens distortion — constitute the **interior orientation (IO)**; the position of the perspective centre $(X_O, Y_O, Z_O)$ and the three rotation angles $(\omega, \varphi, \kappa)$ that orient the camera in the object frame constitute the **exterior orientation (EO)**. Combining them gives the **collinearity equations** (Mathematics section), the single most important formula in this chapter: given IO and EO, every object point maps to a unique image point, and given two or more images, every matched image point determines an object point by intersection.

Real lenses deviate from the pinhole. The **Brown–Conrady model** (Brown 1971) expresses the deviation as radial distortion $\Delta r = k_1 r^3 + k_2 r^5 + k_3 r^7$ and decentring (tangential) distortion with parameters $p_1, p_2$, sometimes with affinity and shear terms $b_1, b_2$. Radial distortion on a consumer wide-angle lens reaches tens of pixels at the image corners; left unmodelled it bends straight lines and, through the bundle adjustment, bends the reconstructed terrain (Section 22.3). The parameters are estimated either in a laboratory or test-field **calibration** (a checkerboard or coded-target field imaged from many angles; Zhang 2000 for the planar-target method now built into OpenCV) or by **self-calibration** inside the bundle adjustment of the mapping block itself. Metric aerial cameras are calibrated by the manufacturer and periodically re-certified (the USGS Optical Science Laboratory performed this for film cameras; digital cameras are now certified under the USGS Quality Assurance Plan for Digital Aerial Imagery); drone cameras almost never are, and their IO is self-calibrated on every project, which is where the doming problem begins.

Two further effects matter for modern sensors. **Rolling shutter** CMOS sensors expose rows sequentially over several milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, so a moving camera records each row from a slightly different pose; at 15 m s$^{-1}$ and a 30 ms readout the first and last rows are 45 cm apart in object space, and a global-shutter assumption produces systematic skew in the block. Pipelines model it explicitly (Agisoft, Pix4D, COLMAP's rolling-shutter extension) or users choose global-shutter cameras. **Pushbroom** sensors — the satellite and many aerial line scanners — have a different model entirely: one perspective centre per scan line, with the platform trajectory providing the second image dimension, which is why satellite imagery is distributed with rational polynomial coefficients rather than a single EO (Section 22.6).

## 22.2 Classical aerial photogrammetry

Photogrammetry predates aircraft: the gis-history timeline dates its beginnings to Johann Heinrich Lambert's perspective geometry (1776 ⟨H⟩), Aimé Laussedat applied the method to terrestrial surveying from 1849, and the Prussian architect Albrecht Meydenbauer coined the word in 1867. Aerial photogrammetry became an industry between the World Wars, and the American Society of Photogrammetry was founded in 1934 ⟨H⟩ (now ASPRS). The classical workflow is still the conceptual backbone of every modern pipeline.

A **stereo pair** is two overlapping photographs taken from stations separated by the **air base** $B$ at flying height $H$ above ground. The same ground point appears displaced between the two images by the **x-parallax** $p$, and for vertical photographs its height above the datum follows from $Z = H - fB/p$ (normal case). Differentiating gives the fundamental precision relation $\sigma_Z = (H^2/(fB))\,\sigma_p = (H/B)(H/f)\,\sigma_p$, so height precision improves with the **base-to-height ratio** $B/H$ and with the measuring precision of parallax $\sigma_p$, and degrades with the square of the flying height for a fixed base. Standard aerial blocks use 60 % forward overlap (giving $B/H \approx 0.6$ for a wide-angle camera) and 20–30 % sidelap; modern digital acquisitions use 80/60 or more because disk space is cheap and redundancy improves matching. The **ground sample distance (GSD)** is $H\,p_{\mathrm{pix}}/f$ for pixel pitch $p_{\mathrm{pix}}$ — 2 cm for a 24 mm lens with 4 µm pixels at 120 m — and the rule of thumb that height precision is roughly 1–3 GSD for well-textured surfaces follows from $\sigma_p \approx 0.3$–1 pixel and $B/H \approx 0.3$–0.6.

**Aerial triangulation (AT)** extends the pair to the block: tie points measured in every overlapping image, plus a sparse set of **ground control points (GCPs)** with surveyed coordinates, are adjusted simultaneously to recover the EO of every image (and, with self-calibration, the IO). Before computers this was done on analogue stereoplotters with mechanical or optical projection; from the 1960s analytical plotters measured image coordinates and solved the adjustment numerically; from the 1990s softcopy workstations did everything digitally. The product of AT is the oriented block; the DEM is then measured by an operator floating a mark on the stereo model (still the gold standard for breaklines and DTMs under canopy, where the operator can see the ground between trees) or by **automatic image matching** (Section 22.4), which produces a DSM of the first visible surface.

> **Definitions that bite.** *Accuracy of an orthophoto* and *accuracy of the DEM used to make it* are routinely confused. An orthophoto's horizontal error at a point is $\delta h \tan\theta$ where $\delta h$ is the DEM error there and $\theta$ the look angle from the image used; at the edge of a wide-angle frame ($\theta \approx 35°$) a 2 m DEM error displaces a building top by 1.4 m. A "true orthophoto" uses a DSM so that roofs are displaced correctly; a conventional one uses a DTM so that roofs lean. Which was used should be stated and usually is not.

## 22.3 Structure from motion

**Structure from motion (SfM)** is photogrammetry that assumes nothing about camera positions or calibration in advance and recovers them, together with a sparse 3D point cloud, from image correspondences alone. The algorithmic ingredients were assembled in computer vision between the 1980s and 2000s: robust estimation with **RANSAC** (Fischler & Bolles 1981 ⟨H⟩); the **factorization** method of Tomasi & Kanade (1992 ⟨H⟩), which recovered shape and motion from an orthographic image stream by singular-value decomposition and showed that the problem was tractable; the **fundamental and essential matrices** that encode the epipolar geometry of a pair (Hartley & Zisserman 2004); scale-invariant feature detection and description with **SIFT** (Lowe 2004), which made automatic correspondence between widely separated views reliable; and efficient sparse **bundle adjustment** (Triggs et al. 2000). Bundler (Snavely, Seitz & Szeliski 2006) assembled these into "Photo Tourism"; VisualSFM, OpenMVG, and COLMAP (Schönberger & Frahm 2016) made the pipeline robust and fast; and from about 2012 geoscientists adopted it wholesale (Westoby et al. 2012; Fonstad et al. 2013) because it turned any camera into a topographic instrument.

The pipeline is: (1) detect and describe features in every image; (2) match descriptors between candidate image pairs and reject outliers by estimating the fundamental (uncalibrated) or essential (calibrated) matrix with RANSAC; (3) **incremental SfM** — initialise from a well-conditioned pair, triangulate points, register the next image by solving the perspective-n-point problem against existing 3D points, triangulate new points, run bundle adjustment, repeat — or **global SfM**, which estimates all rotations from pairwise relative rotations, then all translations, then triangulates and adjusts once, trading robustness for speed and freedom from drift; (4) **bundle adjustment** of all camera parameters and all 3D points by minimising the sum of squared **reprojection errors** (Mathematics section); (5) scale and georeference the result with GCPs or camera positions, because image correspondences alone determine the scene only up to a similarity transform (seven degrees of freedom); (6) dense matching (Section 22.4).

### 22.3.1 Self-calibration and the doming problem

Because drone cameras are uncalibrated, step 4 estimates $f$, $(x_0, y_0)$, and the distortion coefficients along with everything else. In a block of near-parallel, nadir-looking images at constant height, radial distortion and the shape of the reconstructed surface are nearly indistinguishable: a small error in $k_1$ is compensated almost exactly by a smooth bowl- or dome-shaped deformation of the terrain plus small changes in camera heights (James & Robson 2014). The result is a **doming** (or dishing) error that grows quadratically with distance from the block centre, is invisible in reprojection residuals, and can reach decimetres to metres across a few hundred metres — far exceeding the random error. The cures are geometric: add **convergent/oblique images** (10–20° off-nadir, or a cross-grid flight at a different height) so that distortion and surface shape produce different image effects; **fix the IO** from a pre-calibration; distribute **GCPs** across the block *and* around its edges so that the dome is observable and constrained; or use **precise camera positions** from RTK/PPK GNSS as weighted observations in the adjustment, which constrains the camera heights and thereby the dome (Section 22.5). James et al. (2017) and Sanz-Ablanedo et al. (2018) quantify how error falls with the number and distribution of GCPs; the summary is that five GCPs in a cross pattern on a 200 m block are not enough to detect a dome, and twenty well distributed are.

> **Rule of thumb.** For a self-calibrated SfM block: nadir-only imagery at one height with GCPs only in the interior will dome; add either ≥ 10–15° oblique images, or RTK/PPK camera positions, or GCPs at the edges and corners, and preferably two of the three. Validate against *independent* checkpoints at the edges, where the dome is largest. The rule fails to protect you if the GCPs themselves were surveyed with a biased method (Section 22.5).

## 22.4 Dense matching

Once the cameras are oriented, **dense matching** finds a correspondence for (nearly) every pixel. Classical area-based matching (normalised cross-correlation on windows) is fast but blurs edges and fails in low texture; **semi-global matching (SGM)** (Hirschmüller 2008) rectifies image pairs to epipolar geometry, computes a per-pixel matching cost over a disparity range (census or mutual information, which are robust to radiometric differences), and aggregates the cost along 8 or 16 one-dimensional paths with penalties for small ($P_1$) and large ($P_2$) disparity changes between neighbours, approximating a global smoothness prior at a fraction of the computational cost. SGM became the workhorse of aerial and satellite DSM production from about 2010 (it underlies SURE, the DLR and Vexcel pipelines, and most commercial packages) because it preserves building edges while suppressing noise in homogeneous areas. **PatchMatch** stereo (Bleyer, Rhemann & Rother 2011) estimates a slanted plane per pixel with randomised propagation and handles oblique surfaces better; **multi-view stereo (MVS)** methods (Furukawa & Ponce 2010; COLMAP's PatchMatch MVS; OpenMVS) fuse depth from many views with photometric and geometric consistency checks, which is what drone pipelines do. **Deep stereo** networks (PSMNet, RAFT-Stereo, and successors trained on synthetic and lidar-labelled data) now outperform SGM on benchmarks and are entering production pipelines; their failure modes are different (hallucinated plausible surfaces where there is no texture, domain shift between training and application scenes) and their uncertainty is harder to characterise.

The output can be a **depth map per image**, fused into a **point cloud**; a **mesh** (Poisson or Delaunay reconstruction over the point cloud, textured from the images); or a **DSM raster** made by gridding the point cloud or by rasterising the mesh. Each step filters: outlier removal and fusion suppress isolated points, meshing smooths and closes holes (inventing surfaces across gaps), and gridding resamples to a cell size that is usually 1–3 GSD and that sets the effective resolution ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)). The filtering parameters should be reported with the product because they determine whether thin objects, overhangs, and small depressions survive. Whatever the representation, the surface is the **first visible surface** seen from the camera stations: canopy top, roof, car roof, and the water surface if it happened to match.

## 22.5 Georeferencing: control, checkpoints, and direct georeferencing

A bundle adjustment with GCPs constrains the block in three ways: it removes the seven-parameter similarity ambiguity (scale, position, orientation), it suppresses deformations such as doming, and it ties the result to the datum of the GCP coordinates. The three jobs need different numbers and distributions. Three well-separated points fix the similarity; constraining deformation needs points throughout the block and especially on its perimeter and at its height extremes; and the datum tie is only as good as the GCP survey — RTK with a network solution typically gives 1–2 cm horizontal and 2–4 cm vertical, static GNSS better, a handheld receiver nothing useful. The GCP coordinates carry the datum of the product ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)): if they were surveyed as ellipsoidal heights and reported as orthometric with a geoid model, that model's error (centimetres to a decimetre depending on region) is in every elevation.

**Checkpoints** are surveyed points *withheld* from the adjustment and used only to assess the result. They must be independent in three senses: not used as GCPs (the most common violation), not used to choose among processing runs (a softer violation that still biases the reported accuracy), and surveyed by a method whose errors are independent of the GCP survey's (a second RTK session from the same base with the same antenna model shares the base-coordinate and antenna-calibration errors). The ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards (Edition 2, 2023; v2 2024) specify the minimum number of checkpoints by project area, their distribution across land-cover classes, and the statistics to report — RMSE$_V$ for both NVA and VVA, Edition 2 having dropped the 1.96 × RMSE and 95th-percentile conventions of Edition 1 (2014) that many specifications still quote; [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) covers the reporting and [Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md) the targets.

**Direct georeferencing** uses GNSS/IMU observations of each exposure's position and attitude, with lever arms and boresight angles calibrated ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), [Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)), to supply EO directly. On large aerial platforms this has been routine since the late 1990s and reduces GCPs to a handful for quality control. On drones, **RTK/PPK** positioning of the camera (antenna phase centre to camera lever arm of a few centimetres; exposure time-tagged to the GNSS epoch to better than a millisecond, since 1 ms at 15 m s$^{-1}$ is 1.5 cm) gives camera positions at 2–5 cm, which, entered as weighted observations in the bundle adjustment, constrain scale and height and suppress doming even with few GCPs. Attitude from a consumer drone IMU is not accurate enough to use directly and is estimated by the adjustment. Direct georeferencing without any ground checkpoints is a common and indefensible practice: the GNSS solution can be wrong (a bad fix, a wrong antenna height, a datum confusion) by decimetres with no internal symptom. A **precision map** — a raster of the expected error per pixel propagated from the adjustment's covariance and the matching precision (James et al. 2017) — is the honest companion of any SfM DSM and should accompany the checkpoint statistics, not replace them.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Drone SfM DSM of a 20 ha gravel quarry, GSD 2.5 cm, 80/70 overlap, 10 % oblique images, PPK camera positions, 12 GCPs, 15 independent checkpoints. Values are the kind of numbers a well-run project reports; they are not generic.

> | Component | Magnitude (1σ) | How it enters |

## 22.6 Satellite stereo and tri-stereo

Optical satellites make DEMs by the same parallax principle, with two differences: the sensor is a pushbroom line scanner, and the images are distributed with a **rational polynomial coefficient (RPC)** model rather than a physical camera model. The RPC model expresses image line and sample as ratios of cubic polynomials in normalised latitude, longitude, and height — 80 coefficients per image — fitted by the vendor to the rigorous sensor model (Grodecki & Dial 2003). The RPCs reproduce the rigorous model to a fraction of a pixel but inherit its absolute error, which comes from the satellite's orbit and attitude knowledge: a few metres CE90 for modern WorldView and Pléiades without control, tens of metres for older sensors. Because that error is almost constant across an image, it is removed by **bias compensation** — estimating a shift (and sometimes a drift) in image space per image from a few GCPs or from tie points between images — before stereo intersection. Uncompensated RPC bias appears in the DEM as a horizontal shift and a vertical offset of similar magnitude; this is the "metres of bias" in the Pitfalls.

**Stereo acquisition geometry.** Early systems used **cross-track** stereo from different orbits days apart (SPOT 1–4, 1986 onward), with changing illumination and surface between images. **Along-track** stereo acquires both images within a minute or two on one pass, either with fixed fore/aft telescopes (SPOT 5 HRS, ALOS PRISM's nadir/forward/backward triplet, Cartosat-1's +26°/−5° pair, ASTER's nadir and 27.6° backward band 3N/3B) or by slewing an agile satellite (WorldView-1/2/3, Pléiades, GeoEye-1, SkySat), which also permits **tri-stereo**: a third, near-nadir image that reduces occlusion in cities and steep terrain and improves matching reliability. Convergence angles of 30–45° (B/H ≈ 0.5–0.9) are typical for terrain; urban work uses 15–25° to limit occlusion.

**Pipelines and products.** The **NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP)** ⟨H⟩ began in 1996 for planetary work and became a general open-source stereo system supporting RPC and rigorous models (Beyer, Alexandrov & McMichael 2018; Shean et al. 2016 for the Earth-observation workflow). **SETSM** (Noh & Howat 2015) was written for fully automatic, massively parallel processing of WorldView stereo over ice and is the engine behind **ArcticDEM** (2 m posting, north of 60° N; Porter et al. 2018), **REMA** (Antarctica; Howat et al. 2019), and **EarthDEM**, the Polar Geospatial Center products that together cover a large fraction of the land surface with time-stamped strips. **s2p** (de Franchis et al. 2014) and CNES's **CARS** target Pléiades and the forthcoming CO3D constellation; **MicMac** (IGN; Rupnik, Daakir & Pierrot-Deseilligny 2017) handles everything from close-range to satellite with rigorous photogrammetric control. The global products most readers meet are **ASTER GDEM** (v3, 2019; 1″ posting from the full ASTER archive 2000–2013; roughly 10–20 m RMSE and prone to residual cloud artefacts and mole-run noise) and **ALOS World 3D (AW3D30)** (1″ public version of the 5 m commercial product from PRISM; roughly 5 m RMSE). Both are DSMs; both carry the ambiguities of multi-year compositing ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)); and the per-strip PGC products, by preserving acquisition dates, make possible the elevation-change time series of [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md).

## 22.7 Historical imagery

Photogrammetry is the only method that can produce DEMs of the past. **Archival aerial photographs** exist for most developed countries from the 1930s onward; **Corona** reconnaissance imagery (1960–1972, declassified 1995 ⟨H⟩) and **Hexagon KH-9** imagery (1971–1986; the mapping camera frames declassified 2002, the panoramic imagery 2011) provide stereo coverage of much of the world at 2–9 m ground resolution. "Structure from history" reprocesses these with SfM/MVS tools to build DEMs of glaciers, coastlines, river channels, and cities before modern change began, and differences them against current lidar or satellite DEMs (Dehecq et al. 2020 automated KH-9 processing for global glacier change since the 1970s; Maurer et al. 2019 used it across the Himalaya). The difficulties are systematic: scanned film has no interior orientation beyond whatever fiducial marks survived, so distortion from film shrinkage, scanner geometry, and (for panoramic cameras) the sweeping optics must be modelled; the images are often of unknown or imprecise camera calibration; ground control must be transferred from modern data via stable features (road intersections, rock outcrops) with their own identification error; and the datum of the modern control must be propagated carefully. Reported accuracies after careful co-registration on stable terrain are 5–15 m for Corona/KH-9 and sub-metre to a few metres for good archival aerial photographs; the uncertainty is dominated by the co-registration and by residual film and lens distortion, which appears as long-wavelength warps. Nuth & Kääb (2011) co-registration on stable terrain ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md), [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) is mandatory before any differencing.

## 22.8 Failure modes: what an image DSM is

An image DSM is a map of where matching succeeded on the first visible surface. Everything else is interpolation or error, and the failures are predictable from the physics of matching.

**Water** has no stable texture: waves move between exposures, and calm water reflects sky. Matching either fails (holes) or latches onto specular glint, floating debris, or the bottom in clear shallow water, producing a surface that is noise with the statistical character of terrain. Water should be masked and flattened by rule ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)), never accepted from matching. **Snow** and bright sand saturate the sensor and lack texture; matching fails or produces low-frequency "lumps" of several metres, which is why ArcticDEM strips have gaps and artefacts over the ice-sheet interiors and why satellite stereo over snow is checked against ICESat-2. **Shadow** is dark and low-contrast; deep shadow in urban canyons and north-facing slopes at high latitude yields noisy or absent heights; the problem worsens with sensor convergence because the two images see different shadow extents. **Repetitive texture** — façades, crop rows, solar farms, tiled roofs — produces ambiguous matches at integer multiples of the pattern period and therefore gross height blunders; SGM's smoothness penalty suppresses some, and tri-stereo helps. **Moving objects** (vehicles, people, cranes, waves — [Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)) violate the static-scene assumption and are reconstructed as noise, ghosts, or not at all. **Thin objects** — wires, poles, railings, bare branches — are narrower than the matching window and vanish; an image DSM never contains power lines, which lidar does ([Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)). **Occlusion**: building walls, overhangs, and the far side of ridges are seen by only one camera; the DSM there is interpolated and, in dense cities, systematically wrong, which is why building footprints in satellite DSMs are enlarged by a "skirt". **Vegetation** is matched at the canopy top (or inside it, inconsistently, for sparse canopies), so an image DSM minus a lidar DTM is a canopy-height model of uncertain quality, and an image DSM filtered to a "DTM" under forest is an interpolation across the gaps where ground was visible — often none. [Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md) and [Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md) treat the consequences.

> **Case file.** A 2017 drone survey of a reservoir shoreline for a sediment budget reported a DSM with 4 cm checkpoint RMSE, all checkpoints on the beach. Differencing against the previous year's lidar showed 0.5–1.5 m of apparent "deposition" across the water surface, which had been matched on glint and ripples and smoothed by the mesh. The volume change over the reservoir area dwarfed the real beach change, and the error was only caught because the analyst plotted the difference map rather than the summary statistics. The corrected workflow masked water by NDWI from the orthomosaic before gridding and reported the beach-only volume. (Composite of several reported incidents; see Eltner et al. 2016 for a catalogue of similar failures.)

## 22.9 Shape from shading and photoclinometry

A single image also contains height information, in the form of brightness variation caused by slope under known illumination. **Shape from shading** (Horn 1970) inverts a reflectance model — Lambertian, or a planetary model such as Hapke or Minnaert — to recover surface slopes from pixel intensities, then integrates slopes to heights. The problem is ill-posed: albedo variations are indistinguishable from slope changes in one image, and the integration accumulates error. **Photoclinometry** is the planetary science term for the same technique, and it is used there because stereo coverage is sparse and the surfaces (regolith, ice) are nearly uniform in albedo; **photoclinometric refinement** of a stereo DEM (Kirk et al. 2003 for Mars; the USGS ISIS and SOCET implementations) uses the stereo DEM for low frequencies and shading for the pixel-scale detail, achieving DEMs at the image resolution rather than the 3–5 pixel resolution of matching. Multi-image **photometric stereo** with several illumination directions resolves the albedo ambiguity. On Earth, shading is used mainly as a qualitative check and for shadow-length heights: the length of a building's or tree's shadow, with the sun elevation at acquisition time, gives its height to about one GSD divided by the tangent of the sun elevation — a method as old as Galileo's lunar mountains and still useful for checking satellite DSMs of cities ([Chapter 67](ch67-planetary-dems.md) covers the planetary side).

## 22.10 NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting

**Neural radiance fields (NeRF)** (Mildenhall et al. 2020) represent a scene as a neural network mapping a 3D position and viewing direction to colour and volume density, trained by rendering rays through the volume and minimising the difference against the input images, whose poses come from — note — a conventional SfM solution (usually COLMAP). **3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)** (Kerbl et al. 2023) replaces the network with millions of anisotropic Gaussians with colour and opacity, optimised the same way and rendered in real time by rasterisation; it has become the dominant representation for photoreal visualisation of captured scenes ([Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md), [Chapter 57](ch57-visualizing-dems.md)).

For visualisation both are superior to textured meshes: view-dependent effects, reflections, vegetation, and thin structures render convincingly. For measurement they are not, by construction, metrology. The optimisation target is image reproduction, not geometric fidelity; a representation can render perfectly from the training views while placing density or Gaussians away from the true surface (floaters, semi-transparent shells, surfaces displaced along the viewing direction where views are nearly collinear). Extracting a surface requires a secondary step — marching cubes on a density threshold, or depth rendering and fusion — whose output depends on the threshold chosen and whose errors are uncharacterised. The scale and datum are inherited entirely from the SfM poses, so a 3DGS model cannot be more accurate than the COLMAP solution it started from, and the self-calibration issues of Section 22.3 pass straight through. Published comparisons against lidar on built scenes show surface errors from centimetres on well-observed textured façades to decimetres or more on roofs, vegetation, and low-texture areas, with strong dependence on the extraction method (verify). The appropriate posture is to treat NeRF/3DGS as a rendering product derived from an SfM block, to report the SfM block's checkpoint accuracy as the only accuracy that has meaning, and to refuse any claim of "centimetre accuracy" for a splat model that has not been tested against independent geometry.

## Then & now

- **1776 ⟨H⟩.** Lambert's perspective geometry marks the conventional beginning of photogrammetry; Laussedat (1849) and Meydenbauer (1867) make it a surveying method.
- **1900s–1930s.** Pulfrich's stereocomparator (1901) and von Orel's stereoautograph (1908) mechanise parallax measurement; aerial survey cameras and analogue plotters (Zeiss, Wild) industrialise mapping between the wars; ASPRS founded 1934 ⟨H⟩.
- **1950s–1970s.** Analytical photogrammetry (Schmid, Brown) and the bundle adjustment formalise the block; Brown's 1971 distortion model; Corona (1960–72) and Hexagon (1971–86) reconnaissance stereo.
- **1981 ⟨H⟩.** Fischler & Bolles publish RANSAC.
- **1986–1999.** SPOT cross-track stereo; softcopy workstations; ASTER launched 1999 with along-track stereo.
- **1992 ⟨H⟩.** Tomasi & Kanade factorization shows structure and motion are jointly recoverable from image streams.
- **1996 ⟨H⟩.** Ames Stereo Pipeline begins (planetary).
- **2000 ⟨H⟩.** OpenCV first released, carrying Zhang's calibration method to every laboratory.
- **2004–2008.** SIFT (2004), Photo Tourism/Bundler (2006), SGM (2008).
- **2012–2016.** Westoby et al. introduce SfM to geomorphology; consumer drones proliferate; COLMAP (2016); ArcticDEM production begins with SETSM on WorldView.
- **2020–2023.** NeRF (2020) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (2023) move image-based reconstruction toward rendering; deep stereo networks enter production pipelines.

The method has not changed since Lambert; what changed is who can afford to apply it, from national mapping agencies to anyone with a phone — which moved the burden of understanding geometry from a trained operator to software defaults.

## Mathematics

**Collinearity equations.** With rotation matrix $R = [r_{ij}]$ from object to camera frame, perspective centre $(X_O, Y_O, Z_O)$, principal distance $f$, and principal point $(x_0, y_0)$,

$$x - x_0 + \Delta x = -f\,\frac{r_{11}(X - X_O) + r_{12}(Y - Y_O) + r_{13}(Z - Z_O)}{r_{31}(X - X_O) + r_{32}(Y - Y_O) + r_{33}(Z - Z_O)},$$
$$y - y_0 + \Delta y = -f\,\frac{r_{21}(X - X_O) + r_{22}(Y - Y_O) + r_{23}(Z - Z_O)}{r_{31}(X - X_O) + r_{32}(Y - Y_O) + r_{33}(Z - Z_O)},$$

where $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ are the distortion corrections. The Brown radial term is $\Delta r = k_1 r^3 + k_2 r^5 + k_3 r^7$ with $r^2 = (x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2$, and the decentring terms are $\Delta x_t = p_1(r^2 + 2\bar{x}^2) + 2p_2\bar{x}\bar{y}$, $\Delta y_t = p_2(r^2 + 2\bar{y}^2) + 2p_1\bar{x}\bar{y}$.

**Coplanarity, essential and fundamental matrices.** For two calibrated cameras with relative rotation $R$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$, corresponding normalised image rays $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_2$ satisfy the coplanarity condition $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_2^\top E\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1 = 0$ with the **essential matrix** $E = [\mathbf{t}]_\times R$ (five degrees of freedom; recoverable from five correspondences). For uncalibrated pixel coordinates with calibration matrices $K_1, K_2$, the **fundamental matrix** $F = K_2^{-\top} E K_1^{-1}$ satisfies $\mathbf{x}_2^\top F \mathbf{x}_1 = 0$ (seven degrees of freedom; the 8-point algorithm with normalisation). RANSAC estimates $E$ or $F$ from minimal samples and counts inliers by the Sampson distance.

**Parallax to height.** Normal case: $Z = fB/p$, $\sigma_Z = \dfrac{Z^2}{fB}\,\sigma_p$. For a satellite pair with convergence angle $\alpha$ and GSD $g$, the equivalent is $\sigma_Z \approx \dfrac{g\,\sigma_p^{\mathrm{(px)}}}{2\tan(\alpha/2)}$ in the symmetric case — a 30° convergence and 0.5 px matching precision at 0.5 m GSD gives $\sigma_Z \approx 0.5 \times 0.5 / (2 \times 0.268) = 0.47$ m.

**Bundle adjustment.** Minimise $\Phi = \sum_{i,j} \| \mathbf{x}_{ij} - \pi(\mathbf{C}_i, \mathbf{X}_j) \|^2_{\Sigma^{-1}} + \sum_k \|\mathbf{g}_k - \mathbf{X}_k\|^2_{\Sigma_g^{-1}} + \sum_i \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{C}_i^{\mathrm{pos}}\|^2_{\Sigma_p^{-1}}$ over camera parameters $\mathbf{C}_i$ (EO and, with self-calibration, IO) and object points $\mathbf{X}_j$, with GCP observations $\mathbf{g}_k$ and GNSS camera positions $\mathbf{p}_i$ weighted by their covariances. Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt iterations solve the normal equations $J^\top \Sigma^{-1} J\,\delta = J^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{r}$; the Jacobian is sparse (each observation involves one camera and one point), so the point block is eliminated by the **Schur complement** to leave a reduced camera system (Triggs et al. 2000). The inverse of the reduced normal matrix is the camera covariance from which precision maps are propagated. Reprojection RMS alone is not an accuracy measure: a doming block can have 0.3 px reprojection residuals and 0.5 m height error.

**SfM scale and the doming mode.** Image correspondences fix the reconstruction up to a 7-parameter similarity (3 rotation, 3 translation, 1 scale); GCPs or camera positions remove it. For a nadir block at height $H$, a perturbation $\delta k_1$ of the radial distortion coefficient is compensated by a surface deformation $\delta Z(\rho) \approx c\,\delta k_1\,H\,\rho^2$ (with $\rho$ the normalised distance from block centre and $c$ a geometry-dependent constant), which is the dome (James & Robson 2014).

**SGM cost aggregation.** For pixel $\mathbf{p}$ and disparity $d$, along each path direction $\mathbf{r}$,

$$L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}, d) = C(\mathbf{p}, d) + \min\big[L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}, d),\; L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}, d \pm 1) + P_1,\; \min_k L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}, k) + P_2\big] - \min_k L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}, k),$$

and the aggregated cost is $S(\mathbf{p}, d) = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}, d)$, minimised over $d$ with sub-pixel parabolic interpolation (Hirschmüller 2008). $P_2$ is usually scaled inversely with the image gradient so that discontinuities are allowed at edges.

> **Worked example.** *Stereo height precision for three systems.*
> (a) Drone: $H = 100$ m, 24 mm lens on 4 µm pixels so $f = 6{,}000$ px; a 6,000 px-wide sensor covers 100 m on the ground, and 80 % forward overlap gives $B = 20$ m per consecutive pair, but matching also uses images two and three apart, so take an effective $B = 40$ m. $\sigma_p = 0.3$ px: $\sigma_Z = (100^2/(6000 \times 40)) \times 0.3 = 1.25$ cm. GSD = 1.7 cm; so σ_Z ≈ 0.75 GSD, consistent with the 1–3 GSD rule after real-world matching degradation.
> (b) Aerial: $H = 1{,}500$ m, $f = 100$ mm on 5 µm pixels ($f = 20{,}000$ px), 60 % overlap with a 20,000 px swath, $B = 0.4 \times 1500 = 600$ m; $\sigma_p = 0.5$ px: $\sigma_Z = (1500^2/(20000 \times 600)) \times 0.5 = 9.4$ cm, GSD = 7.5 cm.
> (c) WorldView-3 pair at 0.31 m GSD, 35° convergence, $\sigma_p = 0.5$ px: $\sigma_Z \approx 0.31 \times 0.5 / (2\tan 17.5°) = 0.25$ m, before RPC bias.

## Validation & uncertainty

Image-derived DEM error has three layers that must be assessed separately because they have different spatial structure and different remedies.

**Random matching error** is per-pixel, roughly 0.3–1 px of parallax scaled by the geometry, larger in low texture and shadow, and reduced by gridding and smoothing. It is estimated internally from the redundancy of multi-view matching (depth consistency across views) and externally from checkpoint scatter on well-textured bare ground.

**Block deformation** — doming, tilts, scale error, and strip-wise warps in large aerial blocks — is smooth and systematic, invisible to reprojection statistics, and detectable only by independent checkpoints distributed to sample it (edges, corners, height extremes) or by differencing against an independent DEM on stable terrain. A checkpoint set clustered in the block centre will certify a domed block as accurate.

**Datum and control error** is a constant or slowly varying offset inherited from the GCP survey or the GNSS solution: antenna-height blunders, geoid model error, a base station on an old datum realisation. It is detected only by checkpoints surveyed independently of the control.

**Protocol.** (1) Define the product (DSM or DTM, grid spacing, vertical datum) before flying, following [Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md). (2) Survey GCPs and checkpoints as separate sets, by methods with independent error, with the checkpoint set sized and distributed to the ASPRS Edition 2 tables (and at least 20 for any meaningful statistic; 30 or more for class-wise reporting). (3) Place GCPs at block edges and height extremes; place checkpoints where you expect the worst behaviour — edges, far corners, steep ground, under partial canopy — not only where access is easy. (4) Process; record the self-calibrated IO parameters and compare against any prior calibration (a 1 % change in $f$ between projects with the same camera is a warning). (5) Compute residuals at checkpoints (never at GCPs); report RMSE$_V$, mean (bias), and σ separately for non-vegetated (NVA) and vegetated (VVA) terrain, and RMSE$_H$ from photo-identifiable checkpoints; if a specification still asks for Edition 1-style 95 % statistics, report both and say which is which. (6) Difference the DSM against an independent DEM (lidar, previous survey) on stable terrain, co-register (Nuth & Kääb 2011; `xdem`), and fit a low-order polynomial to the residual — a significant quadratic term is a dome. (7) Produce and inspect a water/snow/shadow mask and a matching-confidence or precision map, and state the fraction of the area that is interpolated. (8) Report the surface type (first visible surface), acquisition date and time, sun elevation, overlap, GSD, number and RMSE of GCPs, number and statistics of checkpoints, software and version, and processing parameters (depth filtering, mesh/grid settings).

> **Try it.** Measure block deformation independently of your own checkpoints by differencing an SfM DSM against a lidar DTM on stable bare ground and fitting a quadratic surface to the residual. Expected outcome for a well-constrained block: coefficients of the quadratic terms indistinguishable from zero and residual NMAD of 1–3 GSD; for a domed block: a quadratic term giving decimetres of bowl across the block.
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np, rasterio
> from rasterio.warp import reproject, Resampling
>
> sfm = rasterio.open("sfm_dsm.tif"); lid = rasterio.open("lidar_dtm.tif")
> lid_on_sfm = np.full(sfm.shape, np.nan, dtype="float32")
> reproject(lid.read(1), lid_on_sfm, src_transform=lid.transform, src_crs=lid.crs,
>           dst_transform=sfm.transform, dst_crs=sfm.crs, resampling=Resampling.bilinear,
>           src_nodata=lid.nodata, dst_nodata=np.nan)
> d = sfm.read(1).astype("float32") - lid_on_sfm
> stable = rasterio.open("stable_bare_mask.tif").read(1) > 0   # hand-digitised bare, unchanged ground
> rows, cols = np.nonzero(stable & np.isfinite(d))
> x, y = rasterio.transform.xy(sfm.transform, rows, cols)
> x = np.asarray(x) - np.mean(x); y = np.asarray(y) - np.mean(y)
> A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x, y, x*x, x*y, y*y])
> coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, d[rows, cols], rcond=None)
> resid = d[rows, cols] - A @ coef
> print("offset, tilt_x, tilt_y, quad_xx, quad_xy, quad_yy =", np.round(coef, 6))
> half = 0.5 * max(np.ptp(x), np.ptp(y))
> print(f"dome amplitude at block edge ≈ {coef[3]*half**2 + coef[5]*half**2:.3f} m")
> print(f"NMAD after removing quadratic = {1.4826*np.median(np.abs(resid-np.median(resid))):.3f} m")
> ```

## Pitfalls

- **Doming from self-calibration of nadir-only, GCP-poor blocks.** Radial distortion and surface curvature are nearly confounded. Add oblique images and edge GCPs or PPK positions; test with edge checkpoints or by quadratic fit against lidar.
- **Checkpoints that were also GCPs, or that were used to select the processing run.** The reported accuracy is then an internal residual, not an independent test. Keep the checkpoint file closed until the final product exists.
- **Selling or using a DSM as a DTM.** Image matching sees canopy and roofs; filtering produces an interpolated DTM under vegetation with no measurement in the gaps. Label the product correctly and quantify the interpolated fraction.
- **Smooth "water" surfaces that are matching noise.** Glint and ripple matching produces plausible-looking terrain. Mask water (NDWI, manual polygons) before gridding and before any volume computation.
- **RPC products used with metres of uncompensated bias.** Vendor RPCs carry orbit/attitude error. Apply bias compensation with GCPs or at least tie the pair internally, and co-register the DEM to a reference.
- **NeRF/3DGS outputs quoted with centimetre "accuracy".** The accuracy belongs to the SfM block, if to anything; the surface extracted from a radiance field has not been validated unless you validated it. Report the SfM checkpoint statistics and nothing more.
- **GCP coordinates in the wrong vertical reference.** Ellipsoidal heights entered as orthometric (or the reverse) shift the whole product by the geoid undulation, tens of metres in places. Check the receiver settings and the geoid model, and test one GCP against a published benchmark.
- **Exposure timing error with PPK positions.** A 50 ms timing error at 15 m s$^{-1}$ moves every camera 75 cm along track, which the adjustment absorbs as a tilt. Test the camera–GNSS event synchronisation on a static target.
- **Rolling-shutter cameras flown fast.** Skew enters the block as systematic deformation. Model it, or fly slower, or use a global-shutter camera.
- **Trusting reprojection RMS as accuracy.** It measures internal consistency; a biased block has low reprojection error. Only independent checkpoints measure accuracy.
- **Multi-year or multi-season compositing without dates.** Global stereo products average surfaces that changed. Prefer dated strips when change matters ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).
- **Historical imagery co-registered to modern control via features that moved.** Roads are realigned, shorelines shift. Use bedrock, old buildings, and multiple independent features; test on stable terrain.

## Key takeaways

- An image DEM is determined by three things: the camera model, the network geometry (overlap, convergence, obliques), and the control; software cannot compensate for a deficiency in any of them.
- Height precision follows $\sigma_Z = (Z^2/(fB))\,\sigma_p$: base-to-height ratio and matching precision are the levers, and 1–3 GSD is the practical floor.
- Self-calibrated nadir blocks dome; cure it with oblique images, edge GCPs, or precise camera positions, and prove the cure with edge checkpoints.
- Checkpoints must be independent of the GCPs in use, in selection, and in survey method; reprojection residuals are not accuracy.
- Image DSMs are first-visible-surface models; they fail predictably on water, snow, shadow, repetitive texture, moving and thin objects, and occluded walls — mask these, do not interpret them.
- Satellite stereo needs RPC bias compensation; dated strips (ArcticDEM/REMA/EarthDEM) preserve the time information that composites destroy.
- Historical photographs are the only source of past topography; their value depends entirely on careful distortion modelling and stable-terrain co-registration.
- Shape from shading adds detail to planetary DEMs; NeRF and 3DGS add realism to visualisation; neither adds metrology.
- Report GSD, overlap, calibration source, GCP and checkpoint counts and statistics, interpolated fraction, surface type, and datum with every product.
