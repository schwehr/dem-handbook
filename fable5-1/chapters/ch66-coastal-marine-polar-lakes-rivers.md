# Chapter 66 — Coastal, marine, polar, lakes and rivers

> **Part XIV — Domain deep dives.** The chapter for every place where the surface is wet, frozen, or moving: the datums, sensors, and failure modes peculiar to coasts, ports, the open ocean, the ice sheets, lakes, and rivers.

**In this chapter.** Where land meets water the elevation problem changes character: the reference surface is a tidal or lake datum rather than a geoid, the sensor is acoustic or green-laser rather than near-infrared, the bottom moves between survey lines, and the "terrain" may be a floating ice shelf or a sheet of sea ice that will be gone next month. This chapter walks the water-affected domains in turn—coastal zone, ports and estuaries, shelf and deep ocean, polar ice, lakes, rivers—and for each identifies the datum, the measurement, the characteristic error, and the validation that applies. You will learn to read a GEBCO type-identifier grid honestly, to budget a sound-speed or penetration bias, to convert sea-ice freeboard to thickness and know why the answer is ten times as uncertain as the measurement, to recognize a reservoir surveyed at drawdown, and to see why ellipsoidally referenced surveys remove the largest seam in topobathymetric products. It leans on the sensor chapters ([Chapters 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)–[23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)), [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md) on datums, and [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md) on water in DEMs, and adds what those leave to the domain.

## 66.1 The coastal zone

### 66.1.1 Integration and the white ribbon

A **topobathymetric DEM** merges land elevation (usually airborne lidar in orthometric heights) with bathymetry (sonar and bathymetric lidar in chart-datum depths) into one continuous surface ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)). The merge is where most coastal DEM errors are made, because the two halves differ in datum, sign convention, density, epoch, and accuracy, and because there is almost always a gap between them: the **white ribbon**, the strip from the lowest tide line out to the depth where vessels can safely work (typically 0 to −5 or −10 m), in which neither topographic lidar (water-absorbed) nor ship sonar (too shallow) measures the bottom. Bathymetric lidar ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)) fills it where water clarity permits—to 1–1.5 Secchi depths, i.e., 10–30 m in clear water, under 2 m in turbid estuaries, and nothing in the **surf zone**, where foam and bubbles scatter the green pulse. Satellite-derived bathymetry ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)) fills more at metre-level vertical accuracy; small USVs and jet-skis with single-beam sounders fill the rest where the budget allows. What should never fill it is interpolation presented as measurement: a straight line across a 200 m gap from a +1 m dune toe to a −5 m sounding misses every nearshore bar and trough, which is where wave run-up and flooding are decided. Eakins and Grothe (2014) catalogue the NOAA experience of building coastal DEMs from mixed sources: the problems are overwhelmingly datum and gap problems, not sensor-precision problems.

### 66.1.2 Tidal datums and the transformation

Charted depths are referenced to a **tidal datum**: **Mean Lower Low Water (MLLW)** in the United States, **Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT)** in most of the rest of the world, with national variants. Land heights are referenced to an orthometric datum (NAVD 88, ODN, NAP) or, increasingly, directly to the ellipsoid. Tidal datums are *local*—computed from gauge observations over a **National Tidal Datum Epoch (NTDE)** of 19 years (the US NTDE 1983–2001 is being superseded by 2002–2020)—and vary along a coast by metres with tidal range and shelf geometry. The transformation between them is a spatially varying surface: **VDatum** (NOAA; Parker et al. 2003) models the separation between tidal datums, NAVD 88, and the ellipsoid from gauges, hydrodynamic models, and the geoid; its stated uncertainty runs from ~5 cm in well-gauged estuaries to several decimetres on open coasts, and must be *added* to any topobathymetric merge that uses it. Other nations have equivalents (the UK's VORF, Australia's AusCoastVDT, the Dutch NLLAT surfaces). Short tidal datums—from a few months at a temporary gauge, corrected to the NTDE by simultaneous comparison with a control station—carry several centimetres more. [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md) covers the geodesy; the domain point is that a coastal DEM is only as good as the datum surface that joined its halves, and that surface is typically the least documented layer in the product.

> **Definitions that bite.** *Shoreline.* The US national shoreline on nautical charts is the **Mean High Water (MHW)** line; the FEMA coastal boundary, state property lines (MHW, MLW, or LAT depending on the state and on colonial-era law), the "wet/dry line" digitized from imagery, the lidar-derived zero-contour, and the "instantaneous waterline" in a satellite image are all different lines that can be tens to hundreds of metres apart on a gently sloping beach (slope 1:100 and tidal range 2 m give a 200 m swing). [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md) lists the definitions; in a coastal DEM state which one the land/water mask follows and from which datum and epoch it was derived.

### 66.1.3 Beaches, dunes, cliffs, and structures

The coast is the fastest-changing terrestrial terrain outside volcanoes and mines: beach profiles move metres vertically between summer and winter and tens of metres in plan in one storm; dunes scarp and rebuild; soft cliffs retreat episodically; and that change is what managers want from repeat DEMs ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md)). Detecting a single storm's dune erosion needs decimetre vertical precision and sub-metre registration, and lidar flown at low tide with the tide stage recorded. **Coastal structures**—seawalls, revetments, jetties, groins, breakwaters—are vertical or steep, often overhanging (rubble-mound armour), and partly submerged; a 2.5D DEM smears them into slopes, and a hydrodynamic model will see a seawall as a ramp unless the crest is enforced as a breakline ([Chapter 59](ch59-vector-data.md); [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). Marshes and mangroves carry the vegetation biases of [Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md): a lidar DTM of a *Spartina* marsh is typically 10–30 cm too high, a first-order error for inundation frequency where the whole elevation range is the tidal range.

### 66.1.4 Sea-level-rise exposure mapping

Mapping who and what lies below a future sea level is the most consequential public use of coastal DEMs, and Gesch (2018) set out the best practices that too many studies ignore. The method is to threshold the DEM at a water level; the result is only as good as (1) the DEM's vertical accuracy relative to the increment being mapped, (2) the datum transformation from the tidal datum of the sea-level scenario to the DEM's datum, and (3) hydrological connectivity (a low spot inland of a ridge is not flooded by the sea). Gesch's rule is that the minimum sea-level increment that can be mapped with confidence is roughly twice the DEM's linear error at 95 %: with lidar of RMSE$_z$ 10 cm (LE95 ≈ 20 cm) you can map a 0.4 m increment and should not map 0.1 m increments; with SRTM or ASTER GDEM (LE95 of several metres) you cannot map any plausible twenty-first-century increment, and the global exposure studies built on them disagree with lidar-based studies by factors of two to three in exposed population. **Vertical land motion** ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md))—subsidence of deltas at millimetres to centimetres per year—adds to the sea-level signal and is routinely missing from the DEM's epoch bookkeeping.

## 66.2 Ports, harbours, estuaries

### 66.2.1 Dredging surveys and payment volumes

A port's bathymetry is the land of [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md) with the signs reversed. **Maintenance dredging** removes accumulated sediment; **capital dredging** deepens; both are paid by the cubic metre removed, measured between **pre-dredge** and **post-dredge** surveys against a **design depth**, an **overdepth** allowance, and a tolerance. The USACE *Hydrographic Surveying* manual (EM 1110-2-1003) is the reference for US payment surveys and widely used elsewhere: it specifies multibeam coverage, line spacing, water-level control, sound-speed sampling, and accuracy classes. Volumes follow the methods of [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md) with the same dominant error terms—except that here the *top* surface is water-level-dependent: an error in the tide reduction applied to either survey is a bias over the whole area, and 5 cm on a 500 m × 100 m berth pocket is 2,500 m³. Modern practice references both surveys to the ellipsoid with RTK/PPK GNSS (an **ellipsoidally referenced survey**, [Section 66.7](#667-water-as-a-moving-reference)) and transforms to chart datum once, with a documented separation model.

**Siltation rates** of decimetres to metres per year in estuarine channels mean that the pre-dredge survey must be recent (days, not months) and that **channel condition surveys** are repeated on fixed cycles; the USACE's eHydro program publishes such surveys for US federal channels as public data, which is a useful source of repeat bathymetry for anyone studying estuarine change.

### 66.2.2 Fluid mud and the nautical bottom

In muddy estuaries (Rotterdam, Zeebrugge, Emden, the Gironde, the Amazon shelf, many Chinese ports) the bottom is a gradient, not a surface: above the consolidated bed lies **fluid mud**, a suspension of 100–300 g/L metres thick, through which a hull can pass without damage. High-frequency sounders (200 kHz and up) reflect from the top of the fluid mud; low-frequency sounders (15–33 kHz) penetrate to a denser horizon; the two "depths" differ by metres. Ports therefore define a **nautical bottom** by density—PIANC guidance and port practice use a critical density of roughly 1.15–1.25 t/m³, port-specific—measured with towed density probes rather than acoustically. A multibeam survey of such a port shows the 200 kHz surface and systematically *understates* navigable depth, and a DEM that merges those soundings with offshore data has a semantic discontinuity at the harbour mouth. [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md) covers the acoustics; the domain lesson is that "depth" in a port must be qualified by frequency or density.

### 66.2.3 Air gap and under-keel clearance

Two clearances govern whether a ship can enter: **under-keel clearance (UKC)**, water depth minus dynamic draft (static draft plus squat, heel, and wave response), and **air gap**, the clearance under a bridge or power line minus the ship's air draft. Both depend on the water level at the time, so the chart gives a conservative reference—depths at a low-water datum, bridge clearances at a high-water datum (MHW in the US, HAT in the UK)—and real-time systems supply the rest: NOAA's PORTS provides water levels, currents, and air-gap sensors at major US ports. The S-100 specifications turn these into gridded, time-varying layers—**S-102** bathymetry with uncertainty, **S-104** water levels, **S-111** currents ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md); [Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)). A UKC budget is a sum of uncertainties—survey TVU, water-level prediction, squat model, draft reading, siltation since survey—and the port's allowed UKC (often 10–15 % of draft) is sized to that sum.

**Berth pockets** (dredged boxes alongside quays) silt fastest and are surveyed most often; **turbidity** in the same waters is why satellite-derived bathymetry has nothing to offer inside most working ports.

## 66.3 Continental shelf and deep ocean

### 66.3.1 Multibeam systematics, sound speed, and footprint

Beyond the port, the **multibeam echo sounder (MBES)** is the only instrument that measures the seafloor with full coverage ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). Its systematic errors are the subject of that chapter; here, three matter for the products. **Sound-speed structure**—the profile of sound speed with depth, varying with temperature and salinity—bends each beam by Snell's law and sets its travel-time-to-depth conversion; an unmeasured change of a few metres per second in the profile produces the characteristic "smile" or "frown" across the swath, with outer-beam depth errors of a percent or more. **Footprint**: a 1° × 1° beam at 4,000 m depth illuminates a patch roughly 70 m across at nadir and several hundred metres across in the outer beams, so the resolving power of deep-water bathymetry is hundreds of metres regardless of the grid cell chosen; a 50 m grid of a 4,000 m survey is oversampled by an order of magnitude ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)). **Positioning and attitude**: at 4,000 m a 0.1° roll error is a 7 m depth error at the outer beam, and in deep water the sonar's own motion must be known to that precision through the water column's refraction.

### 66.3.2 Predicted bathymetry and Seabed 2030

Most of the ocean floor has never been ensonified. Where there is no sounding, global grids use **altimetry-predicted bathymetry** ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md); [Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)): marine gravity from satellite radar altimetry, inverted for seafloor topography at wavelengths of about 10–150 km and calibrated against existing soundings. Its vertical accuracy is of order 100–200 m, its horizontal resolution roughly 6–10 km at best, and seamounts under about 1–1.5 km in height are routinely missed. The **Nippon Foundation–GEBCO Seabed 2030** project (Mayer et al. 2018) set the goal of a direct-measurement global grid at depth-dependent resolution: 100 m cells in 0–1,500 m, 200 m in 1,500–3,000 m, 400 m in 3,000–5,750 m, and 800 m below. About a quarter of the seafloor was mapped to those targets as of the 2024 GEBCO release (26.1 %, per Seabed 2030), up from about 6 % in 2017 (Wölfl et al. 2019). The remaining three quarters are prediction.

> **Try it.** Read a GEBCO grid honestly by masking it with its **Type Identifier (TID)** grid. The TID distinguishes direct measurements (codes 10–17: singlebeam 10, multibeam 11, seismic 12, isolated sounding 13, ENC sounding 14, lidar 15, optical 16, combination 17) from indirect ones (40 predicted from satellite gravity, 41 interpolated, 42–45 digitized contours and related, 70–72 pre-generated grids and unknown). The expected outcome of the snippet below is a figure in which most of the deep ocean is grey (predicted) and a table of the fraction of your area that is actually measured.
>
> ```bash
> # Cut the GEBCO_2024 grid and its TID grid (both netCDF, downloaded from GEBCO) with GMT 6.
> gmt grdcut GEBCO_2024.nc -R-130/-120/30/40 -Gz.nc
> gmt grdcut GEBCO_2024_TID.nc -R-130/-120/30/40 -Gtid.nc
> # Fraction of cells by TID class:
> gmt grd2xyz tid.nc -Z | sort -n | uniq -c | awk '{s+=$1; a[$2]=$1} END {for (k in a) printf "TID %3d  %6.2f %%\n", k, 100*a[k]/s}'
> # Mask the depth grid to measured cells only (TID < 40) and plot both.
> gmt grdmath tid.nc 40 LT 0 NAN z.nc MUL = z_measured.nc
> gmt begin gebco_tid png
>   gmt subplot begin 1x2 -Fs8c/8c -A
>     gmt grdimage z.nc -Cgeo -c
>     gmt grdimage z_measured.nc -Cgeo -c
>   gmt subplot end
> gmt end
> ```
>
> Replace the first `grdcut` with a local download (GEBCO distributes the grid and TID as separate netCDF files). In most offshore California cells you will find 30–60 % TID 11 (multibeam) near the margin and mostly TID 40 beyond the continental rise.

### 66.3.3 Hazards, cables, pipelines, and offshore wind

> **Case file.** *USS* San Francisco, *8 January 2005.* The Los Angeles-class submarine, transiting at high speed and about 160 m (525 ft) depth some 560 km southeast of Guam, struck an uncharted seamount; one sailor died and nearly a hundred were injured. The Navy's investigation found that the chart in use showed no hazard, while another chart of the area, not aboard, indicated "discolored water"—a classic sign of a shoal—from a 1999 satellite image, and that the seamount's existence was suggested in other data not transferred to the navigation chart. The incident is cited in every discussion of global bathymetric coverage: in a region where the chart was built on sparse soundings and predicted bathymetry, the absence of a charted hazard is not evidence of its absence, and the TID of a grid cell tells you how much to trust the depth it shows ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)).

**Submarine cables and pipelines** are routed and monitored with MBES, sidescan, and sub-bottom profiling along corridors a few hundred metres wide and thousands of kilometres long. The hazards are migrating **sandwaves** (metres per year, exposing buried cables), **scour** around pipelines and structures, and slope failures; repeat surveys quantify these as DoDs with the moving-bedform aliasing issues of [Section 66.8](#668-validation-in-aquatic-settings). **Offshore wind** site characterization combines MBES bathymetry and **backscatter** (a proxy for sediment type), sub-bottom seismic, magnetometry for unexploded ordnance, and geotechnical sampling; bathymetry feeds foundation design, cable routing, and scour protection, and bathymetry plus backscatter feed **habitat mapping**, whose morphometric derivatives—slope, rugosity, bathymetric position index—carry the same resolution dependence as their terrestrial counterparts ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).

## 66.4 Polar

### 66.4.1 Ice-sheet surface DEMs

The surfaces of Greenland and Antarctica are now mapped at a resolution and repeat frequency unthinkable in 2010, from commercial stereo imagery processed on supercomputers. **ArcticDEM** (Porter et al. 2018) and the **Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica (REMA)** (Howat et al. 2019) are built from WorldView/GeoEye stereo pairs with the open-source SETSM algorithm and delivered as 2 m **strip DEMs** (each one stereo pair with its own date) and as mosaics. Strips are *relative* surfaces with metre-level absolute offsets until registered; mosaics are registered to **ICESat/ICESat-2** laser altimetry (ATL06 has decimetre accuracy over flat ice). Time-stamped strips are what make these products valuable for change: difference two strips a year apart over an outlet glacier and measure metres of thinning, provided co-registration ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) is done on stable rock and the dates are honored. Over featureless interior snow, stereo matching fails and the mosaics are infilled or masked. **CryoSat-2** in SARIn mode, processed with swath techniques, provides margin elevations at a few hundred metres resolution and sub-metre precision, and radar-altimetry time series since the 1990s (ERS, Envisat, CryoSat-2) remain the backbone of ice-sheet volume-change records.

### 66.4.2 Penetration bias

Radar does not stop at the snow surface. At X- and C-band (TanDEM-X, SRTM, Sentinel-1) the signal penetrates dry snow and firn by metres—up to roughly 10 m in the cold, dry percolation and dry-snow zones of Greenland and Antarctica (reported values vary with density, wetness, and season; several metres is typical, more in very cold dry firn)—and at Ku-band (CryoSat-2's altimeter) the retracked surface can sit metres below the physical surface when the snowpack is dry, then jump up to the surface after a melt event. Consequences: a TanDEM-X DEM of an ice sheet is a surface *inside* the firn, lower than the lidar or stereo surface by an amount that varies spatially; differencing it against an optical or laser DEM produces an apparent thinning of metres that is entirely penetration; and a radar-altimetry time series shows apparent elevation jumps when melt changes the penetration depth. Any ice-sheet change study that mixes radar with optical/laser DEMs must model or empirically correct penetration (e.g., by comparing against laser altimetry over the same season), and any reader of such a study should look for that correction before believing the mass balance.

### 66.4.3 Sea ice is not terrain

A surface DEM of the Arctic Ocean in winter shows a rough, ridged surface at a metre or so above sea level: sea ice. It is not terrain, it drifts at kilometres per day, it is gone by September over much of the basin, and it must never be composited into a "coastal DEM" or a land mask—a mistake that has occurred in automated global products built from winter imagery or altimetry. What sea-ice surfaces *are* used for is **thickness**: laser altimetry (ICESat-2) measures the **total freeboard** $F_t$ (snow surface above local sea level, found in leads), radar altimetry (CryoSat-2) measures approximately the **ice freeboard** $F_i$ (the snow–ice interface, if the Ku-band pulse penetrates the snow fully, which is itself uncertain), and hydrostatic equilibrium converts freeboard to thickness ([Mathematics](#mathematics)). The conversion multiplies the freeboard by roughly 10, so a 5 cm freeboard error becomes a 0.5 m thickness error, and the snow depth and densities—which are modelled from climatology, not measured—contribute comparable uncertainty. Sea-ice thickness from altimetry is a derived product with uncertainties of order 0.5 m or more on individual footprints, and the surface DEM that underlies it is ephemeral by design.

### 66.4.4 Ice shelves, grounding lines, and the bed

**Ice shelves** float. Surface elevation above sea level divided by $(1 - \rho_i/\rho_w)$, with a firn-air correction, gives thickness; the surface rises and falls with the ocean tide by a metre or two, and **tidal flexure** in the kilometres-wide zone between floating and grounded ice is how the **grounding line** is mapped—most precisely by differential InSAR and repeat-track altimetry. A DEM of an ice shelf is a snapshot at a tidal stage; differencing two without a tide model yields metre-level artefacts. **Bed topography** under the ice is the deepest "DEM" of this book: measured by airborne **radio-echo sounding** (60–600 MHz; [Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)) along flight lines tens of kilometres apart, interpolated by **mass conservation** (Morlighem et al. 2011, 2017, 2020)—using surface velocity, thinning rate, and surface mass balance to constrain ice flux, hence thickness, in fast-flowing regions—and by kriging elsewhere. **BedMachine Greenland** (v3, Morlighem et al. 2017) and **BedMachine Antarctica** (Morlighem et al. 2020) are products of that method; **Bedmap2** (Fretwell et al. 2013) and **Bedmap3** (Pritchard et al. 2025) are the compiled-sounding products for Antarctica, each distributed with an *error* grid running from tens of metres near flight lines to hundreds of metres in unsurveyed gaps. The error grids are the honest part; the smooth, plausible interpolated fjords between lines are the part to distrust, and sub-ice-shelf cavities and the sea floor in front of glaciers—exactly where warm water reaches the ice—remain among the least mapped places on Earth. **IBCAO** (v4, Jakobsson et al. 2020) and **IBCSO** (v2, Dorschel et al. 2022) are the Arctic and Southern Ocean regional grids within GEBCO and share its coverage problem: much of the Arctic shelf and essentially all under-ice regions are predicted or interpolated, and Arctic charting lags shipping growth.

## 66.5 Lakes

### 66.5.1 Lake datums

Large lakes have their own vertical datums because their surfaces are not at the geoid. The **International Great Lakes Datum (IGLD 1985)**, used for charting and water-level reporting on the Laurentian Great Lakes and St. Lawrence, is a *dynamic-height* datum (heights proportional to geopotential, so a level water surface has constant height) referenced to a gauge at Rimouski/Pointe-au-Père; it is periodically re-established because **glacial isostatic adjustment** tilts the basin—the northeastern shores rise relative to the southwestern by several millimetres per year, so benchmarks move by decimetres relative to one another over 30 years. The successor, **IGLD (2020)**, is being realized from GNSS and a geoid model rather than levelling, with release expected later this decade (verify). Each lake also has a **chart datum** (a low-water reference, e.g., 183.2 m IGLD 1985 for Lakes Michigan–Huron) below which charted depths are given. Smaller lakes and reservoirs typically have a local gauge datum, a **full pool** or **normal pool** elevation, and often a historic reference nobody has converted; a reservoir bathymetric survey must record which it used.

### 66.5.2 Campaigns, water levels, and the SWOT era

Lake bathymetry comes from small boats and **unmanned surface vessels (USVs)** with single- or multibeam sonar, from **bathymetric lidar** in clear lakes, and increasingly from **SDB** in clear shallow lakes ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)); the water-level series comes from gauges, from nadir radar altimetry (TOPEX/Jason/Sentinel-3/-6 along their tracks, decimetre accuracy on large lakes, degraded or absent on small ones), and since 2023 from **SWOT** (Surface Water and Ocean Topography; launched December 2022), whose Ka-band radar interferometer measures water-surface height over two 50 km swaths at a design accuracy of about 10 cm for lakes larger than 1 km², with a 21-day repeat (Biancamaria, Lettenmaier, and Pavelsky 2016). SWOT's lake products give water-surface *elevation*, *area*, and storage change for millions of previously ungauged lakes, and expose how many "lakes" in global datasets are seasonal, ice-covered half the year (the **lake ice season** flattens and misplaces the surface in every optical and radar product), or misclassified.

### 66.5.3 Reservoir sedimentation

Reservoirs trap sediment, lose capacity at roughly 0.5–1 % per year globally (highly variable), and are resurveyed to update their **elevation–area–capacity curves**. The classic survey resurveys range lines by single-beam sonar against the pre-impoundment topography; modern surveys use multibeam below the water line and lidar or UAV above it, at whatever pool level prevails. The recurring pitfall is a reservoir surveyed at drawdown presented as full-pool bathymetry: a survey covering the exposed bottom by lidar and the remaining pool by sonar is complete only if both were tied to one datum and the pool level at the sonar survey was recorded, and a grid whose extent is the drawdown shoreline, labelled with the reservoir's name, will be read later as the whole reservoir. **Sediment volume** is the DoD between original and current surfaces, with the base-surface problems of [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md) made worse by a pre-impoundment map that may be a 1930s contour sheet.

## 66.6 Rivers

### 66.6.1 Channel bathymetry

Topographic lidar does not see the river bed; where water is deeper than a few decimetres the returns are from the surface (if any) and the DTM is flattened across the channel ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). For hydraulic modelling the channel is the conveyance, and the missing bathymetry is the largest single error in most floodplain models ([Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). Channel bathymetry comes from **green (bathymetric) lidar** in clear rivers (to 1–2 Secchi depths, typically 2–8 m in gravel-bed rivers; turbid or white-water reaches fail), from **ADCP** transects (acoustic Doppler current profilers, which give depth along with velocity), from **single- and multibeam sonar** on boats and USVs in navigable rivers, from wading surveys with RTK GNSS in small streams, and from **spectral SDB** in clear shallow rivers (Legleiter, Roberts, and Lawrence 2009) with uncertainties of order 10–20 % of depth. Where none exists, modelers synthesize a channel from width, slope, and a hydraulic-geometry assumption, which should always be declared.

### 66.6.2 Water-surface slope, stage, and datum

The river's water surface is itself a measurement. Lidar returns from the water surface (sparse but present) give the **water-surface elevation (WSE)** along the channel at the flight time, and its slope—of order 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³, i.e., 10–100 cm/km—is a hydraulic quantity that models must reproduce; SWOT measures WSE and slope over reaches of 10 km for rivers wider than about 100 m (design accuracy ~10 cm in height, ~1.7 cm/km in slope). The **stage** at a gauge is referenced to the gauge's own datum—an arbitrary zero set by whoever installed it, which may or may not have been tied to a national datum—and **flood stages** (action, minor, moderate, major, in US NWS practice) are defined in stage, not elevation. Converting between a gauge's stage and a DEM's elevation requires the gauge datum in the DEM's vertical datum, a number that is frequently missing, outdated, or wrong by decimetres, and the **stage–discharge rating** adds its own uncertainty. The fact that a river's water surface is a sloping, time-varying datum is also why "the river's elevation" in a hydro-flattened DEM is a cartographic choice, not a measurement.

### 66.6.3 Dynamics, scour, and navigation channels

**Braided rivers** rework their beds annually; a braidplain DEM is dated to the week, and change detection must handle bars migrating hundreds of metres. **Sediment budgets** from repeat bathymetry/topography inherit the DoD framework of [Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md). **Bridge scour**—bed erosion at piers and abutments, the leading cause of bridge failure in the United States—is surveyed by sonar, often during floods when it is deepest, with fixed sonar monitors on critical piers. **Navigation channels** on large rivers (Mississippi, Rhine, Yangtze) are surveyed continuously by river authorities (USACE eHydro publishes US federal channel surveys) and dredged to maintain depth; the river's **low-water reference plane** (e.g., the Mississippi's LWRP) is yet another datum, a sloping surface defined by a reference discharge. **Riverbed classification** from backscatter and bedform morphology feeds roughness estimates for hydraulic models.

## 66.7 Water as a moving reference

Everything in this chapter is referenced, directly or indirectly, to a water surface that moves. **Tides** move it predictably by centimetres to more than 10 m; **storm surge** and **wind setup** by metres (Lake Erie's end-to-end setup has reached several metres in severe storms); **seiches** oscillate harbours and lakes at periods of minutes to hours; **river stage** varies by metres between low flow and flood; **atmospheric pressure** adds the inverse barometer effect at about 1 cm per hPa. Traditional hydrography reduces soundings to chart datum by subtracting the observed tide at a gauge, assumed to apply across a zone (**tidal zoning**); the error of that assumption—range and phase vary across an estuary—is often the largest component of a shallow-water survey's vertical budget, up to decimetres far from the gauge.

**Ellipsoidally referenced surveys (ERS)** remove it. With RTK or PPK GNSS on the vessel (lever arms and heave handled correctly), the sonar's position is known in ellipsoidal height directly; depth below the ellipsoid is transformed to chart datum through a **separation model** (VDatum's or a locally derived one), so the tidal-reduction error is replaced by the separation-model error, which is smaller, smooth, and documented (Dodd and Mills 2012). ERS also makes the land and water halves of a topobathymetric DEM share a datum natively, so the merge of [Section 66.1](#661-the-coastal-zone) becomes an overlap comparison rather than a datum conversion. The obstacles are GNSS outages under bridges and in fjords, heave–GNSS integration, and the need for an adequate separation model; where none exists, **GNSS buoys** and short-term gauges can build one. **SWOT** and nadir **altimetry** now provide the same moving reference—water-surface height against the ellipsoid—from space for rivers, lakes, and coastal seas, with the footprint and revisit caveats noted above.

> **Rule of thumb.** If your survey vertical budget is dominated by tidal zoning (common when the nearest gauge is more than ~10–20 km away or the estuary has strong tidal gradients), move to ERS and spend the saved effort on the separation model. The rule fails where a separation model cannot be built (no gauges, no geoid of adequate quality) or where GNSS is unusable (under cover, high latitudes with poor geometry and ionospheric scintillation), in which case a dense temporary gauge network is the alternative.

## 66.8 Validation in aquatic settings

Underwater there are no check points you can walk to. Validation substitutes consistency checks and a few hard references ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md); [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

**Reference surfaces** are flat, hard, stable patches of seafloor—or, better, built objects—surveyed at the start of each campaign to detect systematic errors (a **patch test** for roll, pitch, yaw, and latency offsets; a reference-surface comparison for the whole system); ports often maintain one on a concrete apron or a known wreck. **Crosslines**, run perpendicular to the main lines, are the universal internal check: IHO S-44 (Edition 6.1.0, 2022) and the NOAA Hydrographic Surveys Specifications and Deliverables (HSSD) require them and specify the statistics (crossline-to-main-scheme differences against the **Total Vertical Uncertainty (TVU)** allowance for the survey order). The S-44 formula $\text{TVU}_{95} = \sqrt{a^2 + (b \cdot d)^2}$ with order-specific $a$ and $b$ ($a$ = 0.25 m, $b$ = 0.0075 for Special Order; $a$ = 0.5 m, $b$ = 0.013 for Order 1) sets the bar the survey's **Total Propagated Uncertainty (TPU)** model must meet, and crosslines are the empirical test of the model. **Lead-line and diver checks** survive for shoal verification and least-depth determination over wrecks and obstructions, where a feature's minimum depth—not its mean—is the navigationally relevant number and a grid's cell averaging can bias it deeper.

**Soft and mobile bottoms** limit repeatability: fluid mud ([Section 66.2](#662-ports-harbours-estuaries)) returns different depths at different frequencies, and **bedforms that migrate between lines**—sandwaves moving decimetres per tidal cycle in energetic channels—produce apparent crossline disagreements and striping that are real change, not survey error. Diagnose by timing (does the discrepancy correlate with the time between lines and the tidal phase?) and by bedform geometry (does the "error" have the wavelength of the dunes?). **SDB versus MBES** comparisons are the standard validation for satellite bathymetry and give RMSE of about 0.5–1.5 m in depths under 15 m in clear water, degrading sharply with turbidity and depth (Pe'eri et al. 2014 for the reconnaissance use); the comparison itself needs a matched datum and date. Lastly, every aquatic validation must state the **water-level reduction** or ERS method and its uncertainty, because two surveys that disagree by 10 cm everywhere are more likely to differ in tide than in sonar.

## Then & now

Depth was measured with a **lead line** ⟨H⟩—a weighted, marked rope, tallowed to bring up a sediment sample—for three thousand years, and water level with a **tide staff** read by eye; a nineteenth-century hydrographic survey was a boat, a leadsman, a sextant for horizontal angles to shore marks, and a tide observer. The **singlebeam echo sounder** arrived in the 1920s (the German *Meteor* expedition of 1925–1927 made the first systematic acoustic profiles across an ocean basin), turning a point into a profile along track but leaving everything between tracks to interpolation. **SeaBeam**, the first commercial multibeam, entered service in 1977 ⟨H⟩ and gave a swath; GPS in the 1990s gave position without shore marks; RTK and PPK in the 2000s gave the vessel's ellipsoidal height and with it **ERS**; **bathymetric lidar** matured from experimental systems of the 1970s–1980s to operational coastal mapping (SHOALS, LADS, and successors) by the 2000s; and the 2010s added **satellite-derived**, **crowdsourced** (volunteer echo sounders feeding the IHO Data Centre for Digital Bathymetry), and **autonomous** (USV, AUV) bathymetry, with Seabed 2030 to coordinate them. In the polar regions, **radio-echo sounding** of ice thickness began in the 1960s; fifty years of flight lines were compiled into Bedmap (2001, 2013, 2025) and, with mass conservation, into **BedMachine** (2014 onward). The water surface became a satellite observable with **radar altimetry** (Seasat 1978; TOPEX/Poseidon 1992; lake and river applications from the 2000s) and, with **SWOT** in 2022, a two-dimensional one.

## Mathematics

**Tidal datums.** A tidal datum is the mean of a tidal statistic over a 19-year epoch: $\text{MLLW} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \text{LLW}_k$ over all $N$ tidal days in the NTDE (one lower-low water per tidal day). For a short-term station with $n$ months of record, the datum is transferred from a control station by **simultaneous comparison**: with mean range ratio $r = R_{\text{sub}}/R_{\text{ctl}}$ and mean tide level offset $\Delta\text{MTL} = \overline{\text{MTL}_{\text{sub}} - \text{MTL}_{\text{ctl}}}$ over the common period, $\text{MLLW}_{\text{sub}} \approx \text{MTL}_{\text{ctl,19yr}} + \Delta\text{MTL} - r\,(\text{MTL} - \text{MLLW})_{\text{ctl,19yr}}$ (the "standard method" of the NOAA Tidal Datums handbook); its uncertainty falls roughly as $1/\sqrt{n}$ from ~5–8 cm at one month to ~1–2 cm at one year in US practice.

**Sound-speed refraction.** For a layered water column, Snell's law $\sin\theta_i / c_i = \text{const}$ governs each beam's path. If the harmonic mean sound speed $\bar c$ used for the depth conversion is in error by $\delta c$, the nadir depth error is $\delta z / z \approx \delta c / \bar c$ (≈ 0.07 % per 1 m/s at 1,500 m/s); for a beam at angle $\theta$ a surface-layer error $\delta c_s$ produces an additional refraction error that grows approximately as $\tan^2\theta$, so outer beams at 60–70° show errors several times the nadir value—the swath "smile."

**Freeboard to thickness.** Hydrostatic equilibrium of floating ice with thickness $h_i$, snow depth $h_s$, densities $\rho_i, \rho_s, \rho_w$:

$$h_i = \frac{\rho_w}{\rho_w - \rho_i} F_i + \frac{\rho_s}{\rho_w - \rho_i} h_s \quad\text{(radar, ice freeboard } F_i\text{)}, \qquad h_i = \frac{\rho_w}{\rho_w - \rho_i} F_t - \frac{\rho_w - \rho_s}{\rho_w - \rho_i} h_s \quad\text{(laser, total freeboard } F_t\text{)} .$$

With $\rho_w$ = 1,024, $\rho_i$ = 917, $\rho_s$ = 320 kg/m³, the leading coefficient is $1024/107 \approx 9.6$: a 3 cm freeboard error is a 0.29 m thickness error, and a 10 cm snow-depth error is a 0.3 m (radar) or 0.66 m (laser) thickness error. For an ice shelf, $H = h_{\text{surf}} \cdot \rho_w / (\rho_w - \rho_i)$ after subtracting the firn-air content from the surface height.

**Mass-conservation bed inversion (brief).** With depth-averaged velocity $\bar{\mathbf{v}}$ (from surface velocity and a sliding assumption), thickness $H$, surface mass balance $\dot a$, and thinning rate $\partial H / \partial t$, continuity gives $\nabla \cdot (H \bar{\mathbf{v}}) = \dot a - \partial H / \partial t$. Along a flowline this is a first-order equation for $H$ that can be integrated from radar-sounded thicknesses upstream; BedMachine solves it as a constrained least-squares problem minimizing misfit to radar lines plus a regularization term, so that the bed error is small near lines and in fast flow and grows in slow interior ice where the equation loses its constraint.

**Air gap and UKC budgets.** Available air gap $= C_{\text{chart}} + (D_{\text{HW datum}} - \eta(t))$ and available UKC $= d_{\text{chart}} + (\eta(t) - D_{\text{LW datum}}) - T_{\text{static}} - s(v) - w$, with $\eta(t)$ the water level, $T$ the draft, $s(v)$ the speed-dependent squat, $w$ the wave/heel allowance; the uncertainty is the root-sum-square of the survey TVU, water-level error, squat-model error, and draft error, and the allowed minimum is set to cover it at the chosen confidence.

## Validation & uncertainty

The aquatic domains share a structure of error: a **sensor** term (sonar, lidar, radar, stereo), a **water-column** term (sound speed, refraction at the air–water interface, turbidity, penetration into snow/firn/mud), a **reference-surface** term (tide/water level, datum separation, lake or river datum), and a **time** term (bedforms, ice drift, stage, seasonal ice). The validation program should test each separately.

For a **coastal topobathymetric DEM**, test the land half against RTK check points (NVA/VVA; [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)), the bathymetric half against crosslines and reference surfaces (TVU), and the *seam* by comparing overlapping lidar and sonar in the 0 to −5 m band after both are reduced to one datum—the overlap mean difference is the datum-transformation error you will otherwise never see. Report the datum surface used (VDatum version or local separation model) and its stated uncertainty as a component of the DEM's uncertainty. Mask, rather than interpolate, the unsurveyed white ribbon, or flag interpolated cells in an auxiliary layer (as the GEBCO TID does).

For **MBES surveys**, run the patch test and a reference-surface comparison at the start of each project; sample sound speed at a frequency set by the water column's variability (hourly in stratified estuaries, every few hours offshore); compute TPU per sounding with the vessel's offsets and uncertainties (the CUBE/CHRT estimators and BAG format carry these to the grid; [Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)); and compare crossline differences against the TVU allowance (S-44 requires that 95 % of differences fall within it). A systematic crossline difference that scales with beam angle is refraction; one that is constant is tide or draft; one that varies with time is a moving bed or a tide-zoning error.

For **polar DEMs**, co-register on exposed bedrock (nunataks, coastal rock) before differencing; compare radar-derived surfaces against ICESat-2 crossings in the same season to estimate penetration; treat ice-shelf surfaces as tide-dependent and apply a tide model (e.g., CATS2008 via pyTMD) before differencing; and read the bed products' error grids before drawing a fjord.

For **lakes and rivers**, record the water level and its datum at the time of every survey; tie gauge datums to the national datum by GNSS levelling rather than trusting the gauge description; and validate SWOT or altimetry WSE against gauges with the gauge datum conversion stated.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical uncertainty components for a shallow-water (10 m) hydrographic survey reduced to chart datum by tidal zoning versus by ERS (1σ, illustrative values typical of a well-run survey; actual values must come from the project's TPU model).
>
> | Component | Tidal zoning | ERS (RTK/PPK) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Sonar range/bottom detection (10 m depth) | 3–5 cm | 3–5 cm |
> | Sound speed (nadir, profiled 2-hourly) | 1–2 cm | 1–2 cm |
> | Heave / induced heave from attitude | 3–5 cm | 1–3 cm (GNSS height replaces heave at low frequencies) |
> | Draft, settlement, squat | 3–5 cm | 0 (absorbed in GNSS height) |
> | Water-level measurement at gauge | 1–2 cm | — |
> | Tidal zoning (gauge 15 km away, estuary) | 5–15 cm | — |
> | GNSS height (RTK, short baseline) | — | 2–4 cm |
> | Separation model (ellipsoid → chart datum) | — | 3–10 cm |
> | **Root-sum-square** | **~8–17 cm** | **~6–12 cm** |
>
> At 95 % the zoned survey sits at 16–34 cm, close to or above the Special Order TVU of 25 cm at 10 m depth ($\sqrt{0.25^2 + (0.0075\cdot 10)^2}$ ≈ 0.26 m); the ERS survey meets it with margin, and its largest term—the separation model—is one that improves over time as the model is refined, whereas tidal zoning error is re-incurred on every survey.

## Software

**Open source:** **MB-System** (multibeam processing, editing, gridding; the reference open hydrographic toolkit) — caveat: steep learning curve and limited support for the newest proprietary formats. **GMT** (gridding, `grdblend` for composites, `grdtrack` for crossline comparisons, plotting). **VDatum** (NOAA; free, Java) and **vyperdatum** (NOAA's Python wrapper for VDatum-grid transformations in pipelines) — caveat: coverage is US waters only. **NOAA HydrOffice tools** (Sound Speed Manager for profiles; QC Tools for S-57/BAG checks). **pyTMD** (tide and load-tide prediction from global and regional models, including polar models such as CATS2008 and AOTIM) for ice-shelf and coastal reductions. **SWOT toolkits** (NASA PO.DAAC `swot` and `hydrocron` access tools; the SWOT River Database SWORD). **xdem** and **demcoreg** (co-registration of ArcticDEM/REMA strips). **PDAL** (topobathy lidar classification with the ASPRS bathymetric classes), **GDAL** (BAG driver; `gdalwarp` with vertical-grid transformations via PROJ). **QGIS** with the Crayfish/MDAL plugins for mesh and time-varying water products. **Kluster** (NOAA's open multibeam processing in Python; status should be checked). **R `oce`** for CTD/sound-speed handling.

**Free but closed:** **GEBCO/Seabed 2030 grids and TID** are open data; NOAA's **ENC Online** and **nowCOAST**; **HYPACK Viewer** style read-only tools for inspecting deliverables — caveat: viewers do not expose the reductions applied.

**Commercial:** **CARIS HIPS/SIPS and BASE Editor** (multibeam processing, CUBE, BAG; the dominant hydrographic-office suite), **QPS Qimera/Qinsy/Fledermaus** (acquisition, processing, visualization), **HYPACK/HYSWEEP** (acquisition and dredging-survey quantities), **EIVA NaviSuite** (offshore survey and construction), **Teledyne PDS** and **BeamworX** (acquisition/processing), **Chiroptera/Leica HawkEye and Teledyne CZMIL processing suites** (bathymetric lidar). Caveat common to all: the tide/ERS reduction and separation model applied are project settings that must be exported with the deliverable, or the downstream user cannot know the datum.

## Pitfalls

- **Mixing LAT-based and MLLW-based grids** → both are "chart datum" in their metadata → check the issuing agency and nation; the difference is decimetres to over a metre in macrotidal areas; transform through a common ellipsoidal reference.
- **Merging land in orthometric metres with bathymetry in chart-datum feet** → legacy US soundings in feet or fathoms, lidar in metres NAVD 88 → convert units and datum explicitly with VDatum or a local separation model, and test the overlap band.
- **Interpolating across the white ribbon** → no data between +1 m and −5 m → mask it, or fill with SDB/USV data flagged as such; never let a straight line stand in for the nearshore bar.
- **A reservoir surveyed at drawdown presented as full-pool bathymetry** → survey extent equals the shoreline on the day → record pool level and datum; extend with lidar of the exposed bed; label the product with its coverage.
- **Penetration bias in radar ice DEMs read as mass loss** → X/C/Ku-band surfaces sit metres below the snow surface in dry firn → compare against laser altimetry in-season; correct or restrict comparisons to like-with-like sensors.
- **Sea ice in a "coastal DEM" or land mask** → winter imagery or altimetry treated as terrain → use seasonal masks; sea ice drifts and melts.
- **Bedform migration aliased as survey error** → crossline differences with the wavelength of the dunes → check timing and geometry; report as change, not error; tighten survey duration.
- **Fluid mud: high-frequency depth taken as navigable depth** → 200 kHz reflects from the lutocline → use dual-frequency sounders and density profiling in muddy ports; qualify "depth" by method.
- **Tidal zoning from a distant gauge** → single gauge applied across an estuary → ERS, or a temporary gauge network; quantify the zoning error in the TPU.
- **Ice-shelf DEMs differenced without a tide model** → metre-scale tidal displacement between acquisitions → apply an ocean-tide (and load-tide) model via pyTMD before differencing.
- **Gauge stage treated as elevation** → gauge datum unknown or stale → GNSS-level the gauge; publish the datum offset with the DEM.
- **Mapping 0.1 m sea-level increments on a 0.2 m LE95 DEM** → the increment is below the DEM's resolving power → follow Gesch (2018): smallest increment ≈ 2 × LE95; map with connectivity.
- **Trusting a smooth BedMachine/Bedmap fjord** → interpolation between radar lines looks like data → read the error grid; treat unsurveyed troughs as unknown.
- **Deep-water grids posted at 50 m from a 4,000 m survey** → cell size chosen from habit → post at or above the footprint; note effective resolution ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).

## Key takeaways

- Every water body brings its own datum (tidal, lake, river, ellipsoidal) and its own time scale; name the datum, the epoch, and the water level for every aquatic surface you produce or use.
- Ellipsoidally referenced surveys with a documented separation model remove the largest seam in coastal products and the largest error in shallow-water hydrography.
- The deep ocean (three quarters of it) and the polar beds are predicted or interpolated, not measured—read the TID and the error grids before trusting a cell.
- Bathymetric resolution is set by the footprint and the line spacing, not the grid; deep-water DEMs resolve hundreds of metres.
- Radar penetrates snow, firn, and mud; "surface" depends on frequency, and mixed-sensor differencing needs a penetration correction.
- Sea ice and ice shelves float and move; freeboard-to-thickness and tide-dependent ice-shelf heights carry uncertainties ten times the raw measurement.
- Lakes and rivers now have a space-borne water-surface reference (SWOT, altimetry); it still has to be tied to gauges whose datums are known.
- Validation underwater is crosslines, reference surfaces, overlap bands, TPU models, and timing analysis for moving beds—plan them into the survey, not after it.
