# Chapter 14 — Positioning without (or beyond) GNSS: acoustic, terrain-aided, radio, barometric

> **Part IV — Positioning and orientation.** GNSS ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) fails under water, underground, indoors, under dense canopy, in deep urban canyons, and whenever someone switches on a jammer; this chapter covers the methods that take over — and what each one does to the error budget of an elevation product.

**In this chapter.** Every method in this chapter either grows its error with time (dead reckoning, barometric drift), with range (acoustic, radio), or inherits the error of a map (terrain-aided and visual navigation). None gives the "fix and forget" absolute positioning of a GNSS receiver under open sky, and planning a survey that relies on them means budgeting for that explicitly. The chapter treats acoustic positioning under water (LBL, SBL, USBL, inverted USBL) with its dependence on sound speed and calibration; terrain-aided and terrain-relative navigation, from TERCOM to the Mars 2020 Lander Vision System, in which the DEM becomes the positioning system and its errors become yours; visual odometry and map matching; radio methods (eLoran, UWB, WiFi/BLE/cellular, 5G); the classical terrestrial instruments (total station, laser tracker, traverse, levelling) that remain the most accurate positioning tools at short range; celestial navigation as a backup; barometric altimetry and its weather-driven errors, including what a drone means by "AGL"; and signals of opportunity and GNSS reflectometry. You should finish able to write the error budget for a USBL-positioned AUV survey, decide whether a barometric height is usable for a given purpose, and recognize when a terrain-relative fix is only as good as the map it matched.

## 14.1 Acoustic positioning under water

Radio waves attenuate within metres in seawater; sound travels kilometres. Underwater positioning therefore measures acoustic travel times and directions between **transponders** (which reply to an interrogation after a known turnaround delay) or **beacons/pingers** (which transmit on a schedule) and **transceivers** on the vessel or vehicle. Three geometries, named for the length of their baselines, dominate (Milne 1983; Vickery 1998).

**Long baseline (LBL)** uses an array of transponders deployed on the seafloor, hundreds of metres to kilometres apart, whose positions are first determined by surveying each from the surface vessel (ranging from many GNSS-positioned points and solving by least squares — the **box-in** or calibration survey). A vehicle interrogates the array and trilaterates from the slant ranges. Within the array, repeatability is 0.1–1 m at kilometre ranges, limited by sound-speed knowledge and ray bending; absolute accuracy is that of the array calibration plus the surface GNSS, typically 1–5 m in deep water. LBL is the choice when metre-level absolute positioning over a work site is needed for days or weeks (offshore construction, repeat AUV surveys, seafloor geodesy); the cost is deploying and calibrating the array. Low-frequency (8–16 kHz) systems reach 5–10 km; high-frequency (300 kHz) "mini" LBL gives centimetres over tens of metres for metrology.

**Short baseline (SBL)** mounts three or more hydrophones on the hull, metres to tens of metres apart, and locates the transponder from the time differences of arrival. It needs a large vessel for a useful baseline and an accurate survey of the hydrophone positions; it is now rare outside specialized applications.

**Ultra-short baseline (USBL)** packs the hydrophone array into a single transceiver head a few tens of centimetres across and measures the *phase difference* of the arriving signal across the array to obtain the direction of arrival, plus the two-way travel time for range. A single head on a pole or in a hull gate therefore gives range, bearing, and depression angle to a transponder — no seafloor array, immediate operation — at the cost of an accuracy that is **proportional to slant range** (the angular error times the range) and dependent on the vessel's attitude and heading, which must be supplied by an MRU ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)) and whose errors rotate the whole fix. Manufacturer specifications for modern systems (Sonardyne Ranger 2, Kongsberg HiPAP, iXblue Gaps, EvoLogics) quote 0.05–0.3 % of slant range (1σ or "accuracy," definitions vary) under good conditions after calibration; at 3 000 m that is 1.5–9 m. The attitude requirement is the whole budget: a 0.1° heading error is 5 m at 3 000 m, so a USBL positioning a deep AUV needs an MRU of airborne-lidar quality. **Inverted USBL (iUSBL)** puts the transceiver on the vehicle and the transponder on the vessel (whose position is known from GNSS), so that the vehicle computes its own position in real time without a telemetry link; it is attractive for AUVs because the vehicle's INS can integrate the fix directly.

**Sound-speed dependence.** Every acoustic range is a travel time multiplied by a sound speed, and the speed varies by about 1 500 ± 50 m/s with temperature, salinity, and pressure (3 % range), with gradients that refract the ray. A harmonic-mean sound speed error of 1 % gives a 1 % range error — 30 m at 3 km — and refraction bends oblique rays so that the straight-line assumption misplaces the target, most severely for USBL at low depression angles. A **sound velocity profile** (SVP) from a CTD or velocimeter cast ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)) is required for any serious acoustic positioning; the surface sound speed at the transceiver head is needed separately for USBL beamforming (the direction of arrival is computed from phase differences assuming a wavelength, which depends on local sound speed). A USBL operated without an SVP in stratified water is a system with an undocumented bias of metres.

**Calibration.** A USBL head's mounting angles relative to the MRU (three angles), the lever arms, and the system latency must be calibrated by a **box-in**: a transponder is placed on the seabed and the vessel steams a pattern (a box or cloverleaf of reciprocal lines plus a spin on station) while logging fixes; the misfit between fixes from different headings solves for the alignment angles (the acoustic equivalent of a boresight calibration). Residuals after calibration should be at the manufacturer's percentage-of-range level; a repeatable offset that depends on heading is an uncorrected alignment. Transponder depths from their own pressure sensors, if telemetered, constrain the vertical and improve the fix.

> **Worked example.** USBL error budget for an AUV at 2 000 m slant range, 45° depression angle. Components (1σ): USBL angular precision 0.1° → $2000 \times 1.75\times10^{-3} = 3.5$ m perpendicular to the line of sight; range (timing + sound speed, 0.2 %) → 4 m along the line of sight; MRU heading 0.05° → $2000\cos 45° \times 8.7\times10^{-4} = 1.2$ m horizontal; MRU roll/pitch 0.02° → 0.7 m; vessel GNSS (PPK) 0.03 m; lever arms 0.1 m; residual alignment after box-in 0.05° → 1.7 m. Horizontal RSS (projecting the angular terms): roughly $\sqrt{(3.5\cos45°)^2 + (4\cos45°)^2 + 1.2^2 + 0.7^2 + 1.7^2} \approx \sqrt{6.1+8+1.4+0.5+2.9} \approx 4.4$ m; vertical about $\sqrt{(3.5\sin45°)^2 + (4\sin45°)^2 + 0.7^2} \approx 3.8$ m, which a vehicle pressure sensor (±0.5 m) replaces entirely. The AUV's INS+DVL ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.7) then smooths these fixes: the resulting trajectory is smoother than 4.4 m noise suggests, but any *bias* (sound speed, alignment) passes straight through, and the absolute horizontal uncertainty of the AUV's bathymetry is a few metres — which on a 10° slope is 0.5–0.9 m of apparent depth error when compared to a surface survey.

## 14.2 Terrain-aided and terrain-relative navigation

If a vehicle carries a map of the terrain and a sensor that measures the terrain beneath it, it can find its position by matching — the DEM becomes the positioning system. **TERCOM** (Terrain Contour Matching; Golden 1980) did this for cruise missiles from the 1970s: a radar altimeter measured a profile of ground clearance, which, subtracted from the barometric altitude, gave a profile of terrain elevation that was correlated against a stored DEM strip to find the along- and across-track offset; the accuracy was about one DEM cell in rough terrain, and the method failed over flat or self-similar terrain (water, plains) where the profile carried no information. Submarines have navigated by matching bathymetric soundings to charts since the Cold War; **bathymetric navigation** for AUVs (terrain-relative navigation, TRN) matches multibeam or DVL-beam depths against a prior bathymetric grid using a particle filter or point-mass filter, producing fixes of about one to a few grid cells wherever the seafloor has relief — which, over a surveyed site, bounds dead-reckoning drift indefinitely without acoustic infrastructure (Nygren 2005; Meduna, Rock & McEwen 2010).

The space version made the method famous: the **Mars 2020 Lander Vision System** (Johnson et al. 2022) matched descent-camera images against an orbital (HiRISE/CTX) base map and DEM during the parachute phase, estimating the lander's position to roughly 40 m relative to the map within seconds and enabling the divert manoeuvre that landed Perseverance in Jezero crater's hazardous terrain — a direct descendant of the TRN work of Johnson & Montgomery (2008). Terrestrially, aircraft and drones increasingly carry visual terrain-matching as a GNSS backup, and automotive localization against high-definition maps is the same idea at centimetre scale.

The validation point is the one this book keeps returning to: **the map's errors become the vehicle's errors.** A TRN fix has three error components — the matching precision (how sharply the likelihood peaks, which depends on terrain relief, sensor noise, and the number of samples), the map's *internal* geometric accuracy (its own positioning, resolution, and interpolation artefacts), and the map's *absolute* datum and registration. A vehicle navigating against a 50 m-cell bathymetric grid compiled from 1980s LORAN-positioned soundings will match that grid faithfully and inherit its 100–500 m absolute offsets; the resulting new survey, if it is then used to update the grid, propagates the error forward. Mixing TRN-positioned data into a compilation without recording what map it was matched against is a provenance failure ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)). The matching likelihood (Mathematics) also tells you where the method cannot work: where the terrain is flat relative to the sensor noise, the posterior is as wide as the prior, and the filter should report that honestly rather than converge on a false peak.

> **Case file.** Lander Vision System performance on Mars 2020 (Johnson et al. 2022, AIAA SciTech Forum): during the 18 February 2021 landing, the LVS produced its first map-relative position fix at about 4 200 m altitude and converged to an estimate used by the Safe Target Selection algorithm, with a final landing position within the predicted error ellipse and a map-relative accuracy reported at the tens-of-metres level. The map itself — a HiRISE orthomosaic and DEM — had been controlled to the Mars geodetic frame with its own uncertainties, which bounded what "position" could mean; the mission's hazard avoidance depended on map-relative, not absolute, accuracy, and the system was designed around that distinction. The lesson for terrestrial TRN is the same: state the frame the fix is relative to.

## 14.3 Visual odometry, visual-inertial navigation, and map matching

**Visual odometry (VO)** estimates camera motion from the apparent motion of image features between frames; **visual-inertial odometry (VIO)** fuses this with an IMU, which supplies scale (monocular cameras alone cannot measure it), short-term dynamics, and gravity direction. Modern VIO (e.g., OpenVINS, VINS-Mono, and the estimators inside consumer AR frameworks and drone flight controllers) drifts at roughly 0.5–2 % of distance travelled in textured environments and fails in featureless, dark, or dynamic scenes. It is a dead-reckoning method — error grows with distance — and it is the front end of visual SLAM ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)), where loop closures and a map bound the drift. For elevation work VIO appears in drones (as a GNSS-denied hold and a sanity check), in handheld and backpack scanners, and in the camera trajectories of SfM ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)), where the "trajectory" is the bundle adjustment itself.

**Map matching** constrains a dead-reckoned or noisy position to a map: a car to a road network, a train to a track, a pedestrian to a floor plan, a vehicle to a lane-level HD map, an aircraft to a terrain database. It removes error in the direction the map constrains (across the road) and leaves it in the direction it does not (along the road), and it fails where the map is wrong or incomplete — a new road, a closed lane, a moved wall. In 3D, **3D-mapping-aided GNSS** and shadow matching ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.8) use a city model to predict satellite visibility and reflections; the city model's quality (its building heights, from a DSM) sets the ceiling ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

## 14.4 Radio and indoor positioning

**eLoran.** Enhanced Loran transmits the 100 kHz LORAN-C pulse groups with time-of-transmission control to UTC, a data channel carrying differential corrections and integrity, and receivers that use all stations in view rather than one chain. Its absolute accuracy is limited by **additional secondary factors (ASF)** — propagation delays over land and coast that depend on ground conductivity and must be mapped and broadcast as corrections; with ASF corrections, trials in the UK (General Lighthouse Authorities, 2010s) and operations in South Korea demonstrate 8–20 m (95 %) in coastal areas, degrading inland and far from monitors. Its value is not accuracy but **independence**: a high-power, low-frequency ground wave that no GNSS jammer affects, providing a position of tens of metres and, more importantly, a timing reference of about 50–100 ns for infrastructure. For elevation work it is a navigation and timing backup, not a positioning source; its revival (South Korea operational since 2021; the United States and United Kingdom have variously funded, cancelled, and re-studied it — check the current status) is a response to the fragility noted in [Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md).

**Ultra-wideband (UWB)** ranging measures time of flight between small transceivers using nanosecond pulses over a few GHz of bandwidth, achieving 5–30 cm ranging precision over tens of metres indoors, with the usual non-line-of-sight bias (positive, since a reflected path is longer) when a wall or body blocks the direct path. A UWB anchor network surveyed by total station positions a tag — a scanner, a robot, a worker — to 10–30 cm in 3D, with the vertical again weakest because anchors are usually all at similar heights (the same geometry problem as GNSS VDOP). It is the practical indoor positioning technology for construction and industrial mapping and the one used by phones for short-range "find" features.

**WiFi, Bluetooth Low Energy, and cellular** positioning use received signal strength or (for WiFi RTT, 802.11mc/az) round-trip time against databases of access-point and beacon locations; accuracy is 2–10 m for WiFi fingerprinting indoors, 1–5 m for BLE beacons with a dense deployment, and 50 m–1 km for cellular cell-ID and timing-advance methods. These are the positions behind a phone's location when GNSS is unavailable, and they carry no vertical information beyond floor-level inference from the beacon database — a phone-reported "altitude" indoors is barometric (§14.7) or absent.

**5G positioning.** 3GPP Release 16 and later define positioning reference signals and methods (downlink time difference of arrival, multi-cell round-trip time, angle of arrival/departure using antenna arrays) with targets of a few metres outdoors and sub-metre indoors in Release 17/18 for industrial scenarios; real-world performance depends on base-station synchronization, geometry, and multipath, and published measurements at the time of writing are mostly from trials rather than operational networks (verify). As with eLoran, the near-term value is a GNSS-independent position of metres and a timing source, not survey positioning.

## 14.5 Total stations, laser trackers, traverses, and levelling

The most accurate positioning tools available operate without satellites and have done so for a century. A **total station** ([Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md), §11.2) measures horizontal and vertical angles to 0.5–5″ and distances to a prism to 1 mm + 1–2 ppm (reflectorless to a few millimetres at 100–300 m, with surface-dependent biases); set up over a known point and oriented to a known backsight, it positions a prism to a few millimetres at 100 m and about 1 cm at 500 m, with the vertical limited by refraction (the coefficient $k \approx 0.13$ nominally, but varying from −1 to +1 near the ground on sunny or still days, giving vertical errors of centimetres per hundred metres for low lines of sight) and by the instrument and target heights — the same antenna-height blunder in a different costume. **Robotic total stations** track a prism automatically and are the position source for machine control, for monitoring, and for positioning scanners and drones in GNSS-denied sites; a robotic total station tracking a drone-mounted prism yields a 1–2 cm trajectory under a canopy or inside a stadium. **Laser trackers** (interferometric or absolute distance to a spherically mounted retroreflector) reach 10–50 µm at tens of metres and are the metrology instruments for vessel offset surveys and sensor calibration ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.4; [Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)).

A **traverse** carries coordinates from a known point through a chain of total-station setups to another known point; the misclosure at the far end measures the accumulated error, and the adjustment (compass rule, or least squares) distributes it. Traverses under canopy from GNSS points in clearings are how DEM checkpoints get into forests ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.8); a well-run traverse of 1 km with ten setups closes to a few centimetres. **Differential levelling** — reading a staff through a horizontal telescope, backsight minus foresight — remains the most accurate height-transfer method: first-order geodetic levelling achieves 0.5–1 mm/√km, and even ordinary engineering levelling (3 mm/√km) outperforms GNSS heights over short distances. Levelling gives *orthometric-type* height differences directly (strictly, geopotential differences; [Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)), which is why benchmark networks exist and why a levelled tie from a benchmark to a DEM checkpoint tests the geoid model as well as the DEM. These instruments are GNSS-independent in the field but are tied to the datum only through the control they start from — a traverse from a mis-coordinated point is precisely wrong everywhere.

## 14.6 Celestial navigation as backup

A sextant altitude of a celestial body, with accurate time and an almanac, gives a line of position; two or three bodies give a fix to about 1–2 nmi (2–4 km) at sea with practised observers, limited by the instrument (1′ ≈ 1 nmi), refraction near the horizon, and the horizon itself. Automated **stellar-inertial** systems (star trackers aiding an INS) are used in aircraft and missiles and give heading and position fixes independent of radio at accuracies of hundreds of metres; spacecraft attitude is almost entirely star-tracker based. For elevation work celestial navigation positions nothing to a useful accuracy; its relevance is as the backup that keeps a vessel navigating safely when GNSS is jammed (the US Naval Academy restored celestial navigation to its curriculum in 2015 for this reason) and as a reminder, from [Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md), that the oldest layer of charted positions was fixed this way.

## 14.7 Barometric altimetry

A barometer measures pressure, and pressure decreases with height; with a model atmosphere this gives an altitude. Aircraft, drones, phones, hikers' watches, and many legacy survey instruments use it, and each gets a different answer from the same sensor because each uses a different reference.

**The references.** Aviation altimeters convert pressure to altitude using the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA: 1013.25 hPa and 15 °C at sea level, lapse rate 6.5 K/km) and a settable reference. **QNH** is the pressure that makes the altimeter read the airfield's elevation above mean sea level on the ground — so the altimeter reads altitude above MSL (approximately, assuming ISA temperature) near that airfield; **QFE** is the actual pressure at the airfield, so the altimeter reads height above the airfield; the **standard setting** 1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg) is used above the transition altitude so that all aircraft share the same wrong but consistent **pressure altitude** (flight levels). A drone's "altitude" is almost always a *barometric height above its take-off point*, obtained by zeroing the barometer at arming — equivalent to QFE at the launch site — and its "AGL" display is that same number, which equals height above ground only directly above the take-off point on flat terrain. The regulatory 400 ft (120 m) AGL limit (FAA Part 107; EASA open category) refers to height above the terrain *beneath the aircraft*, which a barometer cannot know; flight-planning software that offers "terrain following" does it by reading a DEM — typically SRTM or a national 10–30 m product — and adjusting the barometric target altitude as the drone moves, so that the drone's clearance above the real ground is the DEM's vertical error (several metres to tens of metres in mountains, plus whatever the trees add if the DEM is a DTM) combined with the barometer's drift. This is the pitfall in the TOC: a mission flown "at 400 ft AGL" on SRTM over a forested ridge may be at 60 m above the canopy on one side and 150 m on the other, with consequences for ground sample distance, overlap, and, occasionally, for the drone.

**Sensitivity and weather.** The hydrostatic relation (Mathematics) gives about 8.3 m per hPa near sea level, more at altitude (about 11 m/hPa at 3 000 m). Weather systems change surface pressure by 1–3 hPa per hour during frontal passages and by 20–40 hPa between a deep low and a strong high; a barometric altitude zeroed at the start of a survey day and not corrected drifts by whatever the synoptic pressure does — commonly 10–30 m over a day, occasionally more — and temperature departures from ISA scale the altitude by about 0.35 % per kelvin of mean-column temperature difference (a column 10 K warmer than ISA makes a 1 000 m indicated height about 3.5 % too low). Local effects — wind over a ridge, a building's pressurization, the pressure field around a moving vehicle or a multirotor's propwash — add metres. Sensor resolution is excellent (consumer MEMS barometers resolve 0.01–0.1 hPa, i.e., 0.1–1 m, and are repeatable to about 0.1 m over minutes), so **barometric height differences over short times are good and absolute barometric heights are not**: relative to a reference barometer logging at a known height nearby and corrected for temperature, differential barometric levelling reaches 1–3 m; alone, over hours, expect 10 m or worse.

**Phones** fuse barometric height with GNSS height and sometimes with a pressure-reference service, and report "altitude" with no indication of which; the barometric part gives floor-level resolution indoors (3–4 m per storey) and is the basis of emergency-call vertical location (the FCC's 3 m 80 % requirement for z-axis location in the United States, phased in from 2021). Crowdsourced elevation from phones is largely barometric–GNSS blended and should be treated as having 5–15 m vertical uncertainty with weather-correlated bias ([Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md)).

> **Rule of thumb.** Barometric altitude: 1 hPa ≈ 8 m near sea level. A barometer zeroed at take-off is good to about ±1 m for 10 minutes, ±3 m for an hour, and ±10 m (or the day's pressure trend × 8 m/hPa, whichever is larger) over a survey day, unless corrected against a reference station. It never measures height above the ground beneath you; only a DEM, a rangefinder, or a radar altimeter does that.

## 14.8 Signals of opportunity and GNSS reflectometry

**Signals of opportunity (SoOP)** positioning uses transmissions not intended for navigation — broadcast TV and radio, cellular downlinks, Iridium and Starlink downlinks, even GNSS signals reflected from surfaces — by measuring their Doppler or time of arrival against known transmitter positions. Research systems have demonstrated tens-of-metres positioning from Starlink Doppler and metres from cellular signals; operationally these are GNSS-denied fallbacks for navigation, not sources of survey positions.

**GNSS reflectometry (GNSS-R)** inverts the problem: the GNSS signal reflected from the Earth's surface, received by a satellite or aircraft alongside the direct signal, carries information about the reflecting surface — its roughness (wind speed over oceans), its dielectric properties (soil moisture, flooding), and its *height* (the delay between direct and reflected paths is the path difference, which depends on the receiver height above the reflecting surface). NASA's **CYGNSS** constellation (eight microsatellites launched 2016) was designed for ocean winds in tropical cyclones and has produced soil-moisture and inundation products; **GNSS-R altimetry** — using the code or carrier delay of the reflected signal to measure the sea-surface or ice-sheet height — has been demonstrated from aircraft and from TechDemoSat-1 and CYGNSS at decimetre-to-metre precision over kilometre footprints, far coarser than radar altimetry ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)) but with dense, cheap sampling. **Ground-based GNSS interferometric reflectometry (GNSS-IR)** uses the multipath oscillations in the signal-to-noise ratio of an ordinary geodetic receiver near a coast or lake to measure the water level below the antenna to a few centimetres, turning CORS stations into tide gauges (Larson et al. 2013); it also measures snow depth and soil moisture. GNSS-IR is a genuinely useful by-product of existing infrastructure for vertical-datum work ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)) and vertical land motion ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)) — the one case in this chapter where the "beyond GNSS" method makes a GNSS station into an elevation sensor.

## Then & now

- **Underwater.** Lead line positioned by sextant angles (to the 1930s) → surface radio positioning of the sounding vessel (Decca, LORAN, Raydist, 1940s–1980s) → first LBL arrays for deep-submergence vehicles and offshore oil (1960s–1970s) → USBL on survey ships (1980s) → INS + DVL dead reckoning with USBL/LBL aiding on AUVs (2000s) → terrain-relative navigation against prior bathymetry and inverted USBL on the vehicle (2010s–2020s). Absolute accuracy of deep-water positions went from kilometres to metres.
- **Terrain-aided.** TERCOM on cruise missiles (1970s–1980s, Golden 1980) → submarine bathymetric navigation → AUV TRN in research (2000s) and operations (2010s) → Mars 2020 Lander Vision System (2021) → visual terrain matching as a drone and aircraft GNSS backup (2020s).
- **Radio.** Gee/LORAN/Decca ⟨H⟩ ([Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md)) → shutdowns (Omega 1997, Decca 2000, US LORAN-C 2010) → eLoran revival (South Korea 2021; others studying) as a GNSS-independent backup; WiFi positioning from wardriving databases (2000 ⟨H⟩) → BLE beacons (2013) → UWB in phones (2019) → 5G positioning in 3GPP Release 16–18 (2020–2024).
- **Barometric.** Aneroid barometers for mountain heights in the nineteenth century (hundreds of metres of error from weather, partially corrected by simultaneous base readings) → aviation altimetry standardized on ISA (1920s–1950s) → MEMS barometers in every phone and drone (2010s), with the same physics and the same weather-driven errors.
- **Terrestrial.** Theodolite and chain → EDM (1957 ⟨H⟩) → total station (1970s) → robotic total station and laser tracker (1990s) → total stations tracking drones and scanners in GNSS-denied sites (2010s).
- **Celestial.** Primary method for 300 years → removed from naval curricula (US Naval Academy, 1998) → restored (2015) as GNSS-denial insurance; automated star trackers never left spacecraft.

## Mathematics

**USBL range/bearing error propagation.** A USBL fix in the transceiver frame is $\mathbf{p} = r\,(\cos\beta\sin\alpha,\ \cos\beta\cos\alpha,\ -\sin\beta)^\mathsf{T}$ with slant range $r$, bearing $\alpha$, and depression angle $\beta$. To first order the position covariance is $J\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_r^2, \sigma_\alpha^2, \sigma_\beta^2)\,J^\mathsf{T}$ with the Jacobian $J = \partial\mathbf{p}/\partial(r,\alpha,\beta)$; the angular terms contribute $r\,\sigma_\alpha\cos\beta$ (horizontal, perpendicular to bearing) and $r\,\sigma_\beta$ (in the vertical plane). Rotating into the mapping frame through the vessel attitude $R^m_v$ adds the attitude covariance: a heading error $\sigma_\psi$ contributes $r\cos\beta\,\sigma_\psi$ horizontally, which for $r = 2000$ m, $\beta = 45°$, $\sigma_\psi = 0.05° = 8.7\times10^{-4}$ rad is 1.2 m. The vertical from USBL is $-r\sin\beta$ with uncertainty $\sqrt{(\sigma_r\sin\beta)^2 + (r\sigma_\beta\cos\beta)^2}$; a pressure sensor on the vehicle usually beats it and should be used as a constraint in the solution.

**Sound-speed error.** For a harmonic-mean sound speed $\bar c$ with error $\delta c$, the range error is $\delta r = r\,\delta c/\bar c$; for an SVP with a surface layer of speed $c_1$ and a deep layer $c_2$, Snell's law $\sin\theta_1/c_1 = \sin\theta_2/c_2$ bends the ray, and the horizontal position error from ignoring refraction grows with $\tan\theta$ — several metres at 60° incidence over 1 000 m depth for a 20 m/s layer contrast (compute by ray tracing for the actual profile; [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)).

**Barometric formula and sensitivity.** Hydrostatic equilibrium with the ideal gas law gives $dp/dz = -\rho g = -\dfrac{p\,g}{R_d T}$. With a constant lapse rate $L$ ($T = T_0 - Lz$),

$$z = \frac{T_0}{L}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)^{\frac{R_d L}{g}}\right],$$

with $R_d = 287.05$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, $g = 9.80665$ m s⁻², $L = 0.0065$ K/m, $T_0 = 288.15$ K, $p_0 = 1013.25$ hPa for ISA, so $R_d L/g = 0.1903$. Sensitivity to pressure: $\partial z/\partial p = -\dfrac{R_d T}{g\,p}$, which at $T = 288$ K and $p = 1013$ hPa is $-8.3$ m/hPa, and at $p = 700$ hPa (about 3 000 m) with $T = 269$ K is $-11.3$ m/hPa. Sensitivity to the reference pressure $p_0$ is the same magnitude with opposite sign: a 1 hPa error in QNH (or a 1 hPa synoptic change since zeroing) is 8 m. Sensitivity to temperature: for a height difference $\Delta z$ above the reference, the true difference is $\Delta z_{true} \approx \Delta z_{ISA}\,\bar T/\bar T_{ISA}$, i.e., about 0.35 % per kelvin of mean-column temperature departure — 3.5 m per 1 000 m per 10 K.

**Terrain-matching likelihood.** Given a prior position distribution and a map $m(x,y)$, a set of measured terrain heights $z_i$ at sensor-relative offsets $\mathbf{d}_i$ from the vehicle position $\mathbf{x}$ (after removing the vehicle's own altitude or depth), the measurement likelihood under Gaussian noise $\sigma$ is

$$p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i\big(z_i - m(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{d}_i) - b\big)^2\right],$$

where $b$ is an unknown vertical offset (a bias between the sensor's vertical reference and the map's datum, estimated or marginalized). A particle or point-mass filter evaluates this over candidate $\mathbf{x}$ and combines it with the dead-reckoning prior. The posterior's sharpness is governed by the terrain gradient: for a profile with RMS slope $s$ and $n$ independent samples, the Cramér–Rao lower bound on horizontal position is roughly $\sigma_x \gtrsim \sigma/(s\sqrt{n})$ — with $\sigma = 0.5$ m, $s = 0.05$ (3° RMS slope), $n = 100$: $\sigma_x \gtrsim 1$ m, but with $s = 0.005$ (flat shelf) it is 10 m, and the map's own errors $\sigma_m$ add in quadrature to $\sigma$ (and, if correlated over the match window, do not average down at all). The vertical offset $b$ is where the datum mismatch between vehicle and map lives; if the map is in a different vertical datum than the vehicle's depth sensor, $b$ absorbs it — which is why a TRN system can navigate perfectly on a map that is metres wrong in the vertical, and why its "depth" output must not be trusted as a datum-tied height.

## Validation & uncertainty

Each method in this chapter fails in a characteristic way, and testing targets the failure.

**Acoustic systems.** Errors are (a) sound-speed and refraction biases, proportional to range and worse at low depression angles; (b) alignment and lever-arm errors, heading-dependent; (c) MRU attitude errors, range-proportional; (d) timing and latency, velocity-proportional; (e) outliers from multipath, noise, and false detections. Test by: box-in calibration with residual statistics; repeat fixes on a static seabed transponder from different headings and ranges (the spread is the precision, the heading-dependence is the alignment); comparison of USBL fixes against the vehicle's INS+DVL dead reckoning (innovations should be white and within the stated percentage of range); comparison of the vehicle's bathymetry with a surface multibeam survey over the same ground (the ultimate test; a horizontal offset appears as a slope-dependent depth difference, [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)). Report: system and calibration date, SVP source and age, percentage-of-range precision from repeat fixes, and the resulting horizontal uncertainty of the vehicle's data as a function of range and depth.

**Terrain-aided navigation.** Errors are the map's (internal geometry, absolute registration, datum, resolution) plus the matcher's (false peaks in self-similar terrain, divergence on flat ground). Test by: holding out a portion of the map and checking that fixes against it agree with fixes against the rest; running the filter on simulated profiles over the actual map with known offsets; checking the posterior width against the terrain roughness; and tying the vehicle's data to independent control where any exists. Report the map's identity, version, datum, and stated accuracy as part of the vehicle's positioning lineage; TRN-positioned data inherit the map's absolute uncertainty and must not be used to "improve" the same map without acknowledging the circularity.

**Radio and indoor.** Errors are non-line-of-sight biases (positive, metres), anchor-survey errors, synchronization, and geometry (VDOP-like vertical weakness when anchors are coplanar). Test by walking a tag along a total-station-surveyed path and computing error statistics per zone; place at least one anchor at a very different height for 3D work.

**Barometric.** Errors are synoptic drift (8 m/hPa), temperature scaling (0.35 %/K), local pressure effects, and the reference-surface confusion (QNH/QFE/take-off point/DEM). Test by logging a reference barometer at a known height and differencing; by comparing the drone's barometric altitude record against its PPK GNSS height ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) — the difference over a flight shows both drift and the launch-point offset; and by reading the flight log to see which DEM, if any, terrain following used. Report the reference used and the correction applied; never record a barometric altitude in a dataset as if it were a geodetic height.

**Terrestrial instruments.** Errors are the control's own error, instrument and target heights, refraction on long low lines, and traverse propagation. Test by closure (traverse misclosure against the specification; levelling loop closure in mm/√km) and by redundant ties.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Order-of-magnitude absolute horizontal uncertainty (1σ) of positioning methods in this chapter, with the dominant error and how it grows.
>
> | Method | Typical σ (horizontal) | Grows with | Dominant error |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | LBL within calibrated array (deep water) | 1–5 m absolute; 0.1–1 m repeatable | — (bounded in array) | Array calibration, sound speed |
> | USBL | 0.05–0.3 % of slant range (1.5–9 m at 3 km) | Range | MRU heading, sound speed, alignment |
> | INS + DVL between fixes | 0.05–1 % of distance | Distance | Heading, DVL scale |
> | TRN over good relief | 1–3 map cells + map's absolute error | Map quality | Map registration, flat terrain |
> | Visual-inertial odometry | 0.5–2 % of distance | Distance | Texture, lighting, dynamics |
> | eLoran with ASF corrections | 8–20 m (95 %) coastal | Distance from monitors | ASF, geometry |
> | UWB with surveyed anchors | 0.1–0.3 m (3D worse) | NLOS | Blockage, anchor geometry |
> | WiFi / BLE / cellular | 2–10 m / 1–5 m / 50 m–1 km | — | Database quality, NLOS |
> | Total station from control, ≤ 500 m | 2–10 mm | Range, setups | Control, refraction (vertical) |
> | Celestial (sextant) | 1–2 nmi | — | Instrument, horizon |
> | Barometric altitude (vertical) | ±1 m (10 min) to ±10–30 m (day), uncorrected | Time (weather) | Synoptic pressure, temperature |

**What to report.** The positioning method per segment of data (vehicles routinely switch between GNSS, USBL-aided INS, pure dead reckoning, and TRN within one mission); the calibration state and reference data (SVP, map, anchors) each depended on; the resulting horizontal and vertical uncertainty as a function of time or range; and the vertical reference of any height reported (ellipsoid, pressure-derived depth with its density model, barometric with its zero, or TRN-relative to a map datum). The last item is the one most often missing and the one that turns a positioning uncertainty into a DEM blunder.

> **Try it.** Quantify how much the weather moves a barometric altitude, and how a temperature departure from ISA scales a height difference.
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np
>
> Rd, g, L, T0, p0 = 287.05, 9.80665, 0.0065, 288.15, 1013.25
>
> def isa_alt(p, p_ref=p0):
>     """ISA altitude (m) for pressure p (hPa) relative to reference pressure p_ref."""
>     return (T0 / L) * (1 - (p / p_ref) ** (Rd * L / g))
>
> # Drone zeroed at launch when surface pressure was 1012.0 hPa; a front passes and it falls to 1006.5 hPa.
> p_launch, p_later = 1012.0, 1006.5
> p_drone = p_launch * (1 - L * 120 / T0) ** (g / (Rd * L))   # pressure at a true 120 m above launch, launch conditions
> print("indicated height at launch conditions :", round(isa_alt(p_drone, p_launch), 1), "m")
> # Same true height later: the drone's sensor sees the lower ambient column
> p_drone_later = p_later * (1 - L * 120 / T0) ** (g / (Rd * L))
> print("indicated height after 5.5 hPa drop   :", round(isa_alt(p_drone_later, p_launch), 1), "m")
> # Temperature: column 12 K warmer than ISA scales true height by ~T/T_ISA
> print("true height when indicated 120 m, +12 K:", round(120 * (T0 + 12) / T0, 1), "m")
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: the indicated height after the 5.5 hPa pressure fall is about 165 m for a true 120 m — a 45 m error (≈ 8.3 m/hPa) that a drone holding "120 m" would correct by *descending* 45 m — and a +12 K warm column makes a true height of 125 m read as 120 m. Both are routine conditions, not edge cases.

## Pitfalls

- **Drone missions flown at "400 ft AGL" on SRTM in mountainous terrain** → the planner follows a 30 m DEM with metre-to-tens-of-metres vertical error and no canopy, while the drone holds barometric height relative to launch → true clearance varies widely, GSD and overlap vary, and ridges with trees get close; use a better DEM (national lidar DTM/DSM), add margin, and check PPK height against the DEM after the flight.
- **USBL without a sound-speed profile** → ranges and beamformed angles biased by percent-level sound-speed error → take an SVP at the site each day, enter the surface sound speed at the head, and verify with a seabed transponder.
- **Barometric drift over a long survey day** → synoptic pressure change of a few hPa moves the zero by tens of metres → log a reference barometer or use GNSS height; never splice morning and afternoon barometric heights without correction.
- **Confusing QNH, QFE, pressure altitude, and launch-relative height** → each is "altitude" in some display → state the reference in every record; convert explicitly.
- **Treating TRN output as absolute** → the fix is relative to the map; the map's datum and registration error are inherited silently → record the map's identity and uncertainty; do not feed TRN-positioned data back into the same map without flagging the circularity.
- **Box-in on a calm day only** → alignment errors coupled to roll and pitch are invisible in flat water → calibrate in representative conditions and check heading-dependence of residuals.
- **MRU heading inadequate for the USBL range** → 0.1° heading at 3 km is 5 m; a navigation-grade heading source is required for deep work → budget heading explicitly and use dual-antenna GNSS or gyrocompassing.
- **Indoor UWB anchors all at ceiling height** → vertical geometry degenerate; z errors of a metre with decimetre horizontal → put anchors at different heights; verify against a total station.
- **Total-station traverse from a scaled or mis-coordinated control point** → everything is precise and wrong → check the starting control against a second point before the traverse; close on independent control.
- **Reflectorless total-station heights on vegetation or edges** → the beam returns from a leaf or a corner at a different range → use a prism for checkpoints; inspect reflectorless shots.
- **Celestial or eLoran positions quoted with GNSS-era precision** → a position to 0.001′ from a 1 nmi method → report with the method's uncertainty.
- **Phone "altitude" taken as orthometric** → it is barometric, GNSS-ellipsoidal, or a blend with an unknown geoid → treat as 5–15 m uncertain and weather-biased.

## Key takeaways

- Without GNSS, position error either grows with time or distance (dead reckoning, barometer), with range (acoustic, radio), or inherits the map (terrain-aided, visual map matching); plan the survey around which growth law applies and budget it explicitly.
- Acoustic positioning is a sound-speed measurement in disguise: no SVP, no accuracy; and USBL accuracy is a percentage of range that depends on the vessel's heading sensor as much as on the acoustic head.
- Terrain-relative navigation turns the DEM into the positioning system — a powerful idea that makes the DEM's internal and absolute errors, and its datum, part of the vehicle's position lineage.
- Barometric altitude is a pressure measurement with an 8 m/hPa sensitivity to weather and a 0.35 %/K sensitivity to temperature; it is excellent for short-term height *differences* and useless as an absolute height without a reference; a drone's "AGL" is height above its launch point unless a DEM was consulted, and then it is as good as that DEM.
- Total stations, traverses, and levelling remain the most accurate positioning tools at short range and the way checkpoints get under canopy and indoors — but they are tied to the datum only through the control they start from.
- Radio methods (eLoran, UWB, WiFi/BLE, 5G) give metres-to-decimetres and, more importantly, independence from GNSS; their vertical component is weak for the same geometric reason as GNSS's.
- GNSS reflectometry inverts the positioning problem to measure surfaces; ground-based GNSS-IR turns a CORS station into a tide gauge or snow sensor at centimetre level.
- Always record which positioning method, calibration, and reference (SVP, map, anchors, barometric zero) produced each segment of data; the vertical reference is the item most often omitted and most often fatal.
