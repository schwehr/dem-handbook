# Chapter 32 — DSM → DTM: removing objects, and the definitions problem

> **Part VII — From sensor data to products.** The fourth processing chapter: what "terrain" means once the sensor has recorded everything above it, how buildings, bridges, vegetation, and infrastructure are removed, and what is genuinely unknowable afterwards.

**In this chapter.** A digital terrain model is a digital surface model plus a list of decisions: which objects are not terrain, what to put where they were, and how to say so. You will be able to recognise the objects whose status is ambiguous—sheds, greenhouses, stadium roofs, ruins, roads, bridges, culverts, causeways, dams, piers, retaining walls, tanks, pipelines, conveyors, solar arrays, turbines, vehicles, crops, stockpiles, landfills, snow—and compare how the USGS Lidar Base Specification, the Copernicus DEM editing rules, national lidar specifications, and CityGML's levels of detail treat them. You will understand the single-valued limit of a 2.5D grid at overpasses, arcades, and tunnels, and when voxels, meshes, or CityGML are required. You will be able to remove objects by classification-and-interpolation, by morphology, and by learned DSM→DTM models; quantify the bias and uncertainty each leaves under large footprints, dense canopy, and marsh; apply vegetation-bias corrections; read the "surface definition" of every major public DEM; and propagate both definitions into a normalised DSM for building and canopy heights.

## 32.1 Definitions that bite

[Chapter 4](ch04-names-and-definitions.md) established that DSM, DTM, and DEM are not standardised terms (Guth et al. 2021 surveyed the usage and found no consensus even on whether "DEM" means bare earth). This section is the object-by-object inventory of why a bare-earth definition cannot be written in a sentence. For each class of object the question is the same: is it terrain, and if not, what is the terrain beneath it?

**Buildings** seem the easy case—a structure with a roof and walls is not terrain—until the edges are examined. A garden shed, carport, or greenhouse is a building to a lidar classifier (a planar roof above ground) but is often below the minimum footprint area that a specification requires to be mapped. A tent, a market canopy, or a temporary stadium roof is a building for a week. A building under construction is a slab, then a frame, then a shell; its DTM is the excavated pit, the slab, or the surrounding grade depending on the day. A ruin with no roof is walls on terrain. A building whose ground floor is on stilts (pilotis) or over water has terrain, or water, beneath it. A green roof is vegetation on a building. The USGS LBS defines buildings for classification as "man-made structures" with roofs, removes them from the bare earth, and interpolates beneath; it does not and cannot say what the ground under a 200 m warehouse is.

**Roads** are terrain in nearly every specification: a paved surface laid on the ground is ground. But the road *corridor* contains kerbs, medians, retaining walls, sound barriers, and gantries, and a road on an embankment or in a cutting is engineered terrain—kept, because it is terrain now. The difficulties are **elevated roads** (next paragraph) and **multi-level roads** (Section 32.3).

**Bridges, culverts, and causeways** are where specifications diverge most. A *bridge* is a structure that carries a road, rail, or path over a gap with air (or water) beneath; the USGS LBS removes bridge decks from the bare earth (LAS class 17) and interpolates the gap, so that water and terrain pass beneath. A *culvert* is a pipe or box through an embankment; the embankment is terrain and the LBS keeps it, so the DTM shows a dam across the stream unless **hydro-enforcement** cuts it (the LBS specifies hydro-flattening as the default and hydro-enforcement only when contracted; [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). A *causeway* is a road on fill across water or marsh—terrain by the embankment rule, barrier by the hydrologic one. The boundary between a short bridge and a large culvert is a matter of structural engineering, not of elevation data, and specifications resolve it by span length or by the presence of a visible gap in the point cloud.

**Dams, levees, and dikes** are kept as terrain in the LBS and most national specifications: permanent engineered landforms. A **weir**, **lock gate**, or **flood barrier** is a structure on a dam and is usually removed. **Piers, docks, jetties, and wharves** are removed where they stand on piles over water (the terrain beneath is the seabed, which lidar did not see) and kept where they are solid fill; a specification that does not distinguish the two leaves the operator to guess from the point cloud.

**Retaining walls** are the hardest small case: the terrain is at one level above the wall and another below, and the wall face is a vertical surface that a 2.5D grid cannot represent. Both levels are terrain; the wall is the breakline between them. Removing the wall as a "building" and interpolating produces a smooth ramp that exists nowhere. **Tanks and silos** are removed. **Elevated pipelines, conveyors, aqueducts, and flumes** are removed as structures, but a pipeline buried in a visible berm is a berm. **Solar arrays** are removed as structures in most specifications, but a utility-scale array covers square kilometres with panels 1–3 m above a graded surface; the terrain under it is interpolated from the rows between panels, or from the pre-construction grade, and the choice changes volume and drainage computations. **Antennas, masts, and wind turbines** are removed ([Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)); **vehicles**, aircraft, and ships are removed as transient ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)).

**Vegetation and crops** are not terrain, but the ground under them is sampled only where the laser penetrates. Section 32.6 treats the bias. **Stockpiles and landfills** are the inverse problem: they are terrain-shaped, made of earth or waste, and were there on the day. Most specifications treat them as terrain as of the acquisition date (check the current LBS wording for stockpiles, which has varied between editions), which is right for a mine's volume survey and wrong for a flood model that wants the permanent landform. **Snow and ice** are terrain for a glaciologist and a seasonal contaminant for a surveyor; the LBS requires snow-free acquisition and LAS 1.4 R14 added class 21 for the cases where it cannot be avoided ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md)).

> **Definitions that bite.** "Bare earth" is a project-specific predicate, not a physical surface. Two correct DTMs of the same city, one made to the USGS LBS and one to a specification that keeps bridges (as some national road authorities require for clearance work), differ by the height of every bridge over every river, and a flood model that uses one for the terrain and a DSM made from the other's point cloud for the buildings will have a bridge deck in one layer and open water in the other at the same location. Read the specification before reading the grid.

## 32.2 Specification examples

Specifications resolve the ambiguities of Section 32.1 by rule, and the rules differ. Four families are instructive.

**USGS Lidar Base Specification.** The LBS (online edition, revised 2024) defines bare earth for 3DEP deliverables through its classification requirements and its DEM rules: buildings, bridges, tanks, and other structures are classified out of ground and the DEM is interpolated beneath them; bridge decks are class 17 and removed; dams, levees, and road embankments are ground; culverts are not cut (the road over them remains) unless hydro-enforcement is specified; water bodies are hydro-flattened to a constant (lakes) or monotonic (rivers of ≥ 30 m nominal width) elevation from breaklines; stockpiles are ground; and the classification report documents exceptions. The LBS does not require an "interpolated beneath structure" mask as a deliverable, so the user who needs to know where the DTM is interpolated must compute it from the classified point cloud and building footprints.

**Copernicus DEM.** The Copernicus DEM (GLO-30, GLO-90, EEA-10) is a DSM derived from TanDEM-X and edited by Airbus to a published rule set (Copernicus DEM Product Handbook). The editing flattens water bodies, sets rivers to monotonic flow, removes spikes and voids, and applies **terrain editing** in specific cases, but it does not remove buildings or vegetation—it is a DSM, and it says so. What distinguishes it is the set of auxiliary mask layers delivered with every tile: the **Editing Mask (EDM)**, which records cell by cell what kind of edit was applied (e.g. water body, river, shoreline, airport, infilled void); the **Filling Mask (FLM)**, which records the source of void fill (which auxiliary DEM, or interpolation); the **Height Error Mask (HEM)**, the per-cell height error estimate from the InSAR processing; and the **Water Body Mask (WBM)**. These layers are the model for what every edited DEM should ship: the user can see what was measured, what was edited, and what was filled, and by what.

**National lidar DTM specifications.** The Netherlands' AHN (AHN3 and AHN4) delivers a 0.5 m DTM interpolated from ground points, with a nodata mask where no ground points exist (under buildings and water) rather than an interpolated fill—so the AHN DTM has holes where the LBS DTM has smooth surface, which is a different and arguably more honest convention. Switzerland's swissALTI3D interpolates under buildings and publishes an accuracy layer; the UK Environment Agency's LIDAR Composite DTM removes buildings and vegetation and documents the method per tile; Denmark's DHM and Finland's NLS DTM follow similar patterns. The common thread is that each national product has a written rule set and that the rule sets differ in exactly the cases of Section 32.1.

**CityGML levels of detail.** CityGML (OGC; version 3.0 conceptual model 2021) defines a Relief module with TIN, raster, mass-point, and breakline representations of terrain and a Building module whose **LoD0–LoD3** (LoD4 was folded into the others in 3.0) specify how much of a building's geometry is modelled, from footprint to detailed façade and roof structure. Biljecki, Ledoux & Stoter (2016) refined the LoD specification into sixteen sub-levels. The relevance here is that a city model separates terrain from buildings explicitly, with a `TerrainIntersectionCurve` that defines where each building meets the terrain, which is precisely the information a DTM interpolated under a footprint lacks ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

**Modelled "DTMs".** A growing class of global products presents a bare-earth surface that was never measured as such. **MERIT DEM** (Yamazaki et al. 2017) removed stripe noise, speckle, absolute bias, and a modelled tree-height bias from SRTM and AW3D; **FABDEM** (Hawker et al. 2022) removed buildings and forests from Copernicus GLO-30 with a machine-learning model trained on reference lidar; **DeltaDTM** (Pronk et al. 2024) built a coastal DTM from Copernicus DEM corrected with ICESat-2 and GEDI ground returns and a land-cover-dependent bias model. These products are valuable and widely used, but they are **models of the terrain**, with errors that depend on the training distribution, and the honest name is "modelled bare earth." Each publishes an accuracy assessment; none can report a per-cell uncertainty that reflects what the model did not know. Treat them as [Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md) treats all public products: with their stated accuracy as a global number and local validation as mandatory.

## 32.3 Roads crossing roads and roads under buildings: the 2.5D limit

A grid stores one elevation per $(x, y)$ cell. Wherever the real world has more than one surface at the same horizontal position—an overpass above a road, an arcade or portico above a pavement, a building cantilevered over a street, a tunnel under a hill, a multi-storey interchange, a cliff overhang—the grid must choose one, and a 2.5D DTM cannot represent the stack ([Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md)).

At an **overpass**, the DSM shows the upper deck and the DTM shows—by the bridge rule—the lower road, interpolated across the deck's footprint from the lower road's points on either side. If the deck was classified as ground, the lower road vanishes and a routing model sees a hill where there is a passage. At an **arcade** the DTM shows the pavement only if the laser saw under it (typically not, from the air). **Tunnels** do not exist in a DTM: the surface above them is terrain. **Multi-level interchanges** with three or four stacked decks defeat classification; production practice labels the top deck as bridge, the ground-level road as ground, and leaves intermediate decks as class 1 or 17.

The alternatives are to leave 2.5D. A **voxel** model represents stacked surfaces natively at a storage cost that grows with vertical extent. A **mesh** (a 3D TIN) represents overhangs and multi-valued surfaces exactly and is the standard output of photogrammetric city reconstruction. **CityGML** and other semantic city models represent terrain, buildings, bridges, tunnels, and transportation as separate objects with explicit relationships—the only representation in which "the road under the bridge" is a queryable fact. **Multi-layer rasters**—a DTM plus per-object bridge and tunnel-floor layers—are a pragmatic middle ground used by several road authorities. The decision rule is simple: if the application needs to know what is *under* something, a 2.5D DTM is the wrong product, and no amount of care in its construction will fix that ([Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md)).

## 32.4 Removal methods

Three families of method turn a DSM (or an all-return point cloud) into a DTM, and they differ in what they know about the objects they remove.

**Classification, then interpolation.** The production standard for lidar ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)): label the points, keep class 2, and interpolate ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)). Under a building there are no ground points, so the DTM there is whatever the interpolator does across the footprint—TIN-linear gives a planar patch between the surrounding ground, a spline gives a smooth bulge or dip, IDW gives a flattened average. The honest statement is that **the ground under a building is unknowable from airborne data**: the laser never saw it. The DTM there is a hypothesis of continuity, and the product should carry a mask of the footprints within which it is a hypothesis. For dense urban cores where buildings cover 60–80 % of the surface, most of the DTM is that hypothesis. The same holds under water (hydro-flattening is the explicit version), under dense evergreen canopy, and under any opaque surface.

**Morphological approaches.** Where only a DSM exists (photogrammetry, InSAR, satellite stereo), objects can be removed by grey-scale morphology on the raster: an opening with a structuring element larger than the largest object removes everything narrower than the element and leaves the terrain; the progressive-window logic of PMF and SMRF ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md), Mathematics) applies directly to grids. WhiteboxTools' `RemoveOffTerrainObjects` and SAGA's DTM filter (Vosselman-style slope filter on a grid) are the common implementations. Morphology knows nothing about what it removes—a hill narrower than the window is removed as a building; a building wider than the window is kept as a hill—and it cannot distinguish a bridge from an embankment. Its output is a smoothed lower envelope, biased low on crests and leaving stumps at the bases of large objects where the window could not reach.

**Learned DSM→DTM.** Convolutional networks trained on paired DSM/DTM tiles learn to predict the terrain under objects from context: the surrounding terrain, the object's shape, and the statistical regularities of the training set (Gevaert et al. 2018 trained on rule-based labels from DSM morphology; FABDEM used random-forest regression on Copernicus DEM with land-cover, canopy-height, and building-footprint predictors; many U-Net variants have followed). These methods remove buildings and forests from satellite DSMs at scale and produce plausible terrain where morphology produces stumps. Their output under an object is still a hypothesis, now informed by what terrain under similar objects looked like in the training set; it is better on average and no more *known*. Their errors are spatially structured (systematically wrong in terrain types or urban forms under-represented in training) and must be validated per land-cover class in the target region ([Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md), [Chapter 45](ch45-super-resolution.md)).

> **Try it.** Build a DTM and an interpolation-footprint mask from a classified LAZ tile, so that the deliverable says where the terrain was measured. Expected outcome: `dtm.tif` (1 m), `ground_count.tif` (ground returns per cell), and `interp_mask.tif` (1 where the DTM cell had no ground return within 1.5 m and was interpolated), plus the fraction of interpolated cells printed.
>
> ```bash
> # Ground-only DTM with IDW fallback, and a count raster from the same pipeline.
> pdal pipeline - <<'EOF'
> {"pipeline":[
>   "tile_classified.laz",
>   {"type":"filters.range","limits":"Classification[2:2]"},
>   {"type":"writers.gdal","filename":"dtm.tif","resolution":1.0,
>    "output_type":"idw","radius":1.5,"window_size":6,"nodata":-9999,"data_type":"float32"},
>   {"type":"writers.gdal","filename":"ground_count.tif","resolution":1.0,
>    "output_type":"count","radius":0.7071,"nodata":0,"data_type":"uint16"}
> ]}
> EOF
> # Cells that have a DTM value but no ground return within the cell.
> gdal_calc.py -A dtm.tif -B ground_count.tif --outfile=interp_mask.tif --type=Byte \
>   --calc="logical_and(A!=-9999, B==0)" --NoDataValue=255
> python3 -c "
> import rasterio, numpy as np
> m = rasterio.open('interp_mask.tif').read(1); d = rasterio.open('dtm.tif').read(1)
> print('interpolated fraction of valid DTM cells: %.1f %%' % (100*m[d!=-9999].mean()))"
> ```
>
> In an open rural tile the interpolated fraction is a few percent (hedgerow bases, water); in a dense urban tile it is 40–70 %. Deliver the mask with the DTM; it is the single most useful uncertainty layer a DTM can carry.

## 32.5 Errors and unknowns after removal

Removing an object leaves four kinds of error, and an uncertainty layer should distinguish them.

**Interpolation bias under large footprints.** The terrain under a footprint of width $w$ is estimated from the ground at its edge. If the true terrain has curvature $\kappa$ (second derivative) across the footprint, a linear interpolation across it has a maximum error of order $\kappa w^2 / 8$ at the centre (see Mathematics). For a 100 m warehouse on terrain with a gentle 1 m rise across it (curvature of order $10^{-4}$–$2 \times 10^{-4}$ m⁻¹), the central error is of order 0.1–0.25 m; for a 300 m terminal building on a hillside with a 10 m convex rise, it is metres. Where the site was graded flat for the building—the usual case—the "true" ground is the building's slab, which may be a metre or more above or below the surrounding grade, and the interpolation error is the grading depth, which no interpolator can know.

**Retained basements, foundations, pads, and plinths.** A classifier that removes the roof and walls may keep the raised slab, the loading dock, the surrounding pad, or the basement excavation (if the building was under construction), producing a plateau or pit of the pad's height at the footprint. These are correct terrain by the stockpile logic and wrong terrain by the "what was there before" logic; the specification decides, and the uncertainty layer should flag them.

**Smoothing of terraces, walls, and edges.** Interpolating across a retaining wall or between terraces replaces a step with a ramp; the maximum error is half the step height at the wall's position, and the slope raster is wrong across the whole ramp. Morphological removal does the same to levee crests and road embankments narrower than its window.

**False ground under dense canopy.** Where no laser returns reached the ground, the lowest returns—understorey, fallen logs, lower branches—are classified as ground and the DTM is biased high by their height above true ground; where some returns reached the ground, the sparse sample is interpolated across the gaps with the usual error. Section 32.6 gives the magnitudes.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical error (bias and 1σ) introduced by object removal in a 1 m lidar DTM at 8 pt/m², by situation; magnitudes are indicative ranges from the literature cited in this chapter and from routine QA, not guarantees.
>
> | Situation | Typical bias | Typical 1σ | Known from | Flag in uncertainty layer |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Open ground, no removal | 0 | 2–5 cm | Measurement | — |
> | Under small building (< 20 m) on flat site | ±5 cm | 5–10 cm | Edge interpolation | footprint mask |
> | Under large building (> 100 m) on sloping site | up to grading depth, 0.5–2 m | 0.3–1 m | Unknown | footprint mask, "unknown" class |
> | Retained pad / plinth | +0.2 to +1.5 m | — | Classification policy | pad flag |
> | Across retaining wall (ramped) | up to ½ wall height | — | Breakline absence | breakline-deficit flag |
> | Under closed deciduous canopy, leaf-off | +5 to +30 cm | 10–30 cm | Sparse ground + understorey | canopy-density class |
> | Under closed conifer canopy | +0.3 to > 1 m | 0.3–1 m | Very sparse ground | canopy-density class |
> | Marsh / tall grass platform | +10 to +50 cm | 10–20 cm | Vegetation bias | vegetation-correction flag |
> | Under solar array (interpolated between rows) | ±10–30 cm | 10–30 cm | Row-gap interpolation | structure mask |

Expressing this in an **uncertainty layer** means delivering, at minimum, a categorical raster of the removal situation (measured ground; interpolated under building, water, or canopy with density class; morphologically removed; modelled by ML) and, where an empirical model exists, a per-cell 1σ. The Copernicus EDM/FLM/HEM triplet and the AHN nodata convention are two existing ways; a per-cell "source and confidence" byte raster is the minimum that lets a downstream flood, volume, or canopy model treat interpolated cells differently from measured ones ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

## 32.6 Vegetation and "ground"

Vegetation is not an object that can be removed; it is a medium through which the ground is sampled with a success rate that depends on canopy density, leaf state, scan angle, pulse energy, and footprint. Where enough returns reach the ground, the DTM there is as good as anywhere; where few or none do, the DTM is the lowest thing the laser saw.

**Tall grass, crops, and reeds** are the pervasive case. A dense stand of grass or cereal 0.3–1 m tall returns most of the energy from the canopy top and some from within; the "lowest return" is somewhere inside the canopy, typically 5–30 cm above the soil for grass and more for crops, and a ground filter accepts it because nothing lower exists. The DTM is biased high by that amount over every field and verge, invisibly, and the bias varies with season and crop. Leaf-off, post-harvest, or grazed acquisition reduces it; the LBS requires leaf-off where practical. **Marsh platforms**—salt marsh, reed beds, mangrove floor—are the costliest instance because the applications (sea-level-rise exposure, inundation frequency, habitat zonation) are sensitive to decimetres and the bias is decimetres: Hladik & Alber (2012) measured lidar DTM errors in a Georgia salt marsh with a mean positive bias of order 0.1 m overall and larger, species-dependent biases (approximately 0.1–0.25 m) in dense *Juncus* and tall *Spartina*; Medeiros et al. (2015) and Buffington et al. (2016) found comparable biases in other marshes. **Forest floor under closed canopy** carries the biases of Section 32.5. **Hedges and shrubs** are removed by filters when they are tall enough and accepted as ground when they are not; the threshold is the filter's, not nature's.

**Correction models.** Because the bias is systematic by vegetation type and density, it can be modelled. Hladik & Alber (2012) applied species-specific offsets from a vegetation classification; Medeiros et al. (2015) regressed the bias on an aboveground-biomass proxy; Buffington et al. (2016) introduced **LEAN** (Lidar Elevation Adjustment with NDVI), a regression of the lidar–RTK difference on NDVI from co-registered multispectral imagery that reduced marsh DTM RMSE from roughly 0.2 m to under 0.1 m in their sites (approximate figures; see the papers for exact values); Rogers et al. (2018) used full-waveform features and nonparametric regression for point-by-point correction. All require RTK ground truth transects in the marsh for calibration ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)), all are local, and all should be delivered as an explicit correction layer rather than silently applied, so that the next user can decide whether to use it ([Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md), [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

> **Case file.** Marsh elevation errors propagate directly into sea-level-rise vulnerability. Marsh platforms typically sit within a few tens of centimetres of mean high water; a DTM biased +0.2 m places the platform higher in the tidal frame than it is, which understates inundation frequency and overstates resilience. Studies applying LEAN-style corrections in Pacific and Atlantic marshes (Buffington et al. 2016; Rogers et al. 2018) reported that uncorrected lidar misassigned a substantial fraction of marsh area to higher-elevation habitat classes, and that the corrected DTM changed projected habitat loss under sea-level-rise scenarios by amounts comparable to the differences among the scenarios themselves. The error was not in the lidar; it was in calling the lowest return "ground."

The "Bridges" column is the most overlooked and has the largest local consequence: a flood model built on Copernicus DEM or SRTM has a dam across every river at every bridge unless the user removes them. The "Vegetation" column is the largest in area: the DSM–DTM difference over forest is the canopy height, 10–40 m, and the modelled products' removal of it has residual errors that are largest where the forest is densest.

## 32.8 nDSM = DSM − DTM

The **normalised DSM** (Weidner & Förstner 1995) is the DSM minus the DTM: the height of everything above the terrain, from which building heights, canopy height models, and object detection follow ([Chapter 42](ch42-object-detection-semantics.md), [Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md), [Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md)). Both definitions propagate into it. If the DSM is a first-return maximum and the DTM a ground-class mean, the nDSM over a flat roof is the roof height plus the DSM's upward noise bias; over a tree it is the top-of-canopy height minus whatever the DTM's vegetation bias was; over a bridge it is the deck height above the interpolated gap if the DTM removed the bridge and zero if it did not. Error propagation for the difference of two independent surfaces gives $\sigma_{\text{nDSM}}^2 = \sigma_{\text{DSM}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{DTM}}^2$, and the biases subtract: a +0.2 m vegetation bias in the DTM becomes a −0.2 m bias in canopy height. Where the DSM and DTM derive from the same point cloud, their errors are correlated (trajectory and datum errors cancel), and the nDSM is more accurate than either; where they come from different sources or epochs (a Copernicus DSM minus a FABDEM DTM, or a 2020 DSM minus a 2012 DTM), registration, datum, and epoch differences enter in full and the nDSM inherits every half-cell shift and every geoid mismatch of [Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md) and [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md).

## Then & now

- **1990s.** Photogrammetric DSMs from automated image matching created the need to derive terrain from a surface model; Weidner & Förstner (1995) introduced the nDSM for building extraction, and the first morphological DSM filters appeared alongside the first lidar ground filters.
- **2000–2006.** SRTM (2000) gave the world a DSM and taught a generation of hydrologists that the "DEM" had trees and buildings in it; the SRTM "finishing" (Slater et al. 2006) defined the editing conventions (flat water, monotonic rivers, filled voids) that Copernicus later formalised with masks.
- **2010–2017.** TanDEM-X (2010–2015) produced a global X-band DSM; the USGS Lidar Base Specification (v1.0 2012; revised editions through v2.1; online edition thereafter) codified bare-earth rules for national lidar; MERIT DEM (2017) introduced the statistically modelled bare earth.
- **2018–2024.** Deep-learning DSM→DTM (Gevaert et al. 2018 onward); Copernicus DEM (2019 onward) shipped per-cell editing and filling masks; FABDEM (2022) and DeltaDTM (2024) extended modelled bare earth to global and coastal scales; CityGML 3.0 (2021) unified the semantic city model in which terrain and structures are distinct objects.
- **Now.** The open questions are not how to remove a building but how to say what is under it: per-cell provenance and uncertainty layers, and a shared vocabulary for surface definitions across products ([Chapter 73](ch73-open-problems.md)).

## Mathematics

**nDSM and its error.** $\text{nDSM}(x,y) = \text{DSM}(x,y) - \text{DTM}(x,y)$. With independent errors, $\sigma_{\text{nDSM}}^2 = \sigma_{\text{DSM}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{DTM}}^2$; with correlation $\rho$ (same point cloud, shared trajectory and datum), $\sigma_{\text{nDSM}}^2 = \sigma_{\text{DSM}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{DTM}}^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_{\text{DSM}}\sigma_{\text{DTM}}$, which for $\rho$ near 1 and similar σ is much smaller than either. Biases combine linearly: $b_{\text{nDSM}} = b_{\text{DSM}} - b_{\text{DTM}}$.

**Morphological removal on a grid.** With $Z$ the DSM and $B_w$ a flat structuring element of width $w$, the opening $Z \circ B_w = (Z \ominus B_w) \oplus B_w$ removes positive features narrower than $w$. An object-removal DTM is $\hat T = Z \circ B_W$ for $W$ larger than the largest object, and the nDSM estimate is $Z - \hat T$ (the **top-hat transform**). Terrain features narrower than $W$ with positive relief (hills, levees) are removed with the objects, which is the method's irreducible error. Progressive versions compare $Z \circ B_{w_k}$ across increasing $w_k$ with a slope-dependent threshold, exactly as in PMF ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)).

**Interpolation bias under a footprint.** Let the true terrain along a profile across a footprint of width $w$ be $t(s)$, with ground points at $s = 0$ and $s = w$ and linear interpolation between them. By Taylor expansion about the chord, the interpolation error at $s$ is $e(s) = t(s) - [\,t(0) + (t(w) - t(0))\,s/w\,] \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\,t''\,s\,(w - s)$ for slowly varying curvature $t''$, with maximum magnitude $|t''|\,w^2/8$ at the centre. For $t'' = 10^{-3}$ m⁻¹ (a 5 m rise over 100 m with constant curvature) and $w = 100$ m, the central error is 1.25 m; for $w = 20$ m it is 5 cm. The bias is negative (interpolation below terrain) on convex terrain and positive on concave. This gives a first-order uncertainty model $\sigma_{\text{interp}} \approx c\,|\kappa|\,w^2$ with $\kappa$ estimated from the surrounding DTM and $c \approx 1/8$ to $1/12$ depending on the assumed distribution of the error across the footprint.

**Vegetation bias correction (LEAN form).** A linear model $\Delta z = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{NDVI} + \varepsilon$ is fitted to $\Delta z = z_{\text{lidar DTM}} - z_{\text{RTK}}$ at calibration points; the corrected DTM is $z_{\text{corr}} = z_{\text{lidar DTM}} - (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1\,\text{NDVI})$, with per-cell uncertainty from the regression's prediction interval. Species- or biomass-stratified versions replace NDVI with a class or a biomass proxy.

## Validation & uncertainty

The errors of this chapter are mostly invisible to a standard accuracy test. ASPRS and LBS checkpoints are placed on open, flat, hard ground—exactly where no object was removed—so a DTM can pass its NVA with centimetres to spare while being wrong by a metre under every warehouse, by 20 cm across every marsh, and by a bridge's height at every river crossing. Validating a DTM as a *terrain* model requires tests designed around the removal decisions.

**How the errors arise.** Policy errors (a bridge kept, a pad removed, a stockpile treated as noise) are categorical and constant per object. Interpolation-under-footprint errors scale with footprint width and terrain curvature and are unknowable in the sense of Section 32.4 but boundable. Vegetation biases are systematic by cover type and season. Modelled bare-earth products carry the training distribution's biases into regions it did not represent. Definition mismatches between a DSM and a DTM from different sources appear as nDSM errors equal to the full object height.

**How to test.**
1. *Policy conformance*: overlay the DTM with independent inventories—bridge locations from the road network, building footprints, dam and levee centrelines, culvert points—and check each feature's treatment against the specification. Count exceptions; they are the policy error rate.
2. *Interpolation-footprint audit*: compute the interpolated-cell mask (Try-it box) and report the interpolated fraction by land-cover class; for a sample of large footprints, compare the DTM against any available ground truth (construction drawings, pre-development lidar, survey) and against the curvature-based bound of the Mathematics section.
3. *Stratified checkpoints*: in addition to NVA points, survey RTK transects in marsh, tall grass, crops, and under canopy (the VVA strata of ASPRS 2023), and report mean error—the bias is the quantity of interest, not the RMSE—per stratum with confidence intervals ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).
4. *nDSM sanity*: the nDSM over cells classified as ground should be near zero; over building footprints it should be positive everywhere (negative values mean the DTM is above the DSM, i.e. a pad or a misclassification); over bridges it should match the policy.
5. *Cross-product comparison*: difference the DTM against a second product with a known, different definition (a DSM, or a modelled bare earth) and confirm that the differences are where the definitions predict—forests, buildings, bridges—and not elsewhere.
6. *Reproducibility*: regenerate the DTM from the classified cloud with the recorded rules and confirm identity.

> **Worked example.** A 1 m lidar DTM of a coastal county passes NVA at 6.8 cm RMSE$_z$ (n = 120 checkpoints on roads and car parks). A marsh transect of 85 RTK points gives a mean DTM error of +0.18 m (σ = 0.09 m). The county's 42 km² of salt marsh therefore sits 18 cm too high in the model. Mean higher high water is 1.05 m above the vertical datum; the marsh platform's true median elevation is 1.00 m, the DTM's is 1.18 m. An inundation-frequency model using the DTM classifies most of the platform as above MHHW (rarely flooded) when in fact it is below (flooded most high tides). Applying a LEAN-type correction fitted on the transect ($\hat\beta_0 = 0.02$ m, $\hat\beta_1 = 0.32$ m per unit NDVI) and validated on 30 withheld points (residual mean +0.01 m, σ = 0.07 m) brings the modelled platform to a median of 1.02 m. The NVA statistic was true and irrelevant; the stratified test found the error that mattered.

**What to report.** The surface definition in the chapter's vocabulary (which objects removed, kept, or flagged; the specification followed and the exceptions); the removal method per object class; the interpolated-cell mask and its fraction by land cover; the vegetation-correction model, its calibration data, and whether it was applied; per-stratum bias and σ from stratified checkpoints; the bridge, dam, and culvert treatment explicitly; and for modelled bare-earth products, the training domain and the validation statistics in the target region. A DTM without this statement is a surface of unknown meaning.

## Standards & guides

- **USGS Lidar Base Specification** (online edition, 2024 revision) — bare-earth definition through classification requirements and DEM rules: bridges removed, dams and levees retained, culverts not cut unless hydro-enforced, hydro-flattening of water bodies, classification report content.
- **Copernicus DEM Product Handbook** (Airbus/ESA; current edition) — editing rules and the EDM, FLM, HEM, and WBM auxiliary layers; the reference for per-cell editing provenance.
- **OGC CityGML 3.0 Conceptual Model (2021)** — Relief, Building, Bridge, Tunnel, and Transportation modules; LoD0–3; the representation in which stacked infrastructure is expressible.
- **INSPIRE Data Specification on Elevation, v3.0 (2013)** — defines elevation surface types (DTM/DSM) and requires the surface type and the treatment of objects to be stated in metadata.
- **ASPRS LAS Specification 1.4 – R15 (2019)** — classes 6 (building), 17 (bridge deck), 20 (ignored ground), 21 (snow), and the flag bits used to implement removal policies.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023)** — NVA/VVA strata; the basis for stratified checkpoint design in vegetated and marsh terrain.
- **National specifications** (AHN product descriptions; Environment Agency LIDAR Composite technical notes; swisstopo swissALTI3D documentation) — examples of alternative conventions (nodata versus interpolation under structures).

## Pitfalls

- **Calling a modelled bare earth a "DTM" without a model-uncertainty layer.** Users treat FABDEM-class products as measured terrain. Detect by reading the product's lineage; avoid by labelling modelled surfaces as such and validating locally per land cover.
- **Bridges removed in the DTM but present in the DSM used for the same model.** Two layers from different definitions disagree at every crossing. Detect with an nDSM check at bridge locations; avoid by deriving both from one point cloud under one policy and documenting it.
- **Landfill, stockpile, or construction pad treated as noise or as building.** The landform is deleted or ramped. Detect with volume checks against survey; avoid by stating the policy for engineered earth and flagging the objects.
- **Marsh "ground" biased by vegetation and then used for sea-level-rise exposure.** Decimetre biases become habitat misclassification. Detect with RTK transects; avoid with LEAN-type correction delivered as a layer.
- **Interpolation under large footprints trusted as terrain.** The DTM under a terminal building is a guess. Detect with the interpolated-cell mask; avoid by delivering the mask and, where it matters, obtaining pre-construction or survey data.
- **Retaining walls and terraces ramped by removal.** Steps become slopes; drainage and volumes are wrong. Detect by profiles across walls; avoid with breaklines and by keeping both terrain levels.
- **Culvert embankments left as dams in a hydrologic DTM.** Flow accumulation breaks at every road crossing. Detect with sink and flow-path checks; avoid by specifying hydro-enforcement where the application needs it ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)).
- **Multi-level roads collapsed to one surface.** Routing and clearance applications see hills or tunnels that do not exist. Detect by overlaying the road network; avoid by using a multi-layer or 3D representation where stacks exist.
- **Solar arrays interpolated from row gaps or kept as surface.** Volumes and drainage over the array are wrong either way. Detect against pre-construction grade; avoid by documenting the choice and masking the array.
- **Surface definition absent from metadata.** The next user cannot tell what was removed. Avoid by writing the Section 32.1 inventory decisions into the product metadata ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

## Key takeaways

- A DTM is a DSM plus a set of decisions; the decisions—not the sensor—determine what the product means under buildings, bridges, vegetation, and engineered earth.
- Publish the decisions, the masks (building footprints, bridge decks, interpolated cells, vegetation corrections), and the interpolation footprint with every DTM.
- The ground under an opaque object is unknowable from airborne data; the DTM there is a hypothesis, and its uncertainty grows with footprint width and terrain curvature.
- Vegetation bias is systematic and correctable; in marshes and grasslands it is decimetres and it decides sea-level-rise exposure, so calibrate it with RTK transects and deliver the correction as a layer.
- Every DSM-derived global product contains bridges, buildings, and canopy; modelled bare-earth products remove them statistically and must be validated locally.
- 2.5D cannot represent stacked infrastructure; when the application needs to know what is under something, use multi-layer rasters, meshes, voxels, or CityGML.
- Validate the removal, not just the open ground: policy conformance against feature inventories, stratified checkpoints in vegetated strata, nDSM sanity checks, and cross-product comparisons.
- The nDSM inherits both definitions; keep DSM and DTM from one source and one policy wherever heights above ground matter.
