# Chapter 23 — Optical satellite-derived bathymetry, wave-kinematics bathymetry, and altimetry-predicted bathymetry

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** Three physically unrelated methods are sold under the label "satellite bathymetry"; this chapter separates them so that readers of [Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md) (sonar) and [Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md) (bathymetric lidar) can judge when each is a substitute, a supplement, or a liability, and so that compilers of global grids ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md), [Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)) label them by source.

**In this chapter.** Optical satellite-derived bathymetry (SDB) reads depth from the colour of the water column; wave-kinematics bathymetry reads it from how fast waves travel; altimetry-predicted bathymetry reads it from the gravitational pull of seafloor mass on the sea surface. They differ by three orders of magnitude in resolution, apply in different depth ranges, and fail in different ways. You will be able to write the Lyzenga and Stumpf models and say what each coefficient depends on; design an SDB calibration against sonar, lidar, or ICESat-2 and report its uncertainty as a function of depth; explain why an SDB depth is referenced to the water level at image time and how to reduce it to chart datum; apply the linear dispersion relation to derive depth from wave period and wavelength and know where it stops working; follow the Smith & Sandwell inversion from marine gravity to predicted depth and state its resolution and local error; read GEBCO's type-identifier grid to tell measured from predicted cells; and decide which of these products is fit for reconnaissance, for charting support, or for nothing navigational at all.

## 23.1 Optical SDB: physics and models

### 23.1.1 Light in water

Sunlight entering the water column is absorbed and scattered by water molecules, dissolved organic matter (CDOM), phytoplankton, and suspended sediment; the fraction reaching depth $z$ decays approximately as $e^{-K_d z}$, where $K_d$ is the **diffuse attenuation coefficient**. In the clearest oceanic water $K_d$ at blue–green wavelengths (≈ 480–500 nm) is about 0.02–0.04 m$^{-1}$; in coastal water 0.1–0.5 m$^{-1}$; in turbid estuaries several m$^{-1}$. Red light is absorbed by water itself ($K_d \approx 0.4$ m$^{-1}$ at 650 nm) and barely survives a few metres; near-infrared is gone within centimetres, which makes NIR bands useful for detecting land and sun glint but useless for depth. Light that reaches the bottom is reflected with the bottom's **albedo** (bright sand ≈ 0.3–0.5 in the green; seagrass and dark reef ≈ 0.05–0.1), travels back up with the same attenuation, and emerges to the sensor along with light scattered by the water column itself. Water is **optically shallow** when the bottom-reflected component is detectable above the water-column scattering and sensor noise; it is **optically deep** when it is not, and no amount of modelling recovers depth there. The practical consequence is a depth limit of roughly two to three optical depths (2–3/$K_d$) in the best band: 20–30 m over bright sand in clear tropical water, 5–10 m in typical temperate coastal water, and 1–3 m in turbid rivers — the "20–30 m" of the TOC applies only to the best case.

### 23.1.2 Empirical models

**Lyzenga (1978, 1981)** linearised the problem by observing that the log of the bottom-reflected radiance, after subtracting the deep-water radiance $L_\infty$, is linear in depth for a uniform bottom:

$$\ln\big(L(\lambda_i) - L_\infty(\lambda_i)\big) = a_i - 2 K_{d,i}\,z + \ln(\text{bottom term}),$$

and that a linear combination of two or more bands can cancel the unknown bottom albedo if its spectral ratio is constant. The multi-band form, $z = a_0 + \sum_i a_i \ln(L_i - L_{\infty,i})$, is fitted to calibration depths by least squares (Lyzenga, Malinas & Tanis 2006 added glint correction and a more robust fitting). Its weaknesses are the deep-water subtraction (noisy for dark pixels, and $L_i - L_{\infty,i}$ can go negative) and the assumption that the bottom albedo ratio is constant.

**Stumpf, Holderied & Sinclair (2003)** proposed the **log-ratio** model that has become the default for multispectral SDB:

$$z = m_1\,\frac{\ln\big(n\,R_w(\lambda_1)\big)}{\ln\big(n\,R_w(\lambda_2)\big)} - m_0,$$

where $R_w$ is the water-leaving reflectance (or radiance, or even top-of-atmosphere reflectance, with poorer results), $\lambda_1$ is the less-attenuated band (blue or coastal), $\lambda_2$ the more-attenuated band (green), $n$ a fixed constant (typically 1,000) that keeps the logarithms positive and the ratio approximately linear with depth, and $m_0, m_1$ are tuned to calibration depths. The ratio's virtue is that a change in bottom albedo moves both logarithms by similar amounts and roughly cancels, so the model is more tolerant of mixed sand, rubble, and seagrass than Lyzenga's — at the cost of a weaker physical basis and a response that becomes nonlinear in deeper water, where producers often fit a piecewise or polynomial relation. Sentinel-2's 10 m blue (B2) and green (B3) bands and, in clear water, the 60 m coastal band (B1) are the usual choices; Landsat 8/9 and WorldView-2/3 (with its coastal and yellow bands) are the other main sources.

### 23.1.3 Physics-based and machine-learning models

**Radiative-transfer (semi-analytical) inversion** (Lee et al. 1999; the HOPE/BRUCE/SAMBUCA family) models the water-leaving reflectance as a function of depth, bottom albedo (from a library of spectra), and water optical properties (absorption, backscatter), and inverts the spectrum pixel by pixel — ideally from hyperspectral data (PRISMA, EnMAP, the planned SBG and CHIME, and historically HICO and airborne sensors). Its promise is depth without in-situ calibration, with the water properties and bottom type retrieved alongside; Dekker et al. (2011) intercompared five such algorithms over Australian reefs and found depth retrievals within about 10–20 % where the bottom was in the spectral library and the water was clear, degrading sharply otherwise. **Machine-learning** models — random forests, gradient boosting, convolutional networks — map band reflectances (and sometimes neighbourhood texture) to depth, trained on sonar, lidar, or ICESat-2 depths (Sagawa et al. 2019 with multi-temporal Landsat 8; many since). They fit the training domain well and extrapolate badly; the "confidence" they output is a model statistic, not an accuracy.

### 23.1.4 Pre-processing: atmosphere, glint, waves, turbidity, compositing

Water-leaving reflectance is a small fraction of top-of-atmosphere radiance, so **atmospheric correction** (ACOLITE, Sen2Cor with care, Polymer, SeaDAS) matters more than for land. **Sun glint** — specular reflection of the sun off wave facets — is removed by regressing visible bands against NIR over deep water (Hedley, Harborne & Mumby 2005) or by choosing scenes with low glint geometry. Whitecaps, breaking waves, and surface foam break every model and must be masked. **Turbidity** both limits depth and, when it varies spatially, is read by the models as spurious depth change — a river plume appears as a shoal. **Multi-temporal compositing** (Caballero & Stumpf 2020; the Allen Coral Atlas approach) selects or averages the clearest, calmest pixels from tens of Sentinel-2 acquisitions, substantially reducing glint, cloud, and turbidity noise — but the composite has no single acquisition time, which brings us to the datum.

### 23.1.5 The datum of the result

An optical image measures depth below the **instantaneous water surface** at the moment of acquisition. That surface is at the tide height of that moment plus surge, setup, and seiche, and it is nowhere near chart datum (LAT, MLLW, or a local equivalent — [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)) except by coincidence. The SDB depth is therefore $z_{\mathrm{image}} = z_{\mathrm{CD}} + \eta(t)$, where $\eta(t)$ is the water level above chart datum at image time, which must be obtained from a tide gauge, a tide model, or a water-level grid (NOAA VDatum, Australian AusCoastVDT, national equivalents), with its own uncertainty of 0.1–0.3 m. When the calibration depths are themselves on chart datum (as sonar soundings are), fitting the model to them *absorbs* $\eta$ into $m_0$ for a single image — correct only if the tide was uniform across the scene, which over a 100 km Sentinel-2 tile in a macrotidal estuary it is not. A multi-temporal composite has no single $\eta$; its effective datum is a weighted mean water level that must be estimated from the acquisition times. Every SDB product should state its vertical reference explicitly; many do not, and "depth" without a datum is not a hydrographic quantity.

## 23.2 Calibration and validation of SDB

Empirical SDB is a regression and must be calibrated with depths from an independent sensor and validated with a *different* set of such depths. Sources, in decreasing order of reliability:

- **Multibeam or single-beam sonar** ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)): decimetre accuracy, on chart datum, but rarely available in the shallowest water where SDB is most useful.
- **Bathymetric lidar** ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)): dense, covering the surf zone and the land–water transition; shares the limitation of needing clear water, so lidar and SDB fail in the same places.
- **ICESat-2 ATL03 photons** (Parrish et al. 2019; Babbel, Parrish & Magruder 2021): the laser altimeter's green (532 nm) photons penetrate clear water and return from the bottom down to about 30–40 m in the best conditions; after a refraction correction (the apparent depth below the surface is scaled by about 0.75, the ratio of the speeds of light, with a small geometric term for the off-nadir angle) and photon classification, ICESat-2 provides free, globally distributed depth profiles along its tracks with accuracy of roughly 0.3–0.5 m. Since 2021 this has transformed SDB practice: Thomas et al. (2021) and Caballero & Stumpf (2023) showed that ICESat-2 profiles can calibrate Sentinel-2 SDB in regions with no sonar at all. ICESat-2 depths are referenced to the ellipsoid, so the water-surface photons give $\eta$ at the time of the ICESat-2 pass, and the image's $\eta$ still must be modelled.
- **Charted soundings**: often decades old and sparse; useful as a sanity check, dangerous as calibration where the seabed is mobile.

The **typical uncertainty** of well-calibrated multispectral SDB is about 10–20 % of depth, or 0.5–1 m RMSE in the 0–10 m range and 1–3 m at 10–20 m, with errors increasing with depth, turbidity, and bottom darkness (Hedley et al. 2018; Caballero & Stumpf 2019; Ashphaq, Srivastava & Mitra 2021). The error is not Gaussian: it has a depth-dependent bias (models typically overpredict shallow and underpredict deep), it is spatially correlated with bottom type (a seagrass bed is biased deep), and it has gross errors at the optically deep limit, where the model keeps producing numbers from noise. Validation must therefore be reported by depth bin, by bottom type where known, and with a map of residuals rather than a single RMSE; the maximum depth of validity should be set at the depth where residual scatter exceeds a chosen tolerance and the product truncated there.

> **Worked example.** *Calibrating and reducing a Stumpf-model depth.* A Sentinel-2 scene acquired at 10:32 UTC gives, after atmospheric correction, $R_w(\mathrm{B2}) = 0.045$ and $R_w(\mathrm{B3}) = 0.028$ at a pixel. With $n = 1000$: $\ln(45) = 3.807$, $\ln(28) = 3.332$, ratio $= 1.1424$. Calibration against 240 multibeam soundings on LAT gave $m_1 = 42.0$ m and $m_0 = 41.5$ m (the magnitudes are typical: small changes in the ratio map to large depth changes, so the model's sensitivity to reflectance noise is high). The SDB depth is $z = 42.0 \times 1.1424 - 41.5 = 6.48$ m. But this calibration absorbed the tide at image time, $\eta = +1.8$ m above LAT at the gauge; the chart-datum depth at the gauge is therefore consistent, while 40 km up-estuary where the tide at 10:32 was $+2.3$ m, the SDB depth is 0.5 m too *shallow* on LAT if the single scene-wide $m_0$ is used. A reflectance noise of 0.002 in B3 changes $\ln(28)$ to $\ln(30) = 3.401$, the ratio to 1.1193, and the depth to 5.51 m — a 1 m change from a reflectance perturbation at the sensor's noise level, which is why SDB is averaged spatially and temporally.

## 23.3 Wave-kinematics bathymetry

Surface gravity waves in water of depth $h$ obey, in linear theory, the **dispersion relation**

$$\omega^2 = g\,k\tanh(kh),$$

with angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi/T$, wavenumber $k = 2\pi/L$, and $g = 9.81$ m s$^{-2}$. In deep water ($kh \gg 1$) the depth drops out and the wave carries no information about the bottom; in shallow water ($kh \ll 1$) the phase speed is $c = \sqrt{gh}$ and depends on nothing else. In between — roughly $h < L/2$ — measuring both $T$ (or $\omega$) and $L$ (or $k$, or the phase speed $c = \omega/k$) from a sequence of images yields $h$ by inverting the relation. The sensitivity is best in intermediate depths and degrades as $kh$ grows: for a 10 s swell ($L_\infty = 156$ m), depth is well resolved from about 2 m to perhaps 40–60 m; for a 5 s wind sea ($L_\infty = 39$ m) only to about 15 m. The method needs waves of sufficient energy and coherence, and it fails in calm conditions, in confused seas, and where currents Doppler-shift the waves (a 1 m s$^{-1}$ current changes the apparent phase speed of a 10 s wave by roughly 10 %, which maps to a large depth error in intermediate water).

**Shore-based video** (the Argus stations) was the first operational implementation: **cBathy** (Holman, Plant & Holland 2013) cross-spectrally analyses time stacks of pixel intensity to extract the dominant frequencies and their wavenumbers over a grid, inverts the dispersion relation, and Kalman-filters the estimates over successive hourly collections; reported accuracies at Duck, North Carolina are about 0.5 m RMSE in 0–5 m with degradation in the surf zone during energetic conditions and in deeper water. **Satellite** implementations exploit the small time lag between a sensor's spectral bands: Sentinel-2's detectors acquire the bands up to about a second apart (B2, B3, B4, B8 differ by roughly 0.5–1.0 s), so a wave crest moves a measurable fraction of its wavelength between bands, and the phase shift gives the celerity (Bergsma, Almar & Maisongrande 2019, using a Radon transform to isolate wave directions; Almar et al. 2021 for a global application). **Pléiades/Pléiades Neo persistent (video) mode** and **SkySat video** give sequences of tens of frames over tens of seconds (Almar et al. 2019), and **SAR** images show wave patterns from which wavelength (and, with care, direction) is read, though a single SAR image gives no period and requires an assumed or modelled wave period. Satellite wave-kinematics bathymetry produces depths at 100–500 m resolution with 10–30 % relative errors in 5–50 m of water — coarser and noisier than optical SDB, but independent of water clarity and bottom albedo, and extending deeper. The datum is again the water level at image time, and strong currents, wave breaking, and refraction–diffraction in complex bathymetry degrade it.

## 23.4 Altimetry-predicted bathymetry

The third method does not see the water at all. Seafloor topography is a mass distribution: a seamount of density ≈ 2,700 kg m$^{-3}$ standing in water of 1,030 kg m$^{-3}$ is an excess mass that raises the geoid — and therefore the mean sea surface — by metres over a horizontal scale comparable to its width. Satellite radar altimeters ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)) measure that sea surface to a few centimetres, and after removing the time-varying dynamic ocean topography the along-track slope of the mean surface is the deflection of the vertical, whose gridded field is the **marine gravity anomaly** (Sandwell & Smith 1997; Sandwell et al. 2014). Smith & Sandwell (1994, 1997 ⟨H⟩) then inverted gravity to topography.

The inversion rests on **Parker's (1973)** forward relation between topography $h(\mathbf{x})$ at mean depth $d$ and the gravity anomaly at the sea surface, in the wavenumber domain:

$$\Delta g(\mathbf{k}) = 2\pi G\,\Delta\rho\,e^{-|\mathbf{k}| d}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mathbf{k}|^{n-1}}{n!}\,\mathcal{F}\{h^n\}(\mathbf{k}),$$

whose first term is the linear **admittance** $Z(k) = 2\pi G\Delta\rho\,e^{-kd}$ (in mGal per metre of topography), modified at long wavelengths by isostatic compensation (flexural filter; [Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)) and at short wavelengths by the upward-continuation factor $e^{-kd}$, which attenuates a 10 km wavelength feature at 4 km depth by $e^{-2\pi \times 4/10} \approx 0.08$. The useful band is therefore limited: below about 15 km wavelength the signal is buried in altimeter noise, and above about 160 km isostasy removes it. Smith & Sandwell band-pass the gravity to 15–160 km, downward-continue it, and scale it by an admittance that is *calibrated regionally against shipboard soundings* (the density contrast and sediment cover vary, so a theoretical admittance alone is biased), then add back the long-wavelength depth from the ship data and splice in the ship soundings themselves where they exist. The product is a grid in which every cell is either a shipboard measurement (with its own, often poor, navigation and sound-speed error from pre-GNSS cruises), an interpolation between them constrained by gravity, or a pure gravity prediction.

Its performance is well characterised. **Resolution** is about 6–12 km half-wavelength in the best current gravity (Sandwell et al. 2014; Tozer et al. 2019 for SRTM15+), so features smaller than that — most seamount summits, canyon thalwegs, all shelf morphology — are absent or smoothed. **Local error** in predicted cells is typically 100–300 m RMS against withheld soundings and can exceed 1 km where sediment thickness is unknown, where the density contrast is anomalous (young volcanic terrain, serpentinised crust), or where a flat sediment-covered seafloor hides buried basement topography that gravity sees but the seafloor does not (Marks & Smith 2006). The **depth–age prior** of Parsons & Sclater (1977 ⟨H⟩), $d \approx 2500 + 350\sqrt{t}$ m for crustal age $t$ in Myr (and flattening beyond about 70 Myr), supplies the long-wavelength regional depth where ship tracks are absent and seafloor age is known from magnetic anomalies ([Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)); it fails near hotspots, plateaus, and continental margins. The method's great success is **seamount detection**: Kim & Wessel (2011) and later work catalogued tens of thousands of seamounts from gravity, and the 2014 model revealed features buried under sediment — but the detection gives position and approximate size, not a navigable summit depth.

**SRTM15+** (Tozer et al. 2019; 15″ ≈ 460 m posting) and **GEBCO** (the GEBCO_2024 grid at 15″) are built this way for the deep ocean, with GEBCO adding the Seabed 2030 compilation of multibeam and the SRTM-derived land. GEBCO publishes a **Type Identifier (TID) grid** alongside the depth grid, coding each cell as direct measurement (single-beam, multibeam, lidar, …), indirect (predicted from gravity, interpolated, from charts, …), or unknown; with the GEBCO_2024 release Seabed 2030 reported 26.1 % of the ocean floor mapped by direct measurement at its target resolutions (28.7 % by 2026), the remainder predicted or interpolated. Any use of GEBCO or SRTM15+ must read the TID.

> **Case file.** Predicted bathymetry is reconnaissance, not a chart. In January 2005 the submarine USS *San Francisco* struck an uncharted seamount near the Caroline Islands at speed; the charts in use showed deep water, though a later review noted that satellite imagery and some data sources had indicated a possible hazard nearby. Independently of the specifics of that incident, gravity-predicted grids routinely show seamounts that rise to within hundreds of metres of the surface with summit depths uncertain by hundreds of metres — a predicted summit at 400 m could be at 100 m or at 800 m. GEBCO's own documentation and IHO guidance state that predicted and interpolated cells are unsuitable for navigation. The *San Francisco* grounding predated the 2014 gravity model and is cited here only to illustrate the consequence of treating any deep-ocean compilation as complete (U.S. Navy investigation, 2005 (verify)).

## 23.5 Products and providers

Commercial SDB providers — **EOMAP** (Germany), **TCarta** (USA), and others — produce multispectral SDB with physics-based or hybrid models, typically with quoted uncertainties and in some cases IHO-style quality attributes, and supply hydrographic offices, cable and offshore wind developers, and coastal engineers. National programs increasingly produce SDB in-house: NOAA uses SDB to prioritise and plan surveys and, in some cases, to update charts in remote areas with appropriate CATZOC downgrades; the UK Hydrographic Office, SHOM (France), the Australian Hydrographic Office and Geoscience Australia, and the Canadian Hydrographic Service all run programs. The **Allen Coral Atlas** (Lyons et al. 2020) publishes a global reef-area SDB at 10 m from Sentinel-2 composites, intended for habitat mapping rather than navigation. Open code and notebooks (the `sdb` and `sdb-gui` projects, Google Earth Engine scripts for Stumpf-ratio SDB, ICESat-2 bathymetry extraction via `SlideRule`, `icepyx`, and the C-SHELPh classifier) make the empirical workflow reproducible by anyone with a calibration set. On the deep-ocean side, **GEBCO**, **SRTM15+**, and NOAA's **ETOPO 2022** are the global compilations; all embed Smith–Sandwell-type prediction, and ETOPO 2022 and GEBCO share much of their input. Wave-kinematics products are mostly research and national-agency outputs (SHOM and CNES in France; NOAA's experimental cBathy-based coastal products).

## 23.6 Fitness for use

The IHO's **Zones of Confidence (CATZOC)** scheme in S-57 and the corresponding **Quality of Bathymetric Data (QoBD)** in S-101 encode, per area, the position and depth accuracy and seafloor coverage of the source survey: ZOC A1 requires ± 0.5 m + 1 % depth accuracy and full seafloor search; A2 ± 1.0 m + 2 %; B ± 1.0 m + 2 % without full search; C ± 2.0 m + 5 %; D worse than C; U unassessed ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md), [Chapter 70](ch70-specifications-guided-tour.md)). Multispectral SDB at its best (0.5–1 m RMSE in shallow clear water, with no detection of small objects) corresponds to ZOC C in depth accuracy and fails the seafloor-search requirement entirely — a coral head of 5 m diameter is below the pixel size and a wreck is invisible. The IHO's **B-13 Guidance to Satellite-Derived Bathymetry** (Edition 1.0.0, March 2024) and the SDB notes accompanying S-44 Edition 6 (2020) accordingly position SDB as a tool for reconnaissance, survey planning, change detection, gap filling in areas with no other data, and charting in remote shallow areas with explicit quality downgrade — not as a substitute for a Special Order or Order 1 survey where safety of navigation is at stake. Wave-kinematics bathymetry is coarser still and belongs to reconnaissance and coastal-process modelling. Altimetry-predicted bathymetry is fit for plate-scale geophysics, ocean circulation and tsunami modelling at the resolution of the model, and the planning of ship surveys; it is unfit for anything local, and a GEBCO cell with TID "predicted" should never be read as a depth at a point.

> **Definitions that bite.** Three quantities travel under the word *depth*: depth below the instantaneous water surface (what any optical or wave method measures), depth below chart datum (what a chart needs), and depth below the ellipsoid or the geoid (what a geodetic compilation stores). A 2 m tidal range and a 20 m geoid–ellipsoid separation are both larger than SDB's accuracy. Insist on the datum with every depth.

## Then & now

- **1930s–1940s.** Aerial photographs used to estimate shoal depths by tone; wartime wave-refraction analysis of aerial photos to infer beach gradients for landings (the method behind modern wave-kinematics bathymetry).
- **1972 ⟨H⟩.** Landsat 1 launches; within a few years its MSS bands are used for shallow-water depth mapping.
- **1977 ⟨H⟩.** Parsons & Sclater publish the depth–age relation for oceanic lithosphere.
- **1978–1981.** Lyzenga's log-linear SDB model; Seasat altimetry (1978) first reveals seafloor fabric in the marine geoid.
- **1985–1995.** Geosat (1985–86) and ERS-1 (1994–95) geodetic missions; Smith & Sandwell 1994 bathymetric prediction and 1997 ⟨H⟩ global seafloor topography.
- **2003.** Stumpf, Holderied & Sinclair publish the log-ratio model.
- **2013.** cBathy (Holman, Plant & Holland) formalises video-based wave-kinematics bathymetry.
- **2014.** Sandwell et al. CryoSat-2/Jason-1 marine gravity; GEBCO 2014 grid at 30″ (Weatherall et al. 2015).
- **2015–2017.** Sentinel-2A/B launch, bringing free 10 m multispectral imagery with 5-day revisit — the enabling data for routine SDB and for band-lag wave kinematics.
- **2018–2021.** ICESat-2 launches (2018); its bathymetric capability is validated (Parrish et al. 2019) and becomes the standard free calibration source for SDB (2021). Seabed 2030 begins (2017–18); SRTM15+ (2019).
- **2020–2024.** IHO S-44 Edition 6 (2020) acknowledges SDB; IHO B-13 SDB guidance (March 2024); GEBCO grids reach 15″ with TID layers; hyperspectral missions (PRISMA 2019, EnMAP 2022) revive physics-based inversion.

The direction of travel is from single-image, single-model empirical SDB toward multi-temporal, ICESat-2-calibrated, physics-aware SDB with per-pixel uncertainty; and in the deep ocean from gravity prediction toward direct multibeam coverage, cell by cell, which Seabed 2030 tracks in the TID.

## Mathematics

**Two-flow attenuation model.** For a homogeneous water column of depth $z$ with bottom albedo $A_b$ and diffuse attenuation $K_d$ (assumed equal for down- and up-welling light, a simplification), the subsurface reflectance is approximately

$$R(\lambda) = R_\infty(\lambda) + \big(A_b(\lambda) - R_\infty(\lambda)\big)\,e^{-2K_d(\lambda)\,z},$$

where $R_\infty$ is the optically deep reflectance. Taking logarithms gives Lyzenga's linear form; taking the ratio of logarithms for two bands with $K_{d,1} < K_{d,2}$ gives Stumpf's model, whose linearity in $z$ holds while the bottom term dominates and $A_b$ ratios are stable.

**Stumpf log-ratio.** $z = m_1\,\dfrac{\ln(n R_w(\lambda_1))}{\ln(n R_w(\lambda_2))} - m_0$. Error propagation: with ratio $\rho = \ln(nR_1)/\ln(nR_2)$, $\partial z/\partial R_2 = -m_1\,\ln(nR_1)/\big(R_2\,[\ln(nR_2)]^2\big)$, so for the worked-example values $\partial z/\partial R_2 \approx -42 \times 3.807/(0.028 \times 11.10) = -514$ m per unit reflectance, i.e. 0.5 m per 0.001 of green reflectance. The sensitivity grows as $R_2$ falls with depth — the mathematical statement of the optically deep limit.

**ICESat-2 refraction correction.** For a photon with apparent depth $D_a$ below the water surface at off-nadir angle $\theta_1$ in air and $\theta_2 = \arcsin(\sin\theta_1 / n_w)$ in water ($n_w \approx 1.34$ for seawater at 532 nm), the corrected depth is $D = D_a \cos\theta_1 / (n_w \cos\theta_2) \approx D_a / n_w \cdot \cos\theta_1/\cos\theta_2$, which for ICESat-2's near-nadir geometry is $D \approx 0.746\,D_a$ (Parrish et al. 2019).

**Linear dispersion and its inversion.** $\omega^2 = gk\tanh(kh)$; phase speed $c = \omega/k$; depth from measured $(\omega, k)$: $h = \dfrac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{artanh}\!\left(\dfrac{\omega^2}{gk}\right)$, valid only for $\omega^2 < gk$ (otherwise the wave is in deep water and $h$ is undetermined). Sensitivity, at fixed $k$: differentiating $c^2 = (g/k)\tanh(kh)$ gives $\partial h/\partial c = 2c\cosh^2(kh)/g$, so the relative error amplification is $\dfrac{\delta h/h}{\delta c/c} = \dfrac{\sinh(2kh)}{kh}$, which equals 2 in the shallow-water limit and grows exponentially toward deep water: at $kh = 1$ ($h \approx L/6$) a 5 % celerity error gives an 18 % depth error, at $kh = 2$ it gives 68 %. With a current $U$ along the wave direction the observed frequency is Doppler-shifted, $\omega_{\mathrm{obs}} = \omega + kU$, which must be removed before inversion. For Sentinel-2 band lag $\Delta t$ and measured crest displacement $\Delta x$ between bands, $c = \Delta x/\Delta t$; with $\Delta t \approx 0.5$–1 s and 10 m pixels, sub-pixel displacement estimation (phase correlation, Radon-domain cross-correlation) is required.

**Parker's forward relation and the admittance.** As in Section 23.4; the linear admittance is $Z(k) = 2\pi G \Delta\rho\, e^{-kd}$, with $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ m$^3$ kg$^{-1}$ s$^{-2}$. For $\Delta\rho = 1{,}670$ kg m$^{-3}$ (crust minus water) the zero-depth value is $2\pi G\Delta\rho = 7.0 \times 10^{-7}$ s$^{-2}$ = 0.070 mGal m$^{-1}$: a 1,000 m seamount at the surface would produce 70 mGal, attenuated by $e^{-kd}$ to 70 × 0.28 = 20 mGal for a 20 km wavelength at $d = 4$ km. With flexural compensation the admittance is multiplied by $\big(1 - \Phi_e(k)\, e^{-k t_c}\big)$ where $\Phi_e(k) = \big[1 + Dk^4/(\Delta\rho_m g)\big]^{-1}$ is the flexural response for rigidity $D$ and $t_c$ the crustal thickness — the long-wavelength roll-off ([Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)). The inversion is the regularised inverse, $\hat h(\mathbf{k}) = W(k)\,\Delta g(\mathbf{k}) / Z(k)$, with $W$ a band-pass window (15–160 km) and the linear term only; nonlinear terms are iterated where topography is large relative to depth.

**Depth–age.** $d(t) = 2500 + 350\,t^{1/2}$ m for $t < 70$ Myr; $d(t) = 6400 - 3200\,e^{-t/62.8}$ m for older crust (Parsons & Sclater 1977; later recalibrations such as Stein & Stein 1992 differ in detail).

> **Try it.** Compute the gravity–bathymetry admittance and coherence over a region from a gravity grid and a bathymetry grid with GMT, then check which wavelengths are actually explained by topography. Expected outcome: coherence above 0.5 only in roughly the 20–150 km band over normal oceanic crust; a narrower band over sedimented or compensated regions.
>
> ```bash
> # Cut matching grids (Sandwell/Smith marine gravity v32 and SRTM15+ depth, both 1 arc-minute here)
> REGION=-160/-140/10/25
> gmt grdcut grav_32.1.nc -R$REGION -Ggrav.nc
> gmt grdcut SRTM15+V2.5.5.nc -R$REGION -Gtopo.nc
> gmt grdsample topo.nc -Rgrav.nc -Gtopo_rs.nc
> # Admittance (-E) and coherence between topo (first) and gravity (second); flexural model with Te = 10 km
> gmt gravfft topo_rs.nc grav.nc -D1670 -E -T10000/2800/3300/1030 -N+d > admit.txt
> # Columns: wavelength(m) admittance coherence errors ... ; print the band where coherence > 0.5
> awk '$3 > 0.5 {print $1/1000, $2, $3}' admit.txt | sort -n | awk 'NR==1{min=$1} {max=$1} END{print "coherent band:", min, "to", max, "km"}'
> ```

## Validation & uncertainty

All three methods are inferences, and their uncertainty must be assessed against direct measurement that is independent of the calibration.

**Optical SDB.** Hold out at least 30 % of the reference depths (or an independent survey) stratified by depth and bottom type; never validate on the calibration points. Report RMSE, mean error (bias), and the 95 % interval per 2 m depth bin; plot residuals against depth, against the band ratio, and on a map. Identify the depth beyond which residual scatter exceeds the tolerance and truncate the product there. State the vertical reference and how $\eta(t)$ was obtained and applied. Quantify the fraction of the area masked for glint, cloud, breaking waves, and turbidity. For multi-temporal composites, report the number of scenes per pixel and the acquisition-time distribution, and estimate the effective water level. Compare the achieved accuracy with the IHO S-44 TVU for the order claimed ($\mathrm{TVU} = \sqrt{a^2 + (b\,d)^2}$, with $a = 0.5$ m, $b = 0.013$ for Order 1a) — multispectral SDB typically fails Order 1a beyond a few metres and should be labelled accordingly ([Chapter 70](ch70-specifications-guided-tour.md)).

**Wave kinematics.** Validate against sonar or lidar profiles in the 5–40 m range; report relative error by depth and by wave condition (significant wave height and peak period at acquisition, from a buoy or a wave model); flag areas with strong currents and with wave breaking; and give the effective horizontal resolution of the inversion window (hundreds of metres), which is the real resolution of the product regardless of the grid cell size ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).

**Altimetry-predicted bathymetry.** Validate by withholding entire ship cruises (not random points, which are spatially correlated with the included soundings) and comparing predicted against withheld depths; report RMS by region and by distance from the nearest included sounding, and compare the power spectrum of the prediction with that of multibeam to state the wavelength at which the prediction loses coherence. Read the TID grid and never report an accuracy for predicted cells based on measured cells.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Representative error magnitudes (1σ, metres unless noted) for the three families in their nominal operating ranges; actual values vary by site and must be measured.
>
> | Component | Optical SDB (0–20 m) | Wave kinematics (5–40 m) | Altimetry-predicted (deep ocean) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Sensor noise / matching | 0.3–0.8 (grows with depth) | 10–20 % of depth | n/a |
> | Model misfit (bottom type, turbidity / currents, breaking / density, sediments) | 0.5–2 | 10–30 % of depth | 100–300 (locally > 1,000) |
> | Water-level reduction to datum | 0.1–0.3 | 0.1–0.3 | n/a (ellipsoid/geoid, ≤ 1) |
> | Calibration data error | 0.1–0.5 | 0.1–0.5 | ship soundings 10–100 (old navigation) |
> | Horizontal resolution (effective) | 10–30 m | 100–500 m | 6–12 km |
> | Typical reported RMSE | 0.5–1 (shallow); 1–3 (10–20 m) | 1–3 | 100–300 |

## Software

**Open source.** **ACOLITE** (RBINS): atmospheric correction and glint handling for Sentinel-2, Landsat, PlanetScope over water; the standard first step. **Google Earth Engine** scripts: Stumpf and Lyzenga SDB with multi-temporal compositing (many public notebooks; check any script against the papers). **`sdb` / `sdb-gui`** and similar Python notebooks: empirical and ML SDB with ICESat-2 calibration. **SlideRule**, **icepyx**, **C-SHELPh**: ICESat-2 ATL03 access and bathymetric photon classification with refraction correction. **cBathy** (MATLAB, open): video wave-kinematics inversion. **GMT** (`gravfft`, `grdfft`, `grdfilter`): admittance, coherence, and gravity-to-topography inversion; `grdimage` with the TID grid for source maps. **Fatiando a Terra / Harmonica**: gravity forward modelling and inversion in Python ([Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)). **QGIS**: all of the above as rasters, with the GEBCO TID as a styled layer.

**Free but closed.** NOAA **VDatum** (water-level and datum transformation for US waters); national water-level grids.

**Commercial.** **EOMAP** and **TCarta** services and products (physics-based SDB with uncertainty layers; caveat — proprietary models, so request validation reports). **ENVI** (NV5) with SDB and spectral tools. **CARIS HIPS/SIPS** and **Qimera** (QPS) ingest SDB as auxiliary surfaces for survey planning; their quality attribution should follow the SDB's own validation, not the software default.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting SDB depths referenced to the water level at image time as chart-datum depths.** The tide at acquisition is absorbed into the calibration or simply ignored. Always compute $\eta(t)$ from a gauge or model, apply it spatially, and state the datum.
- **Extrapolating an empirical model beyond its calibration depths or bottom types.** The log-ratio is approximately linear only in a limited band; beyond it the model returns plausible numbers from noise. Truncate at the validated depth; mask bottom types not represented in calibration.
- **Validating on the calibration points.** This reports the fit, not the accuracy. Hold out an independent set, preferably from a different survey.
- **Treating ML-SDB confidence as accuracy.** A random forest's prediction variance says nothing about bias outside the training domain. Validate against withheld in-situ depths by depth bin.
- **Using a single scene with glint, haze, or a river plume.** Spatially varying turbidity is read as bathymetry. Composite multiple clear scenes or mask aggressively, and inspect residual maps for plume shapes.
- **Assuming SDB detects hazards.** A 10 m pixel cannot show a 3 m coral head, a wreck, or a pile. SDB supports charting with CATZOC C or D at best and never satisfies full seafloor search.
- **Using the dispersion relation in deep water or in strong currents.** Beyond $h \approx L/2$ the depth is unobservable; a 1 m s$^{-1}$ current biases intermediate-depth retrievals by tens of percent. Check $kh$ and model the current.
- **Using altimetry-predicted bathymetry for anything local.** Its resolution is 6–12 km and its local error hundreds of metres; a predicted seamount summit is a hypothesis. Read the TID and plan a ship survey.
- **Assuming the depth–age relation holds everywhere.** It fails near hotspots, plateaus, back-arc basins, and margins; it is a regional prior, not a measurement.
- **Mixing ICESat-2 ellipsoidal depths with chart-datum soundings in one calibration.** The two differ by the geoid–ellipsoid separation plus the datum offset — tens of metres. Reduce everything to one reference before fitting.
- **Compositing SDB, wave-kinematics, predicted, and measured depths into one grid without a source layer.** Users cannot tell measurement from inference. Carry a TID-style attribute in every compilation ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).

## Key takeaways

- "Satellite bathymetry" is three methods: optical SDB (colour, 0–30 m, 10 m resolution), wave kinematics (celerity, 5–50 m, 100–500 m), and altimetry-predicted bathymetry (gravity, deep ocean, 6–12 km). Different physics, different depth ranges, different error structures.
- Optical SDB is a regression; it needs independent calibration and validation depths, performs at roughly 10–20 % of depth in clear water over bright bottoms, and must be truncated at the optically deep limit.
- ICESat-2 bathymetric photons are the free calibration source that made SDB practical without sonar; they are ellipsoidal heights and must be reduced to the product datum.
- Every optical or wave-based depth is relative to the water level at acquisition; reducing it to chart datum requires a water-level model applied spatially, and composites need an effective water level.
- Wave-kinematics bathymetry is independent of water clarity but needs energetic, coherent waves, fails in deep water and in currents, and has an effective resolution of hundreds of metres.
- Altimetry-predicted bathymetry is reconnaissance for geophysics and survey planning; local errors of hundreds of metres make it unfit for navigation or any point depth.
- GEBCO and SRTM15+ embed predicted depths; the TID grid tells you which cells are measured. Read it, and carry source attribution in anything you compile.
- None of these methods detects small objects; CATZOC/QoBD attribution must reflect that, and safety-of-navigation surveys remain sonar and lidar work.
