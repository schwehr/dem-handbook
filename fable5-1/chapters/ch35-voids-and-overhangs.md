# Chapter 35 — Voids, occlusion, shadows, overhangs, and multi-valued surfaces

> **Part VII — From sensor data to products.** The chapter about where the data are not: the cells a sensor could not see, the surfaces a grid cannot hold, and the masks that keep the difference between measured and invented honest.

**In this chapter.** Every DEM has holes, and every published DEM has had most of them filled. You will be able to name the void mechanism of each sensor—lidar shadow behind a wall and absorption over water, SAR layover and shadow and decorrelation, optical cloud and shadow and matching failure, multibeam nadir gaps and outer-beam rejection and acoustic shadow, bathymetric-lidar turbidity and surf—and predict from geometry where the voids will fall. You will understand why cliffs, caves, bridges, piers, canopy, and balconies cannot be represented in a 2.5D grid without a collapse rule, and what the alternatives cost. You will choose among void-filling methods (interpolation, delta surface fill, auxiliary-DEM fill, learned inpainting), compute the error each introduces as a function of void size and terrain roughness, and produce the source/void/fill mask without which a filled DEM is indistinguishable from a measured one. You will apply ISO 19157 completeness measures, read the void and feature-detection clauses in the USGS LBS and IHO S-44, and decide when to keep overlapping strips and swaths for quality control and when to thin them.

## 35.1 Void sources per sensor

A void is a location where the product has no measurement. The mechanisms differ by sensor, and knowing them lets you predict where voids will be before the data arrive and judge whether a filled region was ever observable.

**Topographic lidar.** The laser is a line-of-sight instrument from a moving platform. Anything that blocks the line hides what is behind it: the **shadow** of a building on the side away from the aircraft, the foot of a cliff seen from above, the ground under a dense evergreen canopy, the inside of a narrow street canyon when the scan angle is wide. Shadow length is geometric—an object of height $h$ seen at off-nadir angle $\theta$ hides a strip of width $h \tan\theta$ beyond it—so a 20 m building at the swath edge of a ±20° scan hides a 7 m strip, and overlapping strips flown in opposite directions fill most of these shadows, which is one reason specifications require 20–30 % sidelap. **Absorption** over water, wet asphalt, fresh tar roofs, and some dark surfaces produces **dropouts**—no return above threshold—and specular loss does the same over glass, still water, and solar panels at the wrong angle. **Range-gate and scanner artefacts** (dead zones at the ends of an oscillating mirror's travel, pulses lost at the maximum unambiguous range) produce regular stripes. All of these leave voids in the point cloud that interpolation during gridding silently closes unless the gridding tool is told to limit the search radius.

**SAR and InSAR.** A side-looking radar maps slant range to ground; terrain facing the sensor at a slope steeper than the incidence angle is compressed into **foreshortening**, and when the slope exceeds the incidence angle the top of the slope is imaged before the bottom—**layover**—and the pixels are a sum of returns from several ground positions, unresolvable. Slopes facing away from the sensor steeper than the complement of the incidence angle are in **radar shadow**, with no return at all. For a given look direction, layover appears on sensor-facing steep slopes and shadow on the far side; ascending and descending passes see opposite sides, so combining them fills much of each other's voids, which is how SRTM and TanDEM-X were planned. **Decorrelation**—loss of interferometric coherence between the two acquisitions because of vegetation motion, snow, water, or temporal change—leaves pixels whose phase cannot be unwrapped reliably; the SRTM mission's single-pass geometry avoided temporal decorrelation but still produced voids over water, in layover and shadow, and where the phase unwrapping failed in rough terrain. The original SRTM release had voids concentrated in the Himalaya, the Andes, and other high-relief regions, plus water bodies; the C-band 3″ product's void area was quantified by Reuter, Nelson, and Jarvis (2007) in the course of filling it. In the Copernicus DEM, derived from TanDEM-X, the editing process records in the **Filling Mask (FLM)** which cells were filled and from which source, and the **Height Error Mask (HEM)** carries the per-pixel height-error estimate ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)).

**Optical stereo and SfM.** Clouds and their shadows hide the ground; snow, water, sand, and uniform roofs offer no texture to match; steep slopes seen from one side only occlude the other; moving objects break the correspondence. Matching failures appear as voids where the matcher's confidence is below threshold (the honest outcome) or as blunders where it is above threshold but wrong (the dangerous one). Global optical DSMs (ASTER GDEM, AW3D30) are mosaics of many scenes selected for cloud-free coverage, and the per-pixel count of contributing scenes—provided as an auxiliary layer—falls to one or zero in persistently cloudy regions, where the product is either a single noisy estimate or a fill from another source ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)).

**Multibeam echosounders.** A swath system ensonifies a fan beneath the vessel; beyond the outer beams there is nothing, so coverage depends on line spacing relative to swath width, and a survey planned for 100 % coverage at the deepest depth leaves **holidays** between lines where the bottom shoals. Within the swath, the **nadir** region in some systems has poor bottom detection on soft sediment at steep incidence (the specular return saturates or the bottom-detection algorithm fails), leaving a nadir gap; the **outer beams** at low grazing angle have the worst range accuracy and the largest footprint, and are routinely rejected beyond ±60–65° in processing, which narrows the effective swath. Behind a wreck, a boulder, or a pier piling lies an **acoustic shadow** exactly analogous to lidar shadow, and a target must be ensonified from more than one direction to be fully measured. Bubble sweep-down in rough weather, aeration from the vessel's own wake on turns, and fish schools produce dropouts and false bottoms ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)).

**Bathymetric lidar.** The green laser's bottom return weakens exponentially with depth and turbidity; where the product of depth and diffuse attenuation exceeds the system's capability (roughly 2–3 Secchi depths for modern systems, less in the surf), there is no bottom detection and the void begins. The **surf zone** adds entrained air, foam, and a moving surface that scatters the pulse; it is the band most often empty in both topographic and bathymetric lidar, and in multibeam, which is why the white ribbon of [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md) persists ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)).

| Sensor | Void mechanism | Geometric predictor | Typical remedy at acquisition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topographic lidar | Shadow, absorption, dropout | $h\tan\theta$ behind objects; water | Opposite-direction overlap; narrow FOV in cities |
| InSAR | Layover, shadow, decorrelation | Slope vs incidence angle; vegetation, water | Ascending + descending; short baselines |
| Optical stereo | Cloud, shadow, no texture, occlusion | Cloud climatology; slope aspect | Multi-date scene selection; tri-stereo |
| Multibeam | Holidays, nadir gap, outer-beam rejection, acoustic shadow | Swath width vs depth; grazing angle | Line spacing from shoalest depth; crosslines |
| Bathymetric lidar | Turbidity, depth, surf | Secchi depth; wave climate | Calm, clear-water windows; overlap |

## 35.2 Overhangs and multi-valued surfaces

A raster DEM stores one elevation per $(x, y)$. Where the real world has more than one surface above a point—an overhanging cliff, a cave mouth, a bridge deck above a river, a pier above the seabed, a tree canopy above the ground, a balcony above a pavement, an arcade, a highway interchange—the grid must choose, and the choice is a **collapse rule**. There is no neutral choice; each rule serves some uses and damages others.

The common rules and their consequences:

- **Highest surface (first return, maximum):** the DSM convention. The bridge deck covers the river, the canopy covers the forest floor, the cliff overhang covers the ledge below. Right for line-of-sight, aviation, and orthorectification of imagery seen from above; wrong for hydraulics and for anything under the overhang.
- **Lowest surface (last return, minimum, or ground class):** the DTM convention. The bridge disappears, the river surface or interpolated channel is kept; the forest floor is kept; the cliff foot is kept but the overhang above it vanishes, which can make a vertical or overhanging cliff appear as a steep but passable slope. Right for hydrology and terrain analysis; wrong for anything that needs the structure.
- **Last return:** often used as a proxy for lowest, but a last return from a dense canopy or a bridge deck is still on the object; it is a sensor artefact, not a semantic choice.
- **Specification-defined:** the USGS LBS keeps bridge decks out of the bare-earth DEM but keeps dams and culverted road embankments in it ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md)); CityGML's terrain intersection curve and LoD semantics define where a building meets the ground and which surfaces are modelled.

Cliffs deserve special mention because they are the overhang case that most often goes unnoticed. A lidar point cloud of an overhanging sea cliff contains points on the cliff top, on the overhanging face, and on the shore platform beneath the overhang (if the scan angle or a terrestrial scan reached it). Gridding by minimum keeps the platform and the top and interpolates a slope between them that does not exist; gridding by maximum keeps the overhang edge and buries the platform. Cliff retreat measured from such grids is biased by the width of the overhang. Terrestrial and boat-based lidar, which see the face from the side, produce a cloud that is genuinely three-dimensional and should be analysed as a mesh or a point cloud (M3C2 distances, [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)), not as a 2.5D grid.

The alternatives to collapse are to keep the third dimension: a **classified point cloud** (every surface is present, with class labels saying which is which); a **TIN or mesh** that can fold over itself (true 3D surfaces, at the cost of losing raster tooling); **voxels** (occupancy per volume element, natural for canopy and caves, memory-hungry); **multi-layer rasters** (a DTM plus a DSM plus a "second surface" layer such as a bridge-deck raster or a canopy-base raster, which handle the two-surface case cheaply); and semantic 3D city models (CityGML, 3D Tiles) in which bridges, tunnels, and buildings are objects with their own geometry ([Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md), [Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)). For most terrain users the pragmatic answer is DTM + DSM + the classified cloud; for cliffs, caves, and structures, a mesh; for the rare case where the question is genuinely volumetric (how much rock overhangs, how much canopy volume), voxels.

Whichever rule is chosen, the product metadata must state it, and the mask layer should distinguish cells where the rule actually made a choice (a second surface existed) from cells where it did not. A user who knows that a DTM collapsed bridges can add them back from a bridge layer; a user who does not know will route a flood model through a deck or a drone under it.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Void", "NoData", "gap", and "hole" are used interchangeably but are not the same. A **void** is a location where the sensor made no usable measurement. **NoData** is a raster value flag that may mark voids, or masked-out water, or the area outside the project boundary, or cells deliberately removed in editing. A **holiday** in hydrography is a void relative to a coverage requirement. A **gap** in a point cloud may be a void or merely low density. A filled cell is none of these in the raster—it has a value—and only a mask records that it was once a void. Read the product's data dictionary for which meaning NoData carries, and never assume NoData = unobservable.

## 35.3 Void filling

A void in a delivered grid is usually filled, because most users and most software cannot cope with holes. The question is how, with what error, and whether anyone downstream can tell.

**Interpolation from the void's rim** is the default and the worst for large voids. Inverse-distance weighting, splines, kriging, and triangulation all produce a smooth surface constrained only by the edge cells; inside a void larger than the terrain's correlation length they revert toward a plane or a smooth bowl, and the error grows with void size and with terrain roughness. Reuter, Nelson, and Jarvis (2007) tested methods on SRTM voids by artificially voiding known terrain and found that for small voids (a few pixels) in gentle terrain almost any method works, that for large voids in mountains the error of pure interpolation reached hundreds of metres, and that the best choice depended on void size and relief—a result that generalises to any DEM. Interpolation is appropriate for voids a few cells across; beyond that it manufactures terrain ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)).

**Auxiliary-DEM fill** replaces the void with values from another DEM—coarser, older, or from a different sensor—after registering it to the primary. SRTM version 3 (NASADEM's predecessor) filled voids with ASTER GDEM and other sources; the Copernicus DEM fills with ASTER, SRTM, AW3D30, and national DEMs as recorded in the FLM; national lidar programs fill water and small voids from earlier photogrammetric DEMs. The fill's error is the auxiliary DEM's error plus the registration error, and its *character*—its resolution, its surface definition (DSM vs DTM), its epoch—differs from the primary's. A 30 m fill inside a 1 m lidar DTM is a visible patch of smoothness; a 2011 fill inside a 2020 DEM can contain a building that has since been demolished. The FLM concept—a per-pixel source code—is the minimum acceptable documentation.

**Delta surface fill** (Grohman, Kroenung, and Strebeck, 2006) addresses the seam problem of auxiliary fill. Rather than pasting the auxiliary values into the void, it computes the difference between the primary and the auxiliary DEM in a band around the void, interpolates that difference surface (the "delta") across the void, and adds it to the auxiliary DEM inside the void. The result matches the primary at the rim with no step, preserves the auxiliary's terrain shape in the interior, and absorbs the systematic offset (datum, bias) between the two sources. It was developed for SRTM and remains the standard for filling with a lower-quality source; its residual error is the auxiliary DEM's relative error over the void's width plus the delta interpolation error. Dowding, Kuuskivi, and Li (2004) described the operational void-fill workflow for the NGA SRTM products.

**Learned inpainting** trains a convolutional or diffusion network to predict terrain inside masked regions from the surrounding terrain and, optionally, from auxiliary inputs (coarser DEM, imagery, slope). On benchmark voids it produces visually plausible, texture-consistent terrain with lower RMSE than interpolation; its failure mode is confident hallucination—ridges and gullies in realistic positions that are nonetheless wrong—and its error cannot be bounded from the output alone. Where used, it should be validated with held-out voids in similar terrain and the filled cells flagged like any other fill ([Chapter 45](ch45-super-resolution.md)).

Whatever the method, the error inside a fill is spatially correlated and not reflected in the dataset's headline accuracy, which is tested against checkpoints that are almost never inside voids. A filled region in a flood model is a region where the model's inputs are a guess; a filled region in a volume calculation contributes an unknown bias. The product therefore needs a **fill mask**—per cell, measured vs filled, with the fill method and source—and the accuracy report should state accuracy for measured cells and, separately and honestly, an estimate for filled cells. The mask is not optional; a filled DEM without one is a DEM whose accuracy statement is false over an unknown area.

> **Worked example.** A 1 m lidar DTM has a 60 m × 40 m void under a dense evergreen stand on a hillslope of 15° with local roughness (σ of residuals from a 10 m plane) of 0.4 m. Rim interpolation with a thin-plate spline reproduces the planar slope but not the roughness; its expected RMSE inside the void is roughly the terrain's residual σ at the void's half-width—for a correlation length of 10–20 m, essentially the full 0.4 m plus any curvature error, say 0.5–0.8 m. An auxiliary 10 m photogrammetric DTM with 1.2 m RMSE, applied by delta surface fill, yields an error of about the auxiliary's *relative* error over 60 m (perhaps 0.6–0.8 m) rather than its absolute 1.2 m, because the delta absorbs the bias. Neither is close to the 0.1 m NVA reported for the dataset, and the 2,400 fill cells must be flagged so that a 95 % error statement is not silently applied to them.

> **Try it.** Fill small voids by interpolation, keep large ones as NoData, and write the mask. Expected outcome: `dtm_filled.tif` with voids up to 10 cells across filled; `fill_mask.tif` with 1 where filled, 2 where still void, 0 where measured.
>
> ```bash
> # 1. Mask of original voids
> gdal_calc.py -A dtm.tif --outfile=void.tif --calc="A==-9999" --NoDataValue=255 --type=Byte
> # 2. Interpolate voids up to 10 pixels from the rim (no smoothing iterations)
> gdal_fillnodata.py -md 10 -si 0 dtm.tif dtm_filled.tif
> # 3. Fill mask: 0 measured, 1 filled, 2 still void
> gdal_calc.py -A void.tif -B dtm_filled.tif --outfile=fill_mask.tif --type=Byte \
>   --calc="(A==1)*((B!=-9999)*1 + (B==-9999)*2)"
> gdalinfo -hist fill_mask.tif | tail -3
> ```
>
> Deliver `fill_mask.tif` alongside the DEM; the remaining class-2 voids are candidates for delta surface fill from an auxiliary DEM, which should then be recorded as class 3.

## 35.4 Voids as information, voids as failure

Not every void is a defect. A lidar void is the most reliable water detector in the dataset: a flat region with no returns, bounded by returns at a consistent elevation, is water with high probability, and hydro-flattening ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)) depends on it. A SAR decorrelation void over a glacier accumulation zone says the surface changed between passes; a decorrelation void in a forest says the canopy moved. A multibeam void behind a wreck has the shape of the wreck's shadow and tells the surveyor from which side the target still needs ensonifying. An optical matching void over a moving vehicle or a flock of birds marks a transient ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)). In each case the void carries information that filling destroys.

Voids as failure are the other kind: a flight line missing because of an aircraft fault, a swath holiday from a planning error, a dropout from a receiver gain setting, a cloud in a scene that should have been re-tasked. These are the voids that specifications limit and that reflights cure. Distinguishing the two requires looking at the void's shape and context—water voids are flat-bounded and follow hydrography, shadow voids are elongated away from the sensor, failure voids are rectangular or follow the platform track—and recording the diagnosis in the mask. A void mask with a "reason" attribute (water, shadow, cloud, dropout, out-of-swath, edited) is worth far more than a binary one, because the reason determines whether the void should be filled, how, and whether the feature behind it was observable at all.

## 35.5 Completeness metrics

**ISO 19157** (Geographic information — Data quality; 2013, revised as 19157-1:2023) defines **completeness** as one of the data-quality elements, with two sub-elements: **omission** (data absent that should be present) and **commission** (data present that should be absent). For a gridded DEM, omission is the void fraction relative to the specified coverage, and the standard's measures include counts and rates of missing items; commission would be cells with values outside the specified area or spurious filled cells presented as measured. ISO 19157 also provides the framework for reporting the result—the measure, the evaluation method, the scope, and the value—that metadata standards ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)) then carry.

Product specifications turn these into numbers. The **USGS Lidar Base Specification** limits voids in the point cloud: data voids, defined as areas without returns larger than a threshold (the LBS expresses it as voids larger than $(4 \times \text{NPS})^2$, where NPS is the nominal pulse spacing), are not permitted except where caused by water, low-reflectivity surfaces, or legitimate shadowing, and the spatial distribution of points is tested so that most cells of a grid at twice the NPS contain at least one point. The LBS also requires reporting the void areas and their causes. **IHO S-44 edition 6** does not speak of voids directly but of **bathymetric coverage** (the percentage of the area ensonified—100 % or 200 % for the stricter orders, meaning each point covered by one or two independent passes) and of **feature detection** (the smallest cubic feature that must be detected: a 0.5 m cube for Exclusive Order, 1 m for Special Order, and 2 m for Order 1a in depths to 40 m, 10 % of depth beyond) and **feature search** (the percentage of the area in which features of that size must be searched for). A coverage requirement is a completeness requirement, and a feature-detection requirement is a statement that a void smaller than the feature is tolerable but a void that could hide the feature is not. Hydrographic survey reports accordingly include coverage plots, holiday lists, and, for the stricter orders, evidence that the feature-search criterion was met (crossline overlap, target detection trials).

Beyond the binary, completeness should be reported as a map: the per-cell measurement count (points per cell, scenes per pixel, beams per node) is the most useful single quality layer a DEM can carry, because zero means void, one means unverified, and many means redundancy was available for checking. SRTM's "number of looks," ASTER GDEM's "NUM" layer, AW3D30's stacking count, Copernicus's FLM/EDM, and the LBS's required density raster are all instances.

> **Rule of thumb.** Report completeness three ways: the void fraction before filling (what the sensor saw), the fraction filled by each method (what was invented), and the per-cell measurement count (how well what was measured was checked). A headline "100 % coverage" with none of the three is a claim, not a metric, and it usually excludes exactly the places—surf zone, cliff foot, forest floor—where the question was hardest.

## 35.6 Overlap handling

The cure for voids at acquisition is redundancy: overlapping lidar strips, ascending and descending radar passes, multiple stereo scenes, overlapping multibeam swaths and crosslines. Redundancy then becomes a processing question, because the product must choose what to do where two or more measurements of the same place disagree.

For **lidar strips**, overlap is where calibration is checked: the vertical and horizontal offsets between strips in overlap regions are the primary diagnostic for boresight error, GNSS/IMU drift, and scanner scale, and the LBS requires a strip-alignment report with relative accuracy statistics (interswath RMSD, with thresholds of a few centimetres for the higher quality levels). The overlap points are legitimate measurements and most producers keep them all in the classified point cloud, flagging the overlap region (the LAS "overlap" bit, or the older practice of classifying overlap points as class 12) so that users who want a uniform density can exclude them. Gridding then averages, takes the lowest, or takes a weighted mean of points from both strips in a cell; a difference between strips of 10 cm becomes a 5 cm bias either way, but a *systematic* strip offset should be corrected in adjustment, not averaged away. Thinning overlap to the nominal density reduces file size and produces a uniform product but destroys the evidence of relative accuracy; keep the full cloud in the archive and thin for distribution if needed.

For **multibeam**, overlap between adjacent swaths and the deliberate **crosslines** run perpendicular to the main lines are the quality-control mechanism of the survey: crossline soundings compared with main-line soundings at crossings give the statistics—bias and spread as a function of depth—that demonstrate the TVU of S-44 has been met and reveal sound-speed, tide, and motion errors that nothing else catches. Hydrographic practice keeps every sounding, flagged accepted or rejected, and builds the surface by CUBE or similar estimators that weight each sounding by its propagated uncertainty, so that outer-beam soundings from one line contribute less than near-nadir soundings from the adjacent line ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)). Deleting overlap would discard the uncertainty evidence.

For **mosaicked optical and radar DEMs**, the overlap decision is per pixel: average all contributing scenes (ASTER GDEM), take a weighted mean by estimated quality (TanDEM-X/Copernicus using the HEM), or take the best single scene. Averaging reduces noise by $1/\sqrt{n}$ but blurs features if the scenes are misregistered or the surface changed between them; a weighted mean with a per-pixel count layer is the usual compromise, and the count layer belongs in the deliverable.

The general rule is that overlap is evidence, not redundancy to be discarded. It should be kept at least through QC, summarised in the quality report (relative accuracy by overlap region, crossline statistics, per-pixel counts), and thinned only in distribution products whose users have no need of it—with the full data archived and the thinning documented ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md) covers merging across datasets; here the overlap is within one).

## Then & now

Early DEMs had no voids because they were drawn: contours interpolated by a cartographer carried no record of where the photogrammetrist had actually seen the ground, and the first digitised DEMs (DTED from map sources, the USGS 7.5-minute DEMs) inherited surfaces that were complete and uniformly uncertain. SRTM in February 2000 made voids a public problem: a single-pass radar mission with layover, shadow, and unwrapping failures delivered a global product with holes in exactly the mountainous places where users most wanted it, and the decade of SRTM void-filling that followed—CGIAR-CSI's filled version, the delta-surface-fill method at NGA, the SRTM v3 release filled with ASTER GDEM—built the methods and the vocabulary of this chapter. ASTER GDEM (2009) introduced the per-pixel scene-count layer; TanDEM-X (2010–) and the Copernicus DEM (2019–) introduced the per-pixel height-error and fill-source masks as standard deliverables.

Lidar changed the character of voids from large and geometric to small and ubiquitous: shadows, water, and dropouts everywhere, filled by default during gridding, which is why the USGS LBS (2012 onward) specifies void thresholds, density rasters, and breakline-based water handling. Hydrography had been managing coverage and holidays since the first swath systems in the 1970s and had formalised feature detection and coverage percentages in S-44 by the 1998 edition. The current direction is toward per-cell provenance as a first-class layer—source, count, fill method, uncertainty—carried in cloud-native formats (COG with mask bands, BAG with uncertainty and tracking layers, [Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)) and toward learned fills whose plausibility makes the mask more necessary, not less.

## Validation & uncertainty

Voids, fills, and collapse rules are each a source of error that the headline accuracy statistic does not see, because checkpoints are placed on open, flat, measured ground. Validation must therefore target them deliberately.

**Measuring void fraction and its distribution.** Before any filling, compute the void fraction over the project area and stratified by land cover, slope class, and distance from the flight line or vessel track. A void fraction of 0.5 % overall that is 15 % in forest on north-facing slopes is a different product from one that is 0.5 % everywhere. Compare the stratified void fractions with the geometric predictors of §35.1 (shadow length from building heights and scan angle; layover from slope and incidence angle; multibeam coverage from depth and line spacing) to confirm that the voids are where physics puts them; voids elsewhere are failures to investigate.

**Estimating fill error.** The only honest estimator is **artificial voiding**: remove measured data in regions of similar size, shape, terrain, and land cover to the real voids, fill them with the production method, and compare with the removed truth. Report RMSE, mean error, and the 95th percentile of absolute error as functions of void size and terrain roughness; these become the accuracy statement for the filled cells. Reuter, Nelson, and Jarvis (2007) did exactly this for SRTM and found errors ranging from a few metres for small voids in flat terrain to over 100 m for large mountain voids. For lidar DTM voids under canopy, expect fill errors of 0.3–1 m for voids of tens of metres on moderate slopes; for cliff-foot voids, errors are bounded only by the cliff height. Where an auxiliary DEM was used, also report the registration residual between the two DEMs in the rim band, since a 1-pixel misregistration on a 20° slope is a 0.36 pixel-size height error that propagates into the fill.

**Testing the collapse rule.** For a DTM, identify bridges, overpasses, piers, and cliffs from vector data or the classified cloud, and verify that the rule stated in the metadata was applied: profiles across each bridge should show the river or road beneath, not the deck; cliff profiles should be compared with terrestrial scans or field measurements where an overhang is known. For a DSM, verify that the highest surface was retained and that the collapse did not create a spurious slope between a deck and the ground beside it (the "bridge ramp" artefact of interpolating from deck edge to bank).

**Verifying the mask.** Treat the fill mask as a product to be tested: sample filled cells and confirm that the raw point cloud or raw image stack has no measurement there; sample measured cells and confirm that it does. A mask with 2 % misclassification is tolerable; a mask that marks cells as measured where the gridding tool interpolated across a 30 m gap is a false mask, and the gridding search radius is the usual cause.

**Propagating to products.** A flood model, a volume, a viewshed, or a slope map computed over a region containing fills has an uncertainty that depends on the fill error and the fill area. For a volume, the bias is the mean fill error times the fill area; for a 10 ha fill with a 0.3 m mean error that is 30,000 m³. For a flood extent, run the model with the fill perturbed by ± its RMSE and report the extent range. Where the mask exists this is routine; where it does not, it is impossible, which is the strongest argument for the mask.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical error contributions in a 1 m lidar DTM, by cell category (1σ, representative):
>
> | Cell category | Error source | Magnitude |
> |---|---|---|
> | Measured, open ground | Sensor and trajectory | 0.05–0.10 m |
> | Measured, under canopy (sparse returns) | Fewer points, ground misclassification | 0.15–0.40 m |
> | Filled by rim interpolation, void < 10 m | Terrain roughness at that scale | 0.1–0.3 m |
> | Filled by rim interpolation, void 30–100 m | Roughness + curvature | 0.3–1.5 m, slope-dependent |

> | Water (hydro-flattened) | Not an error of the terrain; a datum/date statement | — |
>

> **Case file.** When SRTM's finished C-band product was released, users in the Himalaya, the Andes, and the Alaskan ranges found voids over the steepest terrain and glacier accumulation zones. CGIAR-CSI's hole-filled SRTM v4 (Jarvis et al., 2008) became the de facto global DEM for a decade; its voids were interpolated or, in later versions, filled from auxiliary DEMs including ones derived from topographic maps of varying vintage. Glacier-elevation-change studies that differenced a later DEM against filled SRTM in those regions faced a recognised hazard: inside the fills, the "SRTM" elevation was the auxiliary source's, so apparent change there reflected the map or DEM used for filling rather than February 2000 (Frey and Paul, 2012, evaluate SRTM and ASTER GDEM void and artefact issues for glacier-inventory work). NASA's SRTM v3 (2013) and NASADEM (2020) distribute the fill source per pixel precisely so that such cells can be excluded from change analysis.

## Software

**Open source:** GDAL (`gdal_fillnodata.py` for rim interpolation with a maximum distance; `gdal_calc.py` for masks; `gdalwarp` for auxiliary-DEM registration and resampling); PDAL (`filters.range`, `filters.returns`, `filters.overlay`, density and coverage rasters via `writers.gdal` with `output_type=count`); WhiteboxTools (`FillMissingData`, `LidarPointDensity`, `LidarTileFootprint`); GRASS GIS (`r.fillnulls` with RST or bilinear; `r.mapcalc` for masks; `r.in.lidar` density); SAGA (Close Gaps, Close Gaps with Spline, Close Gaps with Stepwise Resampling); xdem (Python; artificial voiding experiments and fill-error assessment are straightforward with its DEM class); MB-System (`mbgrid` with configurable interpolation distance, `mbeditviz`, coverage and crossline tools; `mbgrid -C` controls the fill extent); CloudCompare (mesh reconstruction for overhangs, M3C2); Open3D / PyMeshLab for meshes; delta surface fill can be implemented in a few lines of numpy over the registered rasters. Caveat: every gridding tool interpolates by default; set the maximum search distance explicitly or voids become invisible.

**Free but closed:** NASADEM and Copernicus DEM auxiliary layers (NUM, FLM, EDM, HEM) and the LBS density rasters are products, not tools, but are the reference for what a mask should contain.

**Commercial:** Terrasolid TerraScan/TerraModeler (strip overlap QC, void reporting); ArcGIS (`Fill`-style tools interpolate across NoData silently unless masked; the LAS dataset tools report density); Global Mapper (void fill from secondary DEM); CARIS HIPS and QPS Qimera (multibeam coverage, holiday detection, crossline statistics, CUBE surfaces with per-node counts). Caveat: "fill holes" buttons in GIS packages rarely write a mask.

## Standards & guides

- **ISO 19157:2013 / ISO 19157-1:2023,** *Geographic information — Data quality.* Completeness (omission/commission) as a quality element; measures, evaluation methods, and reporting structure.
- **USGS Lidar Base Specification** (v2.1, 2020, with online revisions). Void definition relative to nominal pulse spacing, allowable void causes, density-raster and spatial-distribution tests, strip-alignment (relative accuracy) reporting, overlap flagging.
- **IHO S-44 Edition 6.1.0 (2022),** *Standards for Hydrographic Surveys.* Bathymetric coverage percentages, feature detection and feature search by order, crossline and uncertainty evidence.
- **Copernicus DEM Product Handbook** (Airbus/ESA, current issue). Definitions of the Editing Mask (EDM), Filling Mask (FLM), Height Error Mask (HEM), and Water Body Mask (WBM).
- **NASADEM / SRTM v3 user guides** (NASA LP DAAC). Per-pixel source and number-of-looks layers; fill provenance.
- **ASPRS LAS 1.4 (R15, 2019).** Overlap bit flag; class 12 (overlap) in legacy usage.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023).** Vegetated and non-vegetated accuracy classes; implicitly excludes filled cells from NVA testing unless stated.
- **OGC BAG (Bathymetric Attributed Grid) specification.** Elevation plus uncertainty and optional tracking-list layers for hydrographic grids.

## Pitfalls

- **Filled voids indistinguishable from measured cells.** The gridding tool interpolated across gaps and no mask was written. Detect by comparing the DEM's valid extent with the point density raster; avoid by limiting search radius and emitting a fill mask.
- **Collapsing a bridge deck onto the river.** A DSM or a careless DTM keeps the deck; the flood model sees a dam. Detect with profiles at bridge locations from a transport layer; avoid by applying and documenting the collapse rule.
- **Cloud-filled DSM patches from a different year.** A mosaic fills cloud with another scene; a building appears or disappears inside the patch. Detect with the per-pixel date or count layer; avoid by delivering it and by excluding multi-date cells from change analysis.
- **"100 % coverage" that excludes the surf zone, the cliff foot, or the forest floor.** The claim is relative to an undefined area. Detect by asking for the void map before filling; avoid by reporting completeness stratified by land cover and depth.
- **Interpolating a 100 m mountain void from its rim.** The fill is a smooth bowl in terrain that is anything but. Detect by artificial voiding in similar terrain; avoid by delta surface fill from an auxiliary DEM and by flagging the cells.
- **Cliff retreat measured from a 2.5D grid of an overhanging cliff.** The grid invents a slope between top and platform. Detect by comparing with a terrestrial scan or mesh; avoid by analysing cliffs in 3D (M3C2).
- **Thinning strip overlap before QC.** The relative-accuracy evidence is gone. Detect by the absence of interswath statistics in the report; avoid by keeping overlap through adjustment and QC, thinning only for distribution.
- **Treating NoData as "unobservable."** NoData may be masked water, project boundary, or editing. Detect by reading the data dictionary; avoid by delivering a reasoned void mask.
- **Differencing fills between epochs.** Apparent change inside a fill is the difference between two auxiliary sources. Detect with the fill masks of both epochs; avoid by excluding filled cells from change maps.
- **Rejecting outer beams without re-planning line spacing.** The effective swath narrows and holidays appear between lines. Detect with the coverage plot after processing, not before; avoid by planning with the processing swath, not the nominal one.
- **Learned inpainting judged by appearance.** The fill looks like terrain, so it is trusted. Detect with held-out voids; avoid by validating and flagging like any other fill.

## Key takeaways

- Every sensor has a void geometry you can predict: lidar shadow $h\tan\theta$, SAR layover and shadow from slope versus incidence, multibeam holidays from swath versus depth, optical cloud and texture, bathymetric lidar turbidity. Use the prediction to plan overlap and to judge whether a void was ever observable.
- A grid holds one value per cell; overhangs, bridges, piers, canopy, and caves require a collapse rule. State it, mask where it acted, and move to meshes, voxels, or classified clouds when the third dimension is the question.
- Void filling manufactures terrain. Rim interpolation is acceptable for a few cells; beyond that, delta surface fill from a registered auxiliary DEM is the standard, and learned inpainting must be validated with held-out voids.
- Every DEM needs a per-cell source/void/fill mask; without it the accuracy statement is false over an unknown area.
- Voids are often information—water, change, shadow geometry, transients—and a void mask with a reason attribute preserves what filling would destroy.
- Report completeness as ISO 19157 omission/commission, as void fraction before filling stratified by land cover and slope, and as a per-cell measurement count; meet the LBS void thresholds and S-44 coverage and feature-detection requirements where they apply.
- Overlap is evidence of relative accuracy, not redundancy; keep it through QC, publish the overlap statistics, and thin only for distribution.
- Estimate fill error by artificial voiding in similar terrain and publish it separately from the measured-cell accuracy.
