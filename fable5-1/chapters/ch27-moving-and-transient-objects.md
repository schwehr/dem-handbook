# Chapter 27 — Moving and transient objects during collection

> **Part VI — Planning and operating surveys.** The second planning chapter: what to do about the things that are measured but are not terrain — vehicles, vessels, people, animals, cranes, construction, debris, fire, smoke, steam, spray — and how external tracking data (AIS, ADS-B) can help find and mask them.

**In this chapter.** Every survey records objects that were present for seconds, hours, or months and that no user wants in a DEM. You will be able to classify transient objects by their time scale and by their 2.5D consequence (a parked car is a 1.5 m hill; a moored ship is a 10 m shoal or a 20 m building); choose detection and removal strategies — classification, multi-pass temporal filtering, motion cues in scan lines, dynamic masks in photogrammetry, learned object detectors — and state the uncertainty each leaves behind; use AIS and ADS-B rigorously as spatio-temporal masks and QC evidence, knowing what the messages contain, how accurate their positions and times are, what the antenna-offset and reporting-interval limits are, who does not broadcast, and why the verdict is "useful, not sufficient"; handle obscurants and the false returns they create; decide whether stockpiles, dumps, and snow piles are terrain for your product; treat waves, wakes, and ice in bathymetric data; and deliver the masks you applied as a product.

## 27.1 Taxonomy by time scale

Transient objects are a classification problem with a time dimension: the first question is not "what is it?" but "how long was it there relative to the sampling?", because the answer determines which detection method can see it.

**Seconds.** Moving vehicles, trains, aircraft, birds, waves, wakes, people walking, blowing debris. These persist for less time than a swath or an image pair takes to cover them, so they appear *inconsistently* between overlapping observations: a car present in one strip is absent from the next; a moving car in a stereo pair appears at two positions and is reconstructed at a false height by the parallax; a bird is a single isolated return. Inconsistency between passes is the detection signal.

**Hours.** Parked cars, moored and anchored ships, aircraft on aprons, tower cranes (which move their jibs between passes but keep their masts), market stalls, event tents, trucks at a loading dock, livestock in a field. These are stable across one acquisition — every strip and every image sees them in the same place — so overlap does not reveal them, and they survive into a DSM looking like built structures. Only shape, context, and classification (or an external record of what was there) separate them from terrain and permanent features.

**Days to months.** Construction and demolition, excavations, scaffolding, stockpiles, dumps and trash piles, snow piles and plough berms, fire scars and burned canopy, flood debris, war damage, temporary roads and laydown yards. These are stable across an acquisition and often across repeat acquisitions, and they are genuine surfaces at the time of measurement. Here the question changes from detection to **definition**: is a stockpile terrain? For a volumetric survey it is the product; for a national DTM it is an object to remove; for a change-detection series it is the signal ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md), [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)).

The **2.5D consequence** is what makes transients matter. A DEM stores one height per cell, so an object on the ground becomes terrain unless something removes it: a car is a 1.5 m hill 4.5 m long; a bus is a 3.5 m ridge; a moored bulk carrier is a 15–20 m plateau above the water in a DSM and, if a sonar swath catches its hull, a shoal with the least depth at the keel; a crane is a 50 m spike; a cooling-tower plume is a floating cloud of returns at 100–300 m. In a flood-model DTM a row of parked cars is a levee; in a chart a hull is a hazard that is not there.

| Object | Typical height | Footprint | Persistence | Detectable by overlap? | Typical fate if untreated |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moving car | 1.5 m | 2 m × 4.5 m | seconds | yes (strip mismatch, stereo ghost) | smear or blob in DSM; usually filtered from DTM |
| Parked car | 1.5 m | 2 m × 4.5 m | hours | no | DSM blob; DTM "hill" if ground filter is loose |
| Truck/bus | 3–4 m | 2.5 m × 12–18 m | minutes–hours | sometimes | DSM; may be classified as building |

## 27.2 Detection and removal

No single method handles all time scales; production workflows layer several, each leaving a residual the uncertainty statement must acknowledge.

### 27.2.1 Classification

The ASPRS LAS specification (1.4, R15) reserves class 7 for **low noise** and class 18 for **high noise**; most transients that are detected are placed in one of these, in class 1 (unclassified, i.e. not ground), or in a user-defined class (64–255) with a documented meaning. The USGS Lidar Base Specification requires that noise be classified so that it does not enter the bare-earth surface and that classification be consistent across the project; it does not define a "vehicle" class, so a project that wants one must declare it. Ground filters — progressive morphological filtering, cloth simulation, Axelsson's progressive TIN densification ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)) — remove most parked cars from a DTM because a car's 1.5 m step over 2 m violates the slope and distance thresholds, but they fail at the margins: cars against kerbs or retaining walls, cars under trees where the ground has few returns, cars on sloping ramps, and dense parking where the filter's window is smaller than the lot and the roofs become a plausible "ground" plateau. Cattle at 1.5 m on open pasture are removed; sheep at 0.8 m in rough grass may not be. The DSM retains all of this by definition unless the specification says otherwise.

### 27.2.2 Multi-pass and temporal filtering

Where the same ground is observed more than once — strip overlap, cross-strips, repeat passes in mobile mapping, image overlap in SfM, repeat satellite acquisitions — a per-cell **temporal median** or minimum removes objects present in a minority of observations. A moving car present in one of three strips disappears under a median; a parked car present in all three does not. Mobile lidar applies the same logic to forward and rear scanners a few seconds apart. The residual: a median needs at least three observations, lowers effective density, and leaves a lot full of parked cars in every pass untouched. In satellite stereo and tri-stereo, moving vehicles produce **ghosts** — the along-track time separation (tens of seconds for Pléiades or WorldView stereo pairs) means a car at 20 m s$^{-1}$ moves 400–800 m between images, so it either fails to match (a hole) or matches to the wrong object (a spike); left–right consistency checks reject most, and the rest are small but numerous along highways.

### 27.2.3 Motion cues in lidar

A scanning lidar samples a moving object across successive scan lines, so an object moving along-track is stretched or compressed and an object moving across-track is sheared: a car at 30 m s$^{-1}$ scanned by a 100 Hz oscillating mirror is displaced 0.3 m between consecutive lines, and its roof outline shows a parallelogram skew that no static object has. Some processing chains flag **scan-line discontinuities** — height jumps that appear in one scan line and not in its neighbours — as moving-object candidates; the method is cheap but confuses motion with thin static structures (wires, fences; [Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)).

### 27.2.4 Dynamic masks in photogrammetry

SfM and MVS pipelines accept per-image masks that exclude pixels from matching and from texturing. Masks come from hand editing, semantic segmentation (vehicles, people, animals, water), or motion detection between frames. A masked object leaves a hole that the mesh or DSM interpolates across; if the object stood on flat pavement the interpolation is harmless, if it stood on a slope break the interpolated surface is wrong by up to the object's footprint times the slope. Record which images were masked where.

### 27.2.5 Learned object detection

Convolutional and transformer detectors trained on aerial imagery (vehicles, ships, aircraft, containers, cranes) and on point clouds (vehicle, pedestrian classes from autonomous-driving benchmarks) are now standard for finding transients at scale ([Chapter 42](ch42-object-detection-semantics.md)). Their outputs are probabilistic: a detector with 95 % recall on cars in open lots will drop to 60–70 % under trees and in shadow, and precision falls where the training domain differs from the survey (snow, unusual vehicles, different resolution). Use the detector to *propose* and a geometric test (height above local ground, footprint size, consistency across passes) to *confirm*, and report the detector's measured recall and precision on a labelled sample from this project, not the paper's benchmark figures. [Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md) compares learned and rule-based approaches.

> **Try it.** *Flag isolated elevated points and vehicle-sized blobs with PDAL.* Points more than 1 m above the ground surface, in connected clusters whose footprint is 3–25 m² and whose height above ground is 1–4 m, are vehicle candidates; isolated points far above neighbours are high noise.

Document class 64 as "vehicle candidate" in the metadata.

## 27.3 Ships and aircraft as artefacts, and AIS/ADS-B as masks

Vessels and aircraft are large, mobile, and — uniquely among transients — most of them broadcast their own positions, so an external time-stamped record exists that can be fused with the survey. This section states what that record contains, how accurate it is, how to use it, and why it is not enough alone.

### 27.3.1 How ships and aircraft appear in elevation data

A **moored or anchored ship** in lidar and photogrammetry is a solid object 10–40 m above the water that classifiers label building; in bathymetric lidar it also shadows the seabed beneath it. In **multibeam sonar** a hull is encountered when the survey vessel passes close alongside a berthed or anchored ship: the outer beams strike the hull below the waterline and return it as a shoal rising to the keel depth with an acoustic shadow behind it, and CUBE may accept the hull as a well-supported hypothesis because it is consistent across many pings. A hull passing *over* the swath does not appear, but its wake aerates the water column and causes dropouts and refraction artefacts for minutes. The uncaught result is a charted shoal in open water, or — after an editor removes the hull and nothing is re-run beneath — an unflagged gap. **Aircraft** appear parked on aprons in every DSM, and in flight in satellite imagery: band-sequential pushbroom sensors displace a moving aircraft between bands (the "rainbow aircraft" familiar from Sentinel-2), and in stereo pairs its along-track motion produces a parallax that reconstructs it as a spike or pit near airports. In InSAR, ships and aircraft are strong transient scatterers that decorrelate.

### 27.3.2 What AIS gives

The **Automatic Identification System (AIS)** is a VHF data link defined by ITU-R Recommendation M.1371 (first version 1998; current M.1371-5, 2014) ⟨H⟩ and mandatory under SOLAS Chapter V for ships of 300 gross tonnage and above on international voyages, cargo ships of 500 GT and above, and all passenger ships, phased in from 2002 and completed in 2004 ⟨H⟩; national rules extend it (in the United States, 33 CFR 164.46 brought most commercial vessels of 65 ft and above into AIS from 2016). **Class A** transponders send position reports (messages 1, 2, 3) at an interval set by motion: every 3 min when at anchor or moored below 3 kn; every 10 s at 0–14 kn (3⅓ s when turning); every 6 s at 14–23 kn (2 s when turning); every 2 s above 23 kn. **Class B** units (fishing vessels, yachts, small commercial craft) report every 30 s under way above 2 kn and every 3 min otherwise (messages 18/19). Static data (message 5 for Class A, 24 for Class B), every 6 min, give name, call sign, IMO number, type, and the **antenna position on the hull** as four distances A (to bow), B (to stern), C (to port), D (to starboard) in whole metres (A, B ≤ 511 m; C, D ≤ 63 m), from which a hull footprint can be built if heading is known.

Each position report carries latitude and longitude in units of 1/10,000 of a minute (≈ 0.19 m in latitude), speed over ground to 0.1 kn, course over ground to 0.1°, true heading in whole degrees (511 if no heading sensor — common on Class B and many small Class A installations), a **position-accuracy flag** (1: source reports better than 10 m, typically DGNSS; 0: worse or unknown), and a **time stamp that is only the UTC second** of the fix (0–59; 60 unavailable, 61 manual, 62 dead reckoning, 63 inoperative). The full time must be reconstructed from the receiver's clock at reception, with the seconds field disambiguating within the minute. Positions come from the ship's own GNSS, usually code-only with or without differential corrections, so a flagged position is good to a few metres and an unflagged one to perhaps 10–30 m, before any error in the operator-entered antenna offsets, which are frequently zero or stale. Data reach you through terrestrial receivers (20–40 nmi line of sight, with slot collisions and lost reports in dense ports), satellite AIS (global, with message loss and latency of minutes to hours — irrelevant when post-processing against an archive), and aggregators and archives (commercial feeds; coast-guard archives; the Global Fishing Watch public dataset ⟨H⟩, Kroodsma et al. 2018). Decoders are mature: `libais` (2010) ⟨H⟩ and `pyais` in Python, `gpsdecode` from gpsd.

### 27.3.3 What ADS-B gives

**Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B)** has aircraft broadcast GNSS-derived position, velocity, identity, and integrity on 1090 MHz Extended Squitter (worldwide) or 978 MHz UAT (United States below 18,000 ft). Equipage became mandatory in U.S. rule airspace on 1 January 2020 (14 CFR 91.225/91.227) and in Europe over 2020 for larger aircraft; light aircraft outside rule airspace, gliders (often FLARM instead), military aircraft, and most drones (Remote ID) are not required to broadcast, and some equipped aircraft transmit anonymised identity. Airborne position and velocity messages are each sent about twice per second, identification every 5 s. Position uses **Compact Position Reporting (CPR)**, which needs an even and an odd frame (or a reference position) to decode and gives about 5 m resolution; NIC, NACp, and SIL fields state integrity and accuracy, with NACp 10–11 meaning 95 % horizontal accuracy better than 10 m and 3 m respectively. **Altitude is barometric pressure altitude** referenced to 1013.25 hPa in 25 or 100 ft steps; geometric height above the WGS 84 ellipsoid appears only as a difference field in the velocity message and is not always present. The **OpenSky Network** (Schäfer et al. 2014) archives crowdsourced receptions with sub-second receiver time stamps and state vectors at 1 s or 10 s; ADS-B Exchange and FAA feeds are alternatives. Coverage is ground-receiver-limited, so low-altitude aircraft in remote terrain are often unrecorded.

### 27.3.4 Fusion: practical steps

The fusion is a spatio-temporal join between the survey's observation times and footprints and the vessel or aircraft tracks:

1. **One clock.** Lidar and sonar time stamps are GPS time; AIS and ADS-B archives are UTC. GPS led UTC by 18 s from 2017 through at least 2025; apply it explicitly ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)), and check whether the AIS archive time-stamps on reception, on decoding, or at one-minute granularity.
2. **Reconstruct tracks.** Decode messages 1/2/3/18/19 and 5/24 per MMSI; drop reports with time-stamp field ≥ 60 unless the receiver time fixes them; remove impossible speeds (> 50 kn) and default positions (0°N 0°E; 91°/181° "not available"); keep the accuracy flag. Interpolate to the survey times — linearly for gaps ≤ 10 s, with a turning model or rejection for longer gaps; for a ship at 12 kn (6.2 m s$^{-1}$) a 10 s gap bounds the interpolation error at about 30 m if the heading changed and a few metres if not. Moored vessels reporting every 3 min are stationary; use the mean position and allow for swinging at anchor (a circle of radius cable scope plus ship length).
3. **Build the footprint.** From A, B, C, D and true heading construct the hull rectangle, buffered by 10–20 % for flare and overhangs; without heading use course over ground when speed > 2 kn, otherwise a circle of radius max(A + B, C + D); with missing dimensions use a default by vessel type and widen the buffer. Add position uncertainty (10 m flagged, 30 m unflagged) plus the interpolation bound. For aircraft in flight a 100 m circle suffices; for parked aircraft use apron polygons, since ADS-B is often off on the ground.
4. **Intersect in time and space.** Each swath segment, ping, or image frame has a time and a ground footprint; join within the exposure or travel time plus the interpolation window. The output is a set of **candidate masks** with identity, time, buffer, and the sensor records touched.
5. **Confirm in the data.** Inside each candidate, test for the artefact — points more than 3 m above the water surface (lidar), a shoal consistent with a hull (sonar), a stereo ghost (imagery). Confirmed masks are applied by flagging; unconfirmed ones are kept as evidence that a vessel was nearby but not sampled.
6. **Reverse check.** Detect ship- and aircraft-like artefacts independently (height above water, size, shape, intensity) and look for a matching track. Artefacts with no track are non-broadcasting targets or non-vessel features (buoys, platforms, rocks, debris) and need a human decision; this unmatched set measures what AIS/ADS-B did *not* cover — the methodology Pelich et al. (2019) applied to Sentinel-1 ship detections against AIS at continental scale.
7. **Record everything** (Section 27.8): tracks used, time offset, buffer rules, each mask with provenance and decision, and the unmatched artefacts.

### 27.3.5 Limits and the verdict

AIS and ADS-B are **dependent** surveillance: the target reports its own position, from its own GNSS, through its own transponder, with operator-entered static data. Every known failure mode matters to a mask. **Non-broadcasting targets:** small craft, fishing boats below national thresholds, recreational vessels, barges under tow (the tug broadcasts; the barge 200 m behind it does not), naval vessels, vessels switched off; light aircraft, gliders, helicopters outside rule airspace, military, drones — in a busy harbour a substantial fraction of hulls have no track. **Reporting interval:** 2–10 s under way and 3 min moored; 0.5 s for aircraft but with reception gaps. **Antenna offsets and heading:** A/B/C/D often zero or stale after a refit; without heading the orientation is unknown and the polygon can be off by a ship length. **Position quality:** code-only GNSS, multipath against the ship's own superstructure or a quay, and increasingly **jamming and spoofing** — documented GNSS spoofing episodes have placed ships kilometres inland, and AIS itself has no authentication (Balduzzi et al. 2014), so treat tracks that disagree with the data as suspect rather than as truth. **Message loss and latency:** slot collisions in dense traffic, satellite detection probability well below one per pass, archive gaps, terrestrial coverage ending 20–40 nmi offshore. **Time base:** seconds-only stamps, drifting receiver clocks, a forgotten 18 s GPS–UTC offset that moves a 12 kn ship 110 m.

A well-built mask therefore has a positional uncertainty of roughly 10–50 m for a broadcasting target under way and 10–20 m for a moored one — comparable to a ship's beam, not to a sounding's THU — and a completeness anywhere from about 95 % of large commercial hulls to under half of all vessels present. The verdict: **AIS and ADS-B are valuable for masking and QC evidence — they tell you where to look, help decide whether a blob is a ship or a reef, provide a time-stamped record for the metadata, and let you estimate how many transients you did not catch — but they are not sufficient on their own to remove vessel and aircraft artefacts.** Confirm every removal in the data and examine the unmatched set by other means.

> **Worked example.** *Mask uncertainty for a moving ship.* A 180 m container ship at 14 kn (7.2 m s$^{-1}$) reports every 6 s with the accuracy flag set (≤ 10 m) and A = 150, B = 30, C = 12, D = 20 (so the antenna is 150 m from the bow, hull 180 m × 32 m). A lidar strip crosses the hull 2.7 s after the last report; linear interpolation along a steady course moves the position 19.4 m; a heading uncertainty of 2° over 150 m to the bow adds $150 \times \sin 2° = 5.2$ m across-track at the bow. Buffer: 10 m (position) + 5 m (interpolation residual, allowing for a 1 kn speed error over 2.7 s plus course jitter) + 5 m (heading) ≈ 20 m, so the mask polygon is the 180 m × 32 m rectangle grown by 20 m on all sides: 220 m × 72 m, about 2.7 times the hull area. Points inside it more than 3 m above the water surface are flagged as vessel; points inside it at water level are left as water. If the same ship had no accuracy flag and no heading (Class B behaviour), the buffer grows to about 45 m and the orientation comes from COG, and the mask area is five times the hull's.

## 27.4 Fires, smoke, steam, dust, spray, fog

Obscurants are transients made of particles, and sensors respond to them differently. **Topographic lidar** at 1,064 or 1,550 nm penetrates thin smoke and haze with attenuated ground returns; in dense smoke, steam, or dust the pulse scatters from the particle cloud itself, producing returns at the plume (a diffuse cloud tens to hundreds of metres above the source) and weak or missing ground returns beneath. Full-waveform systems record the plume as a broad low-amplitude leading echo that a decomposer can flag; discrete-return systems record first returns that classifiers treat as vegetation or high noise. **Industrial steam** from cooling towers, power stations, refineries, and mills is the chronic case: continuous, large (50–300 m tall in cold weather), and present in every pass, so temporal filtering cannot remove it; statistical outlier filters remove its sparse top but not its dense base, which survives as a floating lump 20–50 m above the stack — and, where the surrounding roofs have been removed and the plume base is contiguous with the stack, can end up as the lowest surface and pass into the bare-earth DTM as a rounded mound. The clean treatment is a plume mask from the known source locations, sized for the wind recorded in the field log, applied to all returns above the stack top; the residual is whatever the plume occluded, which is a void to report, not to interpolate across.

**Photogrammetry** cannot match through smoke or steam and matches *on* a dense textured plume, producing holes, bumps, and in stereo a surface that follows the plume top; wind-blown plumes differ between frames and fail left–right consistency checks, so the typical symptom is a hole rather than a hill. **Heat shimmer** over hot ground, flares, and stacks degrades image matching and adds centimetre-level random ranging noise to lidar; it is not a bias at survey ranges but inflates local noise. **Radar** at X-band and longer is effectively immune to smoke, dust, and dry steam, one reason SAR DEMs are the product of choice for active volcanic and wildfire landscapes; wet plumes and rain cells attenuate X- and Ku-band and leave gaps. **Active fire** also changes the surface during acquisition, and burned canopy days later has a different DSM and a differently penetrable DTM ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md)). **Sea spray and surf** create false surfaces in bathymetric lidar and beach photogrammetry: breaking waves are bright, moving, textured, and 1–3 m above still water, and reconstruct as a ridge along the surf zone that moves between passes. **Fog** over water returns the pulse from the fog top; topographic lidar flown above a valley fog layer maps the fog as terrain — a recurring autumn artefact recognisable as an unnaturally flat, featureless "ground" filling a valley to a common level.

## 27.5 Construction and destruction

Construction sites are the densest concentration of transient geometry a survey encounters: excavations that are negative terrain, spoil heaps that are positive, cranes, scaffolding, formwork, hoardings, site vehicles, portable buildings, and a surface that changes weekly. For a national DTM the convention (LBS, most European specifications) is that the ground at the time of acquisition is the ground — an excavation is a hole and a spoil heap is a hill — but objects (cranes, machinery, scaffolding, temporary buildings) are removed; the metadata should carry the acquisition date so users can date the surface. **Tower cranes** cause a specific artefact: the mast is a thin vertical object that may be entirely missed by sparse lidar or reduced to a few points, while the jib (50–70 m long, 2–3 m deep) slews between passes and appears as a thin elevated ridge in a different azimuth in each strip; it can be falsely used as a tie feature in strip adjustment and appears as a radial spoke in a DSM hillshade. Jib points belong in high noise or a crane class; the mast is a building-like object to remove from a DTM. Scaffolding and formwork are porous multi-return structures that ground filters sometimes penetrate to a false "ground" at deck level.

Demolition, disaster damage, and war damage invert the problem: the pre-event DSM contains buildings that no longer exist, debris fields have heights of 2–10 m with no building footprint, and the DTM beneath rubble is unobservable. Change-detection products ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) built from before/after DSMs are the right representation; a single-epoch DTM through a debris field is a guess and should be flagged as such. **Dating the surface** — stating per tile or per object the acquisition date, and where possible the construction state from imagery — is the most useful thing a producer can do for users who will compare epochs.

## 27.6 Dumps, landfills, stockpiles, snow piles

These are legitimately changing surfaces, and the decision is about **definition**, not detection. A landfill's working face rises by metres per month; a quarry's stockpiles move daily; a port's coal and ore heaps change with every ship; a ski resort's snow piles and a northern city's plough berms are 5–15 m features from December to April; construction material yards hold aggregate heaps for years. For a volumetric or operational product they are the terrain of interest ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)); for a national bare-earth DTM, snow is removed by specification (survey snow-free), stockpiles of material are usually retained as terrain (they are ground, and nobody can draw the line between a stockpile and a spoil heap and a natural mound), and trash piles and dumps are retained with the same logic; for a hydrological DTM a stockpile in a floodplain is an obstacle that should remain, while a snow pile should not. The specification must say which; where it is silent, retain the surface as measured, record the acquisition date, and let users decide, rather than silently "cleaning" a heap that a user needed. Illegal dumps are increasingly detected *from* DEM differencing ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md)); removing them destroys that signal.

## 27.7 Water surface motion: waves, wakes, boat traffic, ice

In bathymetric lidar the water surface is both a transient and a reference: refraction correction requires the surface height and slope at each pulse, and a 10° wave face under a nadir pulse deflects the refracted path by about 7.5° (Snell's law, $n \approx 1.34$), shifting the bottom position by roughly 1.3 m horizontally and the depth by several centimetres in 10 m of water ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)). Systems estimate a local mean surface from the infrared or green surface returns over a window; wind waves add noise, swell adds a slowly varying bias if the window is short, and **wakes** from passing vessels add both a surface disturbance and a bubble layer that attenuates the green pulse for minutes — the usual symptom is a curved band of missing or shoaled bottom returns along the wake. In multibeam, other vessels' wakes aerate the water under the transducer and show as a short section of noisy or missing outer beams. In satellite-derived bathymetry, surf, wakes, and sun glint are per-image masks ([Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)). **Ice** is the seasonal version: floating ice in lidar and photogrammetry reconstructs as terrain at freeboard height, frozen lakes and rivers are perfectly flat "ground" that hydro-flattening then enforces, and ice foot and ridged shore ice create false shorelines; cold-region acquisitions need an ice-state record and a water mask ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)) built from the acquisition imagery, not a summer vector.

## 27.8 Documenting what was removed

A mask is a measurement decision and belongs in the deliverables. Every class of removal — vehicles, vessels, aircraft, plumes, cranes, wake dropouts, surf, ice — should be delivered as (i) a flag or class on the original observations (LAS classes 7/18 or documented user classes; the `withheld` flag for points excluded from products; sonar rejection flags preserved in the processed files), never by deletion; (ii) a vector or raster **mask layer** with, per feature, the reason code, the time or time range of the observations affected, the method (manual, rule, detector, AIS/ADS-B match), the uncertainty buffer, and the identity of any matched track; and (iii) a metadata statement ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)) of the rules, the software and versions, the detector's measured recall and precision on this project, and the count and area of masks by reason. Flag interpolated cells within masks in the DEM ([Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md)).

## Then & now

Long exposures erased pedestrians from nineteenth-century city photographs, and mid-century photogrammetrists flew early mornings to empty the streets; DEMs at 10–30 m posting rarely resolved vehicles, and the problem arrived with sub-metre lidar and imagery in the 2000s and national DTM programs that had to decide what "bare earth" meant in a parking lot. The ASPRS LAS format (2003) ⟨H⟩ gave noise its own classes, the `withheld` bit (LAS 1.1, 2005), and, in LAS 1.4 (2011), the `overlap` flag and extended classes that make non-destructive masking possible. External tracking matured in parallel: AIS was standardised in 1998 ⟨H⟩, mandated under SOLAS from 2002 ⟨H⟩, decoded by open tools such as `libais` from 2010 ⟨H⟩, received from orbit from about 2008–2010, and turned into a public global record by Global Fishing Watch (launched 2016; Kroodsma et al. 2018) ⟨H⟩. ADS-B followed a similar path a decade later: OpenSky's crowdsourced network from 2013 (Schäfer et al. 2014) and mandatory equipage in U.S. and European rule airspace in 2020. Satellite SAR ship detection matched against AIS (Pelich et al. 2019) established the methodology — detect in the data, match to the track, examine the unmatched — that this chapter recommends for elevation data. The current decade adds learned detectors that find vehicles, ships, cranes, and plumes at scale, and a growing expectation, as DEMs become inputs to automated decisions, that the masks be delivered as data rather than described in a report.

## Validation & uncertainty

Transient-object handling introduces two kinds of error and the validation plan must measure both: **misses** (objects left in the product: a car in the DTM, a hull in the chart) and **false removals** (terrain or permanent features taken out: a low wall classed as a vehicle, a rock pinnacle rejected as a hull), plus the **interpolation error** inside every mask where the surface beneath the object was never observed.

**Measure recall and precision on this project.** Draw a stratified sample of tiles — parking lots, highways, harbours, industrial sites, construction, open pasture — and have an analyst label transients from the imagery and point cloud. Compare with the automated result and report recall and precision per object class and per stratum with their binomial confidence intervals: 95 % recall estimated from 100 labelled cars has a 95 % interval of roughly 89–98 %; from 20 cars it is 75–100 %.

**Quantify what remains.** Convert the misses to a height-error contribution: if 5 % of 2,000 parked cars survive into a 1 m DTM, 100 cars × 9 m² × 1.5 m is 1,350 m³ of false volume and 900 cells with 1–1.5 m errors — negligible in the project RMSE, decisive for a flood model of that car park. Report missed transients as a separate blunder count with spatial distribution, not folded into the Gaussian accuracy statement; the NVA/VVA checkpoints are deliberately placed where there are no transients and say nothing about them.

**Quantify the interpolation inside masks.** For each mask, the interpolated surface's uncertainty is bounded by the surrounding terrain's roughness and slope times the mask size; on a flat lot it is centimetres, across a stockpile or a quay edge it is metres. Test by cutting synthetic masks of the same size distribution from unmasked terrain of the same type and measuring the interpolation error — a cheap, honest estimate of what the masked cells are worth.

**Validate the AIS/ADS-B fusion.** Report the time offset used and how it was verified (a moored ship whose hull is visible in the data is a time-independent check on position; a moving one whose displacement matches the predicted motion is a check on time); the match rate in both directions — tracks that produced confirmed artefacts, and artefacts that matched a track — and the unmatched artefact count with its disposition; the buffer rules and the fraction of mask area that is buffer rather than hull. Zero unmatched artefacts in a busy harbour means the reverse check is not looking.

**For bathymetry**, the specific tests are: no sounding retained inside a confirmed hull mask without re-survey beneath; every rejected hull area examined for seabed data from other lines or flagged as a gap; wake dropouts recorded and, where they coincide with a feature-detection requirement, re-run. An uncertain blob in a channel is investigated, not masked.

**What to report.** Per-class counts of objects detected, removed, and left; recall/precision per stratum with intervals; mask area and the fraction interpolated; the AIS/ADS-B match statistics; the environmental log for plumes, surf, ice; and the mask deliverable itself. [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) places these alongside the conventional accuracy statistics.

> **Uncertainty budget.** *Residual transient error in a 1 m urban DTM after a layered workflow (ground filter + multi-pass median + detector + manual review of flagged clusters); indicative values from project-scale experience, to be replaced by measured values for each project.*
>
> | Source | Typical residual | Where it concentrates |
> |---|---|---|
> | Parked cars surviving ground filter and detector | 1–5 % of cars, each a 1–1.5 m local error | tree-covered lots, kerbside on slopes |
> | Vehicles in DSM (by definition retained) | 100 % | everywhere; state it |
> | Moored hulls unmasked (no AIS, no detection) | few per harbour | small-craft marinas, barges |
> | Plume base surviving filters | one lump per unmasked stack | industrial sites in cold weather |
> | Crane jibs as thin ridges | one per crane per strip | construction sites |
> | False removals (walls, hedges, rocks as vehicles) | 0.5–2 % of removals | boundaries, rocky shores |
> | Interpolation inside masks | cm on flat ground; up to m on slopes/edges | quays, ramps, stockpiles |

## Software

**Open source:** PDAL (`filters.outlier`, `filters.hag_nn`, `filters.cluster`, `filters.range`, and the `withheld` flag for non-destructive masking; no built-in temporal median across strips — use per-strip rasters and `gdal_calc` or NumPy); CloudCompare (manual segmentation, cloth-simulation filter, cross-cloud distance for multi-pass comparison); `lidR` (ground filters, per-tile rasterisation, cluster metrics); OpenDroneMap (per-image masks via the `masks` folder); `libais` and `pyais` (AIS decoding, NMEA to structured records); `pyModeS` and `pyopensky`/the OpenSky REST and Trino APIs (ADS-B decoding and historical state vectors); Global Fishing Watch public data and API (vessel presence and identity; fishing-oriented, with gaps where terrestrial AIS is absent); MB-System (sounding flags preserved in processing; `mbedit`/`mbeditviz` for hull rejection); YOLO/Detectron-family detectors trained on aerial datasets (DOTA, xView) for proposals.

**Free but closed:** ADS-B Exchange (web and API, rate-limited); MarineTraffic/VesselFinder free tiers (recent positions only; historical data commercial).

**Commercial:** Agisoft Metashape and Pix4D (image masking, automatic masking by segmentation in recent versions); TerraSolid TerraScan (macro-based noise and vehicle classification; per-seat licensing); CARIS HIPS/Qimera (hull and wake editing with flag preservation; CUBE hypothesis review); commercial AIS archives (Spire, exactEarth/Kpler, MarineTraffic, Lloyd's List Intelligence) for complete historical satellite+terrestrial tracks; Esri ArcGIS Pro deep-learning object detection packages.

## Standards & guides

- ASPRS **LAS Specification 1.4 – R15** (2019): classes 7 (low noise) and 18 (high noise), user-defined classes 64–255, `withheld`/`overlap` flags.
- USGS **Lidar Base Specification** (2024 rev. A): noise classification, bare-earth definition, treatment of objects, void and classification-consistency requirements.
- ITU-R Recommendation **M.1371-5** (2014): AIS technical characteristics, message formats, reporting intervals, time-stamp and accuracy fields.
- IMO **SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19** (carriage of AIS) and IMO Resolution A.1106(29) (2015) *Guidelines for the onboard operational use of shipborne AIS* (static-data entry including antenna position).
- RTCA **DO-260B/C** (1090 ES ADS-B MOPS) and **DO-282B** (UAT); 14 CFR **91.225/91.227**: message content, integrity/accuracy fields, equipage.
- IHO **S-44** Ed. 6.1.0 (2022) and NOAA **HSSD** (2024): feature detection, investigation of anomalies, sounding rejection documentation.
- IHO **S-57/S-101** object catalogues (for what a charted obstruction is, versus a transient).

## Pitfalls

- **Cars baked into "bare-earth" DTMs in parking lots.** The ground filter's window is smaller than the lot, so roofs form a plateau that passes as ground. → Hillshade the DTM at low sun over commercial areas; add a detector pass and a minimum-footprint rule for ground plateaus 1–2 m above surroundings.
- **Moored hulls charted as shoals, or removed leaving unflagged gaps.** CUBE accepts the hull as consistent data; the editor rejects it and nobody records the hole. → Confirm with AIS/berth records, re-run beneath or flag the gap in the deliverable.
- **Steam plumes surviving filtering.** Dense plume bases are not statistical outliers. → Source-based masks from known stacks, sized from the wind in the field log; report the void beneath.
- **AIS positions trusted to the second and the metre.** Reports arrive every 2–10 s under way and every 3 min moored, with seconds-only time stamps and operator-entered antenna offsets. → Buffer by position flag, interpolation bound, and heading uncertainty; confirm in the data.
- **GPS–UTC offset forgotten in the fusion.** 18 s at 12 kn is 110 m. → Verify the time base against a moored vessel visible in the data.
- **Assuming every vessel broadcasts.** Barges, small craft, fishing boats, naval vessels, and dark targets do not. → Run the reverse match and report the unmatched artefacts.
- **Treating ADS-B barometric altitude as height.** Pressure altitude at 1013.25 hPa differs from geometric height by tens to hundreds of metres. → Use the geometric-height field where present or correct with QNH; for masks, use position only.
- **Deleting instead of flagging.** Removed points and rejected soundings vanish from the archive. → `withheld` flags, user classes, and preserved sonar flags; masks as deliverables.
- **Detector performance quoted from the paper.** Benchmark recall does not transfer to shadowed, snowy, or oblique data. → Measure on a labelled sample from this project and report intervals.
- **Silently "cleaning" stockpiles and dumps.** A user needed the volume or the dump location. → Follow the specification; where silent, retain the surface and date it.

## Key takeaways

- Transient objects are a classification problem with a time dimension: seconds-scale objects are inconsistent across passes and can be filtered; hours-scale objects are consistent and need shape, context, or external records; months-scale objects need a definition, not a detector.
- A DEM's 2.5D model turns every unremoved object into terrain — a car into a 1.5 m hill, a hull into a shoal — so the removal rules are part of the product definition and must be stated.
- Layer the methods (ground filter, multi-pass median, motion cues, image masks, learned detectors, manual review) and measure recall and precision per stratum on this project's data.
- AIS and ADS-B are valuable as spatio-temporal masks and QC evidence; their positions are good to 10–50 m, their times to seconds, their coverage incomplete, and their static data unreliable — use them to find and confirm, never as the sole basis for removal.
- Always run the reverse match: artefacts without tracks measure what the tracking data did not cover.
- Flag, never delete; deliver masks with reason, time, method, and uncertainty as data, and flag interpolated cells in the DEM.
- Date the surface: construction, stockpiles, and debris are legitimate terrain at an instant, and users comparing epochs need the instant.
