# Chapter 12 — GNSS for elevation work

> **Part IV — Positioning and orientation.** Having traced how positioning arrived at satellites ([Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md)), this chapter explains how a GNSS receiver turns signals into coordinates, why the height is the weakest of the three, and what it takes to make GNSS heights good enough to control and check a DEM.

**In this chapter.** GNSS positions nearly every modern elevation dataset: the lidar aircraft, the survey launch, the drone, the RTK rover that measures checkpoints, and the CORS station that ties them to the reference frame. This chapter covers constellations and signals, the three observables (code, carrier phase, Doppler), the error sources that make the vertical component two to three times worse than the horizontal, and the processing modes from single-point positioning through SBAS, RTK, PPK, PPP, and PPP-AR. It then turns to practice: infrastructure (CORS, IGS, NRTK, OPUS and its siblings), field procedures for control and checkpoints including the antenna-height blunder, GNSS on moving platforms, the fact that GNSS heights are ellipsoidal, threats such as jamming, spoofing, and ionospheric storms, and what to expect from low-cost and smartphone receivers. After reading it you should be able to write an uncertainty budget for a GNSS height, choose a mode and occupation length for a required accuracy, and audit someone else's GNSS control.

## 12.1 Constellations and signals

A **global navigation satellite system (GNSS)** is a constellation of satellites broadcasting ranging signals and the data needed to compute where each satellite was when it transmitted. Four global systems and two regional ones operate in the mid-2020s:

| System | Operator | Nominal constellation | Civil frequencies (MHz) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPS | United States | 24 baseline; 31 operational (approx.) | L1 1575.42, L2 1227.60, L5 1176.45 | L2C and L5 only on Block IIR-M and later |
| GLONASS | Russia | 24 | G1 ~1602, G2 ~1246 (FDMA); G3 CDMA on newer satellites | FDMA inter-frequency biases complicate ambiguity resolution |
| Galileo | European Union | 24 + spares; ~28 usable (approx.) | E1 1575.42, E5a 1176.45, E5b 1207.14, E6 1278.75 | High Accuracy Service (free PPP corrections) on E6 since 2023 |
| BeiDou (BDS-3) | China | 30 (MEO + IGSO + GEO) | B1C 1575.42, B2a 1176.45, B1I 1561.098, B3I 1268.52 | Global since 2020; regional PPP-B2b service |
| QZSS | Japan | 4 (7 planned) | L1, L2, L5, L6 (CLAS corrections) | Regional, Asia–Oceania; CLAS gives free PPP-RTK over Japan |
| NavIC (IRNSS) | India | 7 | L5 1176.45, S 2492.028; L1 on newer satellites | Regional, India and surroundings |

Satellite counts change with launches and retirements; check status pages before stating a number in a report. The practical effect of the multi-constellation era is geometric: a dual-frequency, four-system receiver at mid-latitude with a 10° mask typically tracks 25–40 satellites, against 7–10 for GPS alone in 2000 — better dilution of precision (§12.2), faster ambiguity resolution, and usable solutions in urban canyons and under partial canopy where GPS-only receivers lost lock.

Three **observables** come from each signal. The **pseudorange** (code) is the transit time of the ranging code times the speed of light: unambiguous but noisy, with a precision of about 0.1–3 m depending on code (wideband L5/E5 at the low end) and receiver. The **carrier phase** is measured to about 1 % of a wavelength (about 2 mm at L1's 19 cm): precise but ambiguous by an unknown integer number of cycles. The **Doppler** observable gives velocity to a few cm/s and is used to detect cycle slips and aid inertial integration ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). Survey-grade positioning is carrier-phase positioning; code positioning is for navigation.

**Dual- and triple-frequency** tracking matters because the ionospheric delay is dispersive, scaling as the inverse square of frequency: two frequencies let you eliminate it (§12.2) and three give additional combinations that make ambiguity resolution faster and more reliable. A single-frequency receiver, however many constellations it tracks, cannot remove the ionosphere and depends on a model or on short baselines to a base station.

## 12.2 Error sources and why the vertical is weak

Every GNSS range measurement is the geometric distance plus a sum of errors. The budget below is for a single-frequency, standalone receiver (the worst case), with the mitigation each processing mode provides in the last column; it is adapted from Misra & Enge (2011), Kaplan & Hegarty (2017), and Teunissen & Montenbruck (2017), and specific values vary by constellation, product, and conditions.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Typical 1σ range-error contributions per satellite, and how each is mitigated.
>
> | Error source | Standalone, broadcast products | After mitigation | How it is mitigated |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Satellite orbit (broadcast ephemeris) | 0.5–2 m (GPS); larger for GLONASS | 2–5 cm (IGS final/rapid), ~5 cm (real-time SSR) | Precise orbits; or differencing to a base (cancels for short baselines) |
> | Satellite clock (broadcast) | 0.5–2 m | 1–3 cm (IGS clocks at 30 s), ~5 cm real-time | Precise clocks; or differencing |
> | Ionosphere (slant, mid-latitude, quiet sun) | 2–10 m; 20–50 m+ at low latitude or during storms; ~50 % removed by Klobuchar | mm–cm with dual-frequency ionosphere-free combination (but noise amplified ~3×) | Dual-frequency; differencing over < 10–20 km; SBAS/NRTK ionospheric grids |
> | Troposphere (zenith total delay ~2.3–2.6 m) | 0.1–0.5 m residual after a blind model; wet component poorly modelled | 1–3 cm ZTD when estimated as a parameter; residual height error 2–3 × ZTD error | Models (Saastamoinen, VMF3/GPT3) + estimation of residual zenith wet delay with a mapping function |
> | Multipath (code) | 0.5–5 m | 0.1–1 m with narrow correlators, choke rings | Antenna design, siting, elevation mask, long occupations averaging |
> | Multipath (carrier phase) | up to ~5 cm (¼ wavelength bound); 0.5–2 cm typical | 0.2–1 cm after sidereal filtering/averaging | As above; it does *not* cancel in differencing |
> | Antenna phase-center offset/variation | 1–10 cm if ignored or mis-modelled | 1–3 mm with ANTEX calibrations | IGS ANTEX (igs20.atx) absolute calibrations; same model at base and rover |
> | Receiver noise (code / phase) | 0.1–3 m / 1–3 mm | Same | Averaging; modern wideband codes |
> | Cycle slips (phase) | Whole-cycle jumps (19–25 cm each) if undetected | Detected and repaired or re-initialized | Dual-frequency slip detectors (Melbourne–Wübbena, geometry-free); Doppler |
> | Earth tides, ocean loading, pole tide | Up to 40 cm (solid Earth tide, vertical) if ignored in PPP/long baselines | mm | IERS Conventions models (cancel in short baselines) |

These range errors become position errors through geometry. The least-squares solution for position and receiver clock from $n$ satellites uses the design matrix $G$ ($n \times 4$) of unit line-of-sight vectors and ones; the position covariance is $\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}^2 (G^\mathsf{T} G)^{-1}$, and the **dilution of precision** factors are the square roots of its diagonal: HDOP, **VDOP**, TDOP, and PDOP (see Mathematics). VDOP is systematically 1.5–2.5 times HDOP for a reason you can see from the sky: satellites surround the receiver horizontally, but none is ever *below* it. The vertical is constrained only from above and is strongly correlated with the receiver clock, because a common delay on all signals looks like the receiver being lower (or its clock later).

Three range errors compound the geometric weakness. The **troposphere** matters most for elevation work. The dry (hydrostatic) zenith delay (about 2.3 m at sea level) is modelled from surface pressure to a few millimetres, but the **wet delay** (0–40 cm) varies by centimetres in hours and must be estimated — yet a zenith delay error and a height error are nearly indistinguishable at high elevation angles, and the mapping function separates them only through low-elevation satellites, which are the noisiest and most multipath-prone. The rule from the literature (Rothacher 2002; Santerre 1991) is that a zenith delay error $\delta_{ZTD}$ produces a height error of roughly $2$–$3\,\delta_{ZTD}$ with a 10–15° cutoff, with a height–ZTD correlation of about −0.9. Short baselines cancel the troposphere only if base and rover are at similar heights; with a 500 m height difference the differential delay scales with height difference, not distance ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)). **Multipath** biases the vertical more than the horizontal because ground reflections arrive asymmetrically from below. And **antenna phase-center variations** are elevation-dependent, so an uncalibrated or mis-modelled antenna becomes a height bias of up to several centimetres — the reason NGS and IGS publish absolute calibrations and the reason base and rover must use the same calibration model with the correct antenna and radome codes.

The empirical outcome, confirmed across decades of CORS time series and campaign statistics, is that GNSS **vertical repeatability is 2–3 times worse than horizontal** in every mode: for 24 h static PPP, about 2–3 mm horizontal and 6–8 mm vertical; for RTK, about 1 cm + 1 ppm horizontal and 2 cm + 1 ppm vertical (manufacturer 1σ); for SBAS, about 1 m and 2 m; for standalone code, about 2–3 m and 4–6 m. These ratios are structural, not a matter of receiver price.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Accuracy 1 cm + 1 ppm" in a receiver datasheet is a 1σ (sometimes RMS) horizontal figure for a fixed RTK solution under open sky with a short baseline; the vertical figure is 1.5–2× larger and printed in smaller type. It excludes base-station coordinate error, antenna-height measurement, datum and geoid transformation, site multipath, and the possibility that the "fixed" solution is wrong. A 95 % vertical accuracy for a real RTK checkpoint survey is more like 3–5 cm. Report what you measured, not what the datasheet promised.

## 12.3 Modes: from SPP to PPP-RTK

**Single-point positioning (SPP)** uses code pseudoranges and broadcast orbits and clocks; accuracy is 2–5 m horizontal and 4–10 m vertical (95 %) for a modern multi-constellation receiver. It is the navigation mode of phones and cars and the "autonomous" fallback of survey receivers; its heights are not usable for DEM control.

**Differential GNSS (DGNSS)** and **SBAS** correct code observations with pseudorange corrections from a reference station (maritime beacon DGPS, now largely decommissioned) or from a geostationary satellite broadcasting corrections and integrity computed from a regional network (**WAAS**, **EGNOS**, MSAS, GAGAN, SDCM, BDSBAS). SBAS gives about 1 m horizontal and 1.5–2 m vertical (95 %) — the accuracy of a consumer drone when it says "GPS fixed" — fine for navigation, inadequate for all but the coarsest elevation work.

**Real-time kinematic (RTK)** positioning differences carrier phase between a base at known coordinates and the rover and resolves the double-differenced integer ambiguities on the fly (Mathematics). Once fixed, accuracy is about 1–2 cm horizontal and 2–3 cm vertical (1σ) relative to the base, plus about 1 ppm of distance, which becomes limiting beyond 10–20 km as ionospheric decorrelation makes fixing slow or unreliable. **Network RTK (NRTK)** replaces the single base with a network of CORS whose observations are interpolated to the rover, either as a **virtual reference station (VRS)** or as master-auxiliary (**MAC**) corrections; NRTK removes most of the distance dependence over the network's area and, critically, puts the rover on the network's datum and epoch rather than on whatever the surveyor typed into a local base. Over good networks, NRTK vertical accuracy is about 2–4 cm (1σ), degrading at network edges and during ionospheric activity.

**Post-processed kinematic (PPK)** is RTK after the fact: the rover logs raw observations, the base's observations come from a logged file or a CORS download, and the office processing runs forward and backward, fixing ambiguities with data from both sides of any gap. PPK is the standard for airborne, marine, and drone trajectories (§12.6) because it needs no radio link and is at least as accurate as RTK for the same data.

**Precise point positioning (PPP)** uses a single receiver with precise satellite orbits and clocks (from the IGS or a commercial service), models every other error explicitly (tides, loading, antenna phase centers, phase wind-up, relativity), and estimates position, receiver clock, zenith wet delay, and float ambiguities. Its weakness is convergence: float ambiguities separate from the other parameters only as geometry changes, so float PPP takes 20–60 minutes to reach decimetre accuracy and several hours to reach a few centimetres; 24 h static PPP reaches roughly 1 cm horizontal and 2 cm vertical. **PPP-AR** adds satellite phase-bias products that make the ambiguities integer-resolvable, cutting convergence to 10–30 minutes; **PPP-RTK** adds regional atmospheric corrections so convergence drops to minutes, approaching NRTK without the two-way link. Galileo's High Accuracy Service (initial service 2023) and QZSS CLAS are free broadcast implementations; commercial services (Trimble CenterPoint RTX, Hexagon TerraStar, Fugro, u-blox PointPerfect, and others) deliver corrections by L-band or internet.

**Ambiguity resolution** is the pivot of every precise mode. The double-differenced integer ambiguities are estimated as floats, decorrelated and searched (the LAMBDA method of Teunissen 1995 is standard), and the best integer set is accepted if a validation test passes — classically, if statistically dubiously, a ratio of second-best to best residual norms above 2–3. A **false fix** — accepting the wrong integers — produces a decimetre position error in a solution that reports centimetre precision. False fixes are the most dangerous failure in GNSS surveying because nothing in the solution flags them; only redundancy (a second occupation, a check to a known point, overlap between flight lines) exposes them.

> **Rule of thumb.** Convergence and fix times under open sky, multi-constellation, dual-frequency, mid-latitude, quiet ionosphere: RTK/NRTK fix in 5–60 s; PPK fix in the office essentially always given a few minutes of clean data either side of a gap; PPP-AR to 5 cm in 10–20 minutes, to 2 cm in 1–2 hours; float PPP to 10 cm in 30–60 minutes. Multiply by 2–5 under canopy, in urban canyons, at low latitudes near solar maximum, or with single-frequency hardware. Plan for these, then verify.

## 12.4 Infrastructure and services

**Continuously operating reference stations (CORS)** are permanently installed geodetic receivers whose coordinates and velocities are determined in the reference frame and published; they are the physical realization of the datum for GNSS users ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)). The US NOAA CORS Network comprises roughly 1 800–2 000 partner-operated stations coordinated by NGS, with coordinates in NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00 and IGS14/IGS20; formal uncertainties are a few millimetres, but actual accuracy relative to the frame depends on station history and monument stability. Equivalent networks exist in most countries (CACS in Canada, the EUREF Permanent Network in Europe, GEONET's ~1 300 stations in Japan, Geoscience Australia and state networks).

The **International GNSS Service (IGS)** (Dow, Neilan & Rizos 2009) coordinates about 500 stations and the analysis centers that produce the precise orbits, clocks, Earth-rotation parameters, antenna calibrations (ANTEX), and station coordinates on which PPP, long-baseline processing, and the ITRF itself depend. Products (IGS20 era): **ultra-rapid** (predicted half in real time, ~5 cm orbit, ~3 ns clock), **rapid** (17–41 h, ~2.5 cm, ~75 ps), **final** (12–18 days, ~2.5 cm, ~75 ps), plus the Real-Time Service for real-time PPP. Reprocessing with final products instead of broadcast ephemerides is the cheapest accuracy improvement available for any post-processed workflow.

**Online processing services** give geodetic-quality static solutions to anyone with a RINEX file. NGS **OPUS** (Soler et al. 2006) processes a 2–48 h dual-frequency file against three CORS and returns NAD83(2011) and ITRF coordinates, with the peak-to-peak differences among the three baselines as a quality indicator; OPUS-RS handles files down to 15 min; OPUS Projects adjusts networks of sessions. **CSRS-PPP** (Natural Resources Canada) processes static or kinematic files by PPP (with AR since 2020) in NAD83(CSRS) or ITRF at a chosen epoch, anywhere on Earth. **AUSPOS** (Geoscience Australia) processes static files by network baselines in ITRF and GDA2020. Each service's formal vertical uncertainties are optimistic by 2–5× (see Validation & uncertainty); each returns $h$ and a geoid-derived $H$ whose geoid model must be noted.

**Formats and protocols.** **RINEX** (versions 3.0x and 4.0x, maintained by the IGS RINEX Working Group) is the archival observation and navigation format; its header carries antenna type, antenna height (vertical to ARP — see §12.5), and receiver type, and a wrong header is a wrong result downstream. **RTCM 10403.x** (version 3, with Multiple Signal Messages) is the real-time correction format for RTK and NRTK, and its SSR messages carry PPP corrections; **NTRIP** delivers them over the internet; **BINEX** is a binary archival alternative. **NMEA 0183** sentences (GGA, RMC, GST, …) carry positions and quality flags; the GGA quality indicator (1 = autonomous, 2 = differential, 4 = RTK fixed, 5 = RTK float) is often the only solution-quality record a drone or launch logs, and 4 does not mean the fix was correct ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)).

## 12.5 Field practice for control and checkpoints

Most GNSS for elevation work is done to establish **control** (points of known coordinates that constrain a survey or a base station) and **checkpoints** (independent points used to assess a DEM's accuracy, [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)). The procedures differ in rigor but share the same failure modes.

**Antenna height is the classic blunder.** Published coordinates refer to a monument; the receiver measures to its antenna phase center; the antenna calibration relates the phase center to the **antenna reference point (ARP)**, usually the bottom of the preamplifier housing or the mount thread. You must measure from the mark to the ARP (or to a manufacturer-defined measurement point whose offset is in the ANTEX or spec sheet) and enter it as a *vertical* height, or enter a slant height to the ground-plane edge and let the software reduce it. Every permutation has produced published control wrong by the full antenna height (1.5–2.0 m), the slant–vertical difference (centimetres), the ARP-to-measurement-point offset, or the phase-center offset (5–10 cm, applied twice or not at all). The defense is procedural: measure twice with two tapes or in two units, photograph the setup with the tape in view, record the antenna model and the point measured to, and verify every control point against an independent one.

**Occupation time and redundancy.** NGS guidelines for GNSS-derived ellipsoid heights (Zilkoski, D'Onofrio & Frakes 1997, "NGS-58") specify, for 2 cm (local) and 5 cm (network) standards at 95 %: baselines under 10 km; three 30-minute occupations per point on two days at different times of day; fixed-height poles; a 15° mask; and ties to at least three published CORS or high-accuracy control points. NGS-59 (Zilkoski et al. 2008) extends these to orthometric heights with geoid-model and benchmark-tie requirements. Both are conservative today, but their *structure* — repeated occupations at different times, independent ties, fixed heights — remains the standard of care. The **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, Edition 2, Addendum II** gives best practices for checkpoint surveys: positioning at least three times more accurate than the product tested; static or fast-static GNSS, RTK/NRTK with repeated occupations, or total station from GNSS-controlled points; RTK checkpoints occupied at least twice with re-initialization between and a limit on the difference (typically 2–3 cm vertical); and the checkpoint survey's own accuracy reported.

**Monumentation and siting.** A DEM checkpoint need not be a permanent monument but must be on a surface the DEM represents — flat, open ground for a bare-earth DTM, not a curb, slope break, or tree shadow — and must be recoverable and photographed. Control that will be reused should be a stable, described monument ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)). GNSS siting needs unobstructed sky above about 10–15°, no reflecting surfaces (vehicles, buildings, fences, water) within a few metres, and no nearby transmitters.

**The base station's coordinates are the survey's coordinates.** RTK and PPK positions are *relative to the base*; a 30 cm base error shifts every rover position by 30 cm with no indication in the solution. The base should be on published control with a measured antenna height, or on a new mark fixed by a multi-hour OPUS/CSRS-PPP/AUSPOS solution, and its coordinates, datum, and epoch must be recorded with the project. "Here" positions (the base's autonomous SPP position used as its coordinate) are off by metres and are a documented cause of whole-survey offsets.

> **Worked example.** Budget for an RTK checkpoint height. A checkpoint is observed by NRTK with a survey-grade rover on a 2.000 m fixed-height pole, two 30 s occupations 20 min apart with re-initialization, difference between occupations 1.4 cm. Components (1σ): rover fixed-solution vertical precision, 1.5 cm; residual NRTK distance/atmosphere error, 1.0 cm; network datum realization, 0.5 cm; pole height and plumbing, 0.3 cm; antenna phase-center model, 0.3 cm; site multipath (open field), 0.5 cm. Root-sum-square: $\sqrt{1.5^2+1.0^2+0.5^2+0.3^2+0.3^2+0.5^2} = \sqrt{3.93} \approx 2.0$ cm. Averaging two independent occupations reduces the uncorrelated parts to roughly 1.6 cm; the 95 % vertical uncertainty is about $1.96 \times 1.6 \approx 3.1$ cm in ellipsoidal height. Converting to orthometric height adds the geoid model's uncertainty — about 1–2 cm (1σ) relative in a well-surveyed region ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)) — for roughly 4 cm (95 %) in NAVD88-type height: adequate to test a DEM with a 10 cm RMSE$_z$ requirement (ASPRS asks for checkpoints three times better) but marginal for a 5 cm one.

## 12.6 GNSS on moving platforms

Lidar aircraft, survey launches, mobile mapping vans, and drones carry a GNSS receiver whose output is a **trajectory**, fused with an IMU ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)) into a smoothed best estimate of trajectory (SBET) at 100–200 Hz. The GNSS part is almost always PPK or PPP, occasionally RTK where a radio link is reliable (drones), and never SBAS for survey products.

**Baseline length** is the dominant design variable for PPK. Residual atmospheric and orbit errors grow at about 1 ppm (1 cm per 10 km) and fixing becomes unreliable beyond roughly 30–50 km for single-base processing; the USGS Lidar Base Specification and most airborne specifications limit base-to-aircraft distance to about 30–50 km or require multiple bases, and marine specifications typically require a base within 20–40 km or NRTK/PPP alternatives. Multi-base processing and PPP-AR have loosened this: PPP with IGS final products gives decimetre-to-few-centimetre kinematic trajectories anywhere, competitive with PPK but with weaker vertical performance; many operators run both and compare.

**Base-station ties.** The base must itself be tied to the frame (§12.5); for airborne work its mark should be fixed by static processing against CORS or by OPUS/CSRS-PPP, with antenna height documented. A base set on an airport apron "by eye" in NAD83 epoch 2010.00 while the project is in a state plane realization at another epoch is a classic source of a 2–5 cm whole-survey vertical shift.

**Trajectory quality metrics** every lidar or MBES project should archive: forward–backward separation of the combined solution (under 2–3 cm vertical for a good flight; spikes indicate ambiguity problems); fix status per epoch; PDOP and satellite count; the filter's position standard deviations (optimistic, see Validation & uncertainty); and, for multi-base, the differences between bases. Loss of lock in turns (banking shadows satellites; fly flat turns and discard the start of each line) and under bridges or canopy is the main failure, and it appears in the mapped data as a vertical step between adjacent swaths — which is why overlap analysis of the final data ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.8, [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)) is the ultimate trajectory check.

**Drones** add an inexpensive variant: PPK or RTK with a dual-frequency module (u-blox ZED-F9P class) and a local base or NRTK. Drone PPK trajectories at 2–5 cm (1σ) are routine, but exposures must be time-tagged against GNSS time to sub-millisecond precision (at 15 m/s, 1 ms is 1.5 cm; 10 ms is 15 cm; [Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)) and the antenna-to-sensor lever arm applied ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.5). SfM with PPK camera positions reduces ground control to a few checkpoints, but a systematic vertical offset — "doming" or a constant bias from camera calibration or a timing offset — is common and must be checked ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)).

## 12.7 Heights from GNSS are ellipsoidal

GNSS solves for Cartesian coordinates in the Earth-centred frame of the satellite orbits (ITRF or a national frame aligned to it) and converts them to latitude, longitude, and **ellipsoidal height** $h$ above GRS80 or WGS84 (whose semi-minor axes differ by 0.1 mm — negligible — but whose *frames* differ by up to 1–2 m between NAD83 and ITRF, which is not). The orthometric height users want is $H = h - N$, with $N$ from a geoid model (GEOID18, CGG2013a, OSGM15, AUSGeoid2020, EGM2008, …; [Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)), or a tidal or chart-datum height via a further separation model ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). Two consequences follow:

First, **a GNSS height acquires the geoid model's uncertainty as soon as it becomes orthometric.** Hybrid geoids fitted to GNSS-on-benchmark data have relative accuracies of 1–2 cm over tens of kilometres in well-levelled regions and absolute accuracies of 2–5 cm; gravimetric geoids are worse in absolute terms; and geoid error is spatially correlated, so it does not average out. A checkpoint file that mixes $h$ and $H$ — or uses a different geoid model than the DEM — produces a DEM "error" that is entirely a datum artefact; the *first* diagnostic for a large, smooth vertical bias is to check geoid models and height types.

Second, **GNSS makes ellipsoidal heights a viable working datum** for projects that need an orthometric height only at the final step — airborne lidar, most multibeam, and drone work are processed entirely in $h$ — and keeping data in $h$ preserves the option of applying a better geoid model later. Product metadata should state the ellipsoid, frame, epoch, geoid model and version, and whether heights are $h$ or $H$ ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)); an astonishing fraction of published DEMs state none of these.

## 12.8 Threats and hard environments

**Jamming** — deliberate or accidental interference in the GNSS bands — is cheap (illegal "personal privacy devices" costing tens of dollars disrupt receivers within hundreds of metres) and widespread near conflict zones and some ports. The 2009–2011 Newark Liberty Airport incidents, in which a truck driver's jammer repeatedly disrupted a GBAS test installation, are the canonical case; since 2022, interference across the eastern Mediterranean, Baltic, Black Sea, and Middle East has made aviation and shipping routinely log GNSS loss. For a survey, jamming shows up as loss of lock, degraded C/N₀ on all satellites at once, and trajectory gaps the IMU must bridge ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). **Spoofing** — counterfeit signals that make the receiver compute a false position or time — is more insidious: the 2013 University of Texas yacht experiment (Humphreys and colleagues) and recurring reports of ships' AIS positions jumping to airports show that unprotected receivers accept a plausible false solution without complaint. Defenses include multi-constellation and multi-frequency consistency checks, controlled-reception-pattern antennas, Galileo's Open Service Navigation Message Authentication (OSNMA; public test phase from 2023, service declared 2025), and — for mapping — checking the trajectory against the IMU and against overlap in the mapped data. A spoofed timebase, however, can corrupt lidar and sonar timestamps in ways no spatial check catches until the data meet another source ([Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)).

**Ionospheric storms** near solar maximum (the 2024–2026 peak of cycle 25 is the current example) degrade GNSS in two ways: irregular total electron content inflates single-frequency and SBAS errors to tens of metres, and **scintillation** — rapid amplitude and phase fluctuations, strongest at low magnetic latitudes after sunset and at high latitudes during geomagnetic storms — causes cycle slips and loss of lock even for dual-frequency receivers. RTK fix rates drop and false fixes rise; PPP convergence lengthens. During the May 2024 geomagnetic storm (the strongest since 2003), agricultural RTK users in the central United States reported widespread outages, and CORS data showed TEC gradients that NRTK interpolation could not model. Plan precision work away from storm periods (NOAA SWPC and ESA forecast them), use dual-frequency hardware, and extend occupations.

**Urban canyons** block low satellites, reflect signals from façades (multipath of tens of metres on code, cycle slips on phase), and admit non-line-of-sight signals — reflections received when the direct path is blocked, which bias the pseudorange by the extra path length and look like direct signals to the receiver. Multi-constellation tracking helps because more satellites fall in the sky slot above the street; **shadow matching** (Groves 2011) uses a 3D city model to predict which satellites should be visible from candidate positions, improving cross-street accuracy from tens of metres to a few — the map becomes the positioning system, the theme of [Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md). RTK in a street canyon is untrustworthy without redundancy, and mobile-mapping trajectories in cities depend on the IMU and on control ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.6).

**Under forest canopy**, attenuation (several dB to over 20 dB through wet, dense canopy) and multipath from trunks and branches produce exactly the conditions that break ambiguity resolution: few satellites, low C/N₀, frequent slips. Studies of survey-grade receivers under canopy report horizontal errors of 0.5–5 m for short occupations and vertical errors as large or larger; long static occupations (1–4 h) with multi-constellation receivers can recover decimetre results, but checkpoints under canopy are more reliably established by total station traverse from GNSS points in clearings ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md), §14.5). The honest treatment of an RTK checkpoint observed under canopy is to downweight or exclude it.

## 12.9 Consumer, low-cost, and smartphone receivers

The gap between "survey-grade" and "consumer" receivers narrowed sharply after 2018. Two tiers matter here, plus a test that applies to both.

**Low-cost dual-frequency modules** (u-blox ZED-F9P and successors, Septentrio mosaic, Unicore UM980, and others, roughly US$100–500 for the board) track multiple constellations on two frequencies, run RTK internally, and accept RTCM corrections. The antenna is the limiting component: a US$30 patch has poor multipath rejection and uncalibrated phase-center variations, while a US$100–300 helical or calibrated patch approaches survey performance. Published evaluations show RTK fixed accuracies of 1–3 cm horizontal and 2–5 cm vertical under open sky, with longer fix times and more float fallback than geodetic receivers under obstruction. Without an absolute antenna calibration, base–rover phase-center offsets cancel only if both use the same antenna model in the same orientation, and the absolute height can carry a several-centimetre bias. For DEM checkpoints, low-cost RTK is acceptable when the antenna is calibrated or matches the base, occupations are repeated, and results are verified against a known point.

**Smartphones** with dual-frequency chips (since 2018) expose raw pseudorange, carrier phase, and Doppler through the Android GNSS raw measurements API; research PPK/RTK processing of phone data under open sky reaches decimetre horizontal accuracy with long occupations, and centimetre fixes only with external antennas, which removes the point of using a phone. The integrated antenna has low gain and strong multipath sensitivity, and duty-cycled tracking in many models destroys carrier-phase continuity. Treat phone positions as 2–5 m horizontal and 5–10 m vertical in the open, worse elsewhere, and do not use them as DEM checkpoints — but at population scale phones are an ionospheric and tropospheric sensor network (Smith et al. 2024, ⟨H⟩; [Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md), §11.9).

**How to verify any receiver.** Occupy a published control point (CORS monument or national benchmark with a GNSS-derived ellipsoid height) for several sessions with the field antenna and setup, compute the mean offset and standard deviation in $h$ against the published value, and repeat under the project's sky conditions. This thirty-minute test reveals antenna-height errors, phase-center problems, datum mismatches, and whether the receiver's reported precision has any relationship to its accuracy. Results go in the project report.

## Then & now

The GNSS of 2000 and of the mid-2020s differ in almost every operational parameter, which is why specifications from the earlier era (NGS-58 in 1997, the first USGS LBS in 2010) look conservative today.

- **Constellations.** One usable system (GPS, 24–28 satellites; GLONASS had collapsed to about 8) → four global and two regional systems, more than 120 satellites, 25–40 in view. ⟨H⟩ entries: GPS first launch 1978, GLONASS 1982, BeiDou 2000, Galileo 2011/2016.
- **Frequencies.** Civil L1 only, with L2 by semi-codeless tracking → three civil frequencies per system on modern satellites, with wideband codes (L5/E5) that cut code noise and multipath by an order of magnitude.
- **Standalone accuracy.** 100 m (95 %) under SA → 10 m after SA was switched off on 2 May 2000 ⟨H⟩ → 2–3 m with multi-constellation, SBAS, and better receivers.
- **Precise positioning.** RTK over a radio link to one base; PPP as a research technique (Zumberge et al. 1997) → NRTK as a utility in most developed countries; PPP-AR and PPP-RTK as broadcast (Galileo HAS, QZSS CLAS) and commercial services; convergence from hours to minutes.
- **Infrastructure.** A few hundred CORS and IGS stations, final orbits in two weeks → thousands of CORS, real-time IGS products, and online services (OPUS from 2001, CSRS-PPP from 2003) returning geodetic coordinates in minutes.
- **Reference frames.** NAD83 (HARN) and ITRF97 with static coordinates → ITRF2020, NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00, and the approaching NATRF2022 with time-dependent coordinates ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md), [Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)).
- **Formats.** RINEX 2 ⟨H⟩ (1989), RTCM 2.x, NMEA 0183 ⟨H⟩ (1984) → RINEX 3/4, RTCM 3 MSM and SSR, NTRIP; NMEA 0183 survives unchanged as a weak link.
- **Hardware.** Geodetic receivers at US$20 000–40 000 → survey receivers at a quarter of that, dual-frequency boards at US$200, and dual-frequency phones (2018) feeding ionospheric science (2024 ⟨H⟩).
- **Threats.** Selective Availability → jamming and spoofing, with solar-maximum ionospheric storms as the natural threat; authentication (OSNMA) and resilience planning are now part of practice.

What has not changed: the vertical is still the weak component, the troposphere still limits heights, and antenna heights are still measured wrong.

## Mathematics

**Observation equations.** For receiver $r$ and satellite $s$, the pseudorange $P$ and carrier phase $\Phi$ (in metres) on frequency $j$ are

$$P_{r,j}^{s} = \rho_r^s + c\,(\delta t_r - \delta t^s) + T_r^s + I_{r,j}^s + b_{r,j} - b_j^s + M_{P} + \varepsilon_P,$$

$$\Phi_{r,j}^{s} = \rho_r^s + c\,(\delta t_r - \delta t^s) + T_r^s - I_{r,j}^s + \lambda_j N_{r,j}^s + \beta_{r,j} - \beta_j^s + \lambda_j w + M_{\Phi} + \varepsilon_\Phi,$$

where $\rho_r^s = \|\mathbf{x}^s(t - \tau) - \mathbf{x}_r(t)\|$ is the geometric range from satellite position at transmission to receiver at reception, $c$ the speed of light, $\delta t_r$ and $\delta t^s$ the receiver and satellite clock offsets, $T$ the (non-dispersive) tropospheric delay, $I_j$ the first-order ionospheric delay (positive on code, negative on phase, $I_j \propto f_j^{-2}$), $b$ and $\beta$ the code and phase hardware biases, $\lambda_j$ the wavelength, $N$ the integer ambiguity, $w$ the phase wind-up in cycles, $M$ multipath, and $\varepsilon$ noise. The receiver position enters through $\rho$, linearized about an approximate position: $\rho_r^s \approx \rho_0^s - \mathbf{e}^s \cdot \delta\mathbf{x}$, with $\mathbf{e}^s$ the unit vector from receiver to satellite.

**Ionosphere-free combination.** With $f_1$ and $f_2$, $\Phi_{IF} = \dfrac{f_1^2 \Phi_1 - f_2^2 \Phi_2}{f_1^2 - f_2^2}$ removes the first-order ionospheric term (99.9 % of it) at the cost of amplifying noise by a factor of about 3 for GPS L1/L2 and of destroying the integer nature of the combined ambiguity (hence the wide-lane/narrow-lane strategy in PPP-AR).

**Single and double differences.** Differencing simultaneous observations of satellite $s$ between receivers $r$ (rover) and $b$ (base) removes the satellite clock and, for short baselines, most of the orbit, ionosphere, and troposphere errors:

$$\Delta \Phi_{rb}^{s} = \Phi_r^s - \Phi_b^s = \Delta\rho_{rb}^s + c\,\Delta\delta t_{rb} + \Delta T + (-\Delta I) + \lambda \Delta N_{rb}^s + \ldots$$

Differencing again between satellites $s$ and $k$ removes the receiver clock difference and the receiver hardware biases:

$$\nabla\Delta \Phi_{rb}^{sk} = \nabla\Delta\rho_{rb}^{sk} + \lambda\,\nabla\Delta N_{rb}^{sk} + \nabla\Delta T + \nabla\Delta I + \nabla\Delta M + \nabla\Delta\varepsilon.$$

The double-differenced ambiguity $\nabla\Delta N$ is an integer; this is what RTK and PPK resolve. Multipath and noise do not difference away (they are uncorrelated between sites and amplified by $\sqrt{4} = 2$ in the double difference). For a baseline of length $d$, residual orbit error $\delta\rho_{orb}$ maps into the baseline as roughly $\delta d \approx d\,\delta\rho_{orb}/R$ with $R \approx 20\,200$ km: a 2 cm orbit error on a 50 km baseline gives 0.05 mm — negligible — so residual differential troposphere and ionosphere, not orbits, dominate the ppm term in practice.

**Dilution of precision.** With $n$ satellites and unknowns $(\delta x, \delta y, \delta z, c\,\delta t_r)$ in a local east-north-up frame, the design matrix is

$$G = \begin{bmatrix} -e_{E}^{1} & -e_{N}^{1} & -e_{U}^{1} & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ -e_{E}^{n} & -e_{N}^{n} & -e_{U}^{n} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q = (G^\mathsf{T} G)^{-1},$$

and with equal, independent range errors of standard deviation $\sigma_{UERE}$ the position covariance is $\sigma_{UERE}^2 Q$. Then $\mathrm{HDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{11}+Q_{22}}$, $\mathrm{VDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{33}}$, $\mathrm{PDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{11}+Q_{22}+Q_{33}}$, $\mathrm{TDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{44}}$, and $\sigma_U = \mathrm{VDOP}\cdot\sigma_{UERE}$. Because all $e_U^s \ge \sin(\text{mask})$ are positive, the third column of $G$ is nearly collinear with the fourth (all ones) — the formal statement that height and clock are confounded — and $Q_{33}$ is inflated. Typical open-sky multi-GNSS values are HDOP 0.6–0.9, VDOP 1.0–1.6. Weighted versions use elevation-dependent weights: $Q = (G^\mathsf{T} W G)^{-1}$.

**Tropospheric mapping functions (sketch).** The slant delay is modelled as $T = m_h(\epsilon)\,\mathrm{ZHD} + m_w(\epsilon)\,\mathrm{ZWD} + m_g(\epsilon)\,[G_N \cos\alpha + G_E \sin\alpha]$, with $\epsilon$ the elevation angle and $\alpha$ the azimuth. The Saastamoinen hydrostatic zenith delay is $\mathrm{ZHD} \approx 0.0022768\,p\,/\,(1 - 0.00266\cos 2\varphi - 0.00028\,h_{km})$ metres with $p$ in hPa, about 2.3 m at sea level; the wet zenith delay (0–0.4 m) is estimated. Mapping functions have the continued-fraction form $m(\epsilon) = \dfrac{1 + \frac{a}{1 + \frac{b}{1+c}}}{\sin\epsilon + \frac{a}{\sin\epsilon + \frac{b}{\sin\epsilon + c}}}$ (Niell 1996; VMF1/VMF3 take $a, b, c$ from numerical weather models); $m \approx 5.6$ at 10° and $\approx 10$ at 5°. The height–ZWD correlation arises because the phase partials with respect to height ($-\sin\epsilon$) and ZWD ($m_w \approx 1/\sin\epsilon$) are both monotone in elevation, distinguishable only by their different curvature at low elevation.

**INS-free kinematic position noise.** In a PPK trajectory, the per-epoch position standard deviation from the filter is $\sigma_{pos} \approx \mathrm{DOP}\cdot\sigma_{\Phi,DD}$ once fixed, with $\sigma_{\Phi,DD} \approx 4$–$6$ mm; the ppm term is added in quadrature: $\sigma_U \approx \sqrt{(\mathrm{VDOP}\cdot 5\,\text{mm})^2 + (1\,\text{ppm}\cdot d)^2}$. For VDOP 1.5 and $d = 30$ km, $\sigma_U \approx \sqrt{7.5^2 + 30^2}$ mm $\approx 31$ mm — the baseline term dominates, which is why short baselines matter more than receiver quality for airborne heights.

## Validation & uncertainty

GNSS errors in elevation products appear as three signatures: a **constant vertical bias** across the project (wrong base coordinates, antenna height, datum or geoid mismatch, frame epoch), a **slowly varying bias** along the trajectory or between sessions (troposphere, orbit, float/fix transitions), and **step discontinuities** between lines or sessions (false fixes, re-initializations). Testing targets each.

**Test the control before the survey.** Occupy a published point with the project setup; the mean difference in $h$ should be within a few centimetres and the session-to-session spread should match the receiver's expected repeatability. If not, the problem is in the antenna height, antenna model, datum, or published coordinate — all cheaper to find now than after the flight.

**Build redundancy into every control and checkpoint survey.** Independent re-occupations (different time of day, re-initialized ambiguities) are the only defense against false fixes and multipath. For RTK checkpoints, a vertical difference over about 3 cm between two fixed occupations indicates a problem with at least one; for static sessions, OPUS peak-to-peak vertical over 4–5 cm or CSRS-PPP sigma over 2–3 cm after several hours suggests multipath, a short session, or an obstructed site. Report the repeatability statistics as the checkpoint survey's own accuracy; ASPRS Ed. 2 requires it to be at least three times better than the product's tested accuracy.

**Know that formal uncertainties are optimistic.** Standard deviations reported by RTK receivers, PPK software, and PPP services assume white noise, a correct stochastic model, and correct ambiguities; empirical studies routinely find true errors 2–5 times the formal values, more for the vertical and under obstruction. Treat a reported σ$_U$ of 1 cm as "probably 2–4 cm" until the redundancy says otherwise.

**Validate trajectories through the mapped data.** For airborne and marine platforms the ultimate GNSS test is geometric: compare overlapping swaths or crossing lines (relative accuracy) and compare the mapped surface to independent checkpoints and control surfaces (absolute accuracy). A whole-project vertical offset constant across all lines is a base or datum problem; one that differs between flights or days is a per-session base or troposphere problem; one that grows along a line is float-ambiguity drift. [Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.8 and [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) give the statistics.

**Check datum, epoch, and geoid by inspection.** A smooth, large (decimetre to metre) vertical bias varying slowly across a project is the signature of a geoid or datum mismatch, not of GNSS measurement error. Before touching the processing, confirm that control, checkpoints, and product share the same frame, epoch, height type, and geoid model, and that the CORS coordinates used for the base were at the epoch the software assumed ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md), [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)).

**What to report** for any GNSS-controlled elevation product: receiver and antenna models (ANTEX names) at base and rover; base coordinates with source, frame, and epoch; processing mode and software version; precise products used (broadcast / rapid / final; analysis center); baseline lengths; occupation times and independent occupations per point; fix-status and forward–backward separation statistics for trajectories; the control-point verification result; and the geoid model used for $H$. [Appendix G](../appendices/appendix-g-checklists.md) gives this as a checklist.

> **Try it.** Compute DOP and the expected vertical/horizontal ratio from a real sky, and see how a 30° mask damages the vertical.
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np
>
> def dops(az_deg, el_deg):
>     az, el = np.radians(az_deg), np.radians(el_deg)
>     e = np.c_[np.cos(el)*np.sin(az), np.cos(el)*np.cos(az), np.sin(el)]  # E, N, U unit vectors
>     G = np.c_[-e, np.ones(len(el))]
>     Q = np.linalg.inv(G.T @ G)
>     return dict(HDOP=np.sqrt(Q[0,0]+Q[1,1]), VDOP=np.sqrt(Q[2,2]),
>                 PDOP=np.sqrt(np.trace(Q[:3,:3])), TDOP=np.sqrt(Q[3,3]))
>
> rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
> # 30 satellites scattered over the sky, elevations biased toward the horizon as in reality
> az = rng.uniform(0, 360, 30)
> el = np.degrees(np.arcsin(rng.uniform(np.sin(np.radians(5)), 1, 30)))
> for mask in (5, 10, 15, 30):
>     keep = el >= mask
>     d = dops(az[keep], el[keep])
>     print(f"mask {mask:2d}°  n={keep.sum():2d}  HDOP={d['HDOP']:.2f}  VDOP={d['VDOP']:.2f}  ratio={d['VDOP']/d['HDOP']:.2f}")
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: with all satellites, VDOP/HDOP is about 1.5–2; raising the mask to 30° (a street canyon or tree line) removes the low satellites that discriminate height from clock, and VDOP grows faster than HDOP, often past 3 — multiply by a 5 mm fixed-phase UERE for RTK or 1 m for code to see the position effect.

## Software

**Open source:** **RTKLIB** (Takasu & Yasuda 2009; and the maintained rtklibexplorer/demo5 fork) — SPP, DGNSS, RTK/PPK, PPP, multi-constellation; the reference tool for learning and for low-cost receivers, though configuration is intricate and defaults are not survey-grade. **PRIDE PPP-AR** (Geng et al. 2019) — PPP with ambiguity resolution using open phase-bias products. **gLAB** (gAGE/UPC) — educational processing with transparent modelling. **GNSS-SDR** — software-defined receiver for interference research. **GAMIT/GLOBK** (MIT; free to academic users under license), **Bernese** (AIUB), and **GipsyX** (JPL) — licensed geodetic network and PPP processing for reference-frame work. **PROJ/pyproj** — frame, epoch, and geoid transformations of GNSS results. **Services:** NGS **OPUS**, NRCan **CSRS-PPP**, Geoscience Australia **AUSPOS**, and national equivalents; archive each report with the project. **Commercial:** **Trimble Business Center**, **Leica Infinity**, **Topcon Magnet** — static and kinematic processing, network adjustment, and site calibration (a frequent source of hidden datum distortion); **NovAtel Inertial Explorer** and **Applanix POSPac** — GNSS/INS trajectory processing, the de facto standard in lidar and MBES production; **NovAtel GrafNav** — kinematic GNSS only. Across all trajectory software, the forward–backward separation plot is a necessary, not sufficient, quality indicator — a solution can be smoothly wrong.

## Pitfalls

- **Antenna height to the wrong reference** → mark-to-ARP, mark-to-measurement-point, slant versus vertical, and phase-center offsets are different quantities, and software asks for one of them → measure twice, photograph, record the antenna model and point measured to, and verify every control point independently; a whole-height error (1.5–2 m) is obvious, a slant–vertical error (2–5 cm) is not.
- **Ellipsoidal and orthometric heights mixed in one checkpoint file** → field software exports whichever the operator configured, and geoid names look alike (GEOID12B vs GEOID18, EGM96 vs EGM2008) → store $h$, $N$, geoid model name, and $H$ as separate columns; a smooth 20–60 cm bias in a DEM check is a geoid or height-type mismatch until proven otherwise.
- **Short occupations under canopy or near buildings** → low C/N₀ and multipath yield float solutions or false fixes reported with centimetre precision → lengthen occupations, repeat with re-initialization, compare, and move the checkpoint to open ground or carry it in by total station.
- **RTK fixed to a base with wrong coordinates** → "here" positions or mistyped coordinates shift the entire survey by decimetres or metres without any indication → fix base coordinates by static processing against CORS, document them, and check one rover position against published control before starting.
- **NRTK datum differing from the project datum** → the network's realization (e.g., NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00 or ETRF2000) may not match the project's realization or epoch; differences of 1–5 cm vertical and more horizontally → read the network's datum statement; transform explicitly; never "calibrate" the discrepancy away without understanding it.
- **Ignoring the CORS coordinate epoch** → CORS coordinates are published at a reference epoch and move with the plate (and local subsidence or uplift); processing in ITRF at the observation epoch and reporting in NAD83 at 2010.00 without HTDP introduces centimetres per year of drift in deforming regions → use the correct frame/epoch pipeline and record it ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)).
- **Trusting the receiver's reported σ** → formal uncertainties assume correct ambiguities and white noise; real errors are 2–5× larger → use repeat occupations and control checks for the uncertainty you report.
- **PPK with a base too far away** → 1 ppm plus unreliable fixing beyond 30–50 km produces decimetre vertical errors that look like a tilt → limit baselines per the specification, use multiple bases, or add PPP-AR as a check.
- **Lever arm or time offset between antenna and sensor ignored** → on moving platforms, a 10 ms timing error at 60 m/s is 60 cm along track; an unmeasured antenna-to-IMU lever arm maps attitude into position → measure and apply offsets ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)), and check with overlapping lines.
- **Processing a solar-maximum survey as if the ionosphere were quiet** → scintillation and TEC gradients break fixing and NRTK interpolation → check space-weather indices for the survey dates when diagnosing unexpected trajectory noise; reprocess with final products and tighter fix validation.

## Key takeaways

- GNSS vertical accuracy is bounded by geometry (no satellites below you), the wet troposphere (nearly indistinguishable from height), multipath, and antenna modelling — not by receiver price; expect the vertical to be 2–3 times worse than the horizontal in every mode.
- Carrier phase with resolved integer ambiguities is what makes centimetre positioning possible; a false fix is a decimetre error with a centimetre label, and only redundancy exposes it.
- Choose the mode by the need: SBAS for navigation, RTK/NRTK with repeated occupations for checkpoints in open terrain, PPK with short baselines for trajectories, static with online services for control, PPP-AR where no base is practical.
- Every GNSS survey needs documented base coordinates with their frame and epoch, the antenna models and heights at base and rover, the processing mode and precise products used, and repeat-occupation statistics.
- GNSS heights are ellipsoidal; the geoid model's error is added the moment you convert, and a smooth large bias in a DEM check is almost always a datum, epoch, or geoid mismatch rather than a GNSS error.
- Test the setup on published control before the survey and validate trajectories through the mapped data afterward; formal uncertainties are optimistic by a factor of 2–5.
- Modern multi-constellation, multi-frequency receivers — including low-cost boards with a good antenna — fix faster and survive more obstruction than GPS-only equipment, but canopy, canyons, and ionospheric storms still break them.
- Plan for jamming, spoofing, and solar-maximum storms as operational realities: log raw data, carry an IMU, keep a check on known control, and know what the data look like when GNSS is lying.
