# Chapter 39 — Earthquakes, volcanoes, landslides: sudden deformation and its datum consequences

> **Part VIII — The dynamic Earth.** Where the previous chapter dealt with motion that is slow enough to model as a velocity, this one deals with motion that happens in seconds to weeks — and that invalidates control networks, DEMs, and charts faster than any agency can republish them.

**In this chapter.** You will learn how large earthquakes move the ground — metres of coseismic offset near the rupture, centimetres thousands of kilometres away — and how the motion continues for years afterward as post-seismic relaxation, so that a deformation model needs both a step and a decaying tail. You will see what national agencies actually do after a major event (Japan after Tōhoku, New Zealand after Kaikōura), how coseismic displacement is measured with GNSS, InSAR, optical image correlation, lidar differencing, and repeat multibeam, and how to compute the expected displacement field from a fault model (Okada 1985). You will study volcanic deformation and the terrain that eruptions create (Kīlauea 2018, La Palma 2021), landslide volumes and their uncertainty (Oso 2014), submarine mass movements (Grand Banks 1929), and tsunami erosion and deposition. Finally you will learn when and how to withdraw a DEM or chart, label rapid-response products as provisional, and treat pre-event control as a hypothesis rather than a fact.

## 39.1 Coseismic displacement

An earthquake is slip on a fault, and slip displaces the surrounding crust by an amount that decays with distance from the rupture. For the largest events the numbers are not subtle. In the **2011 Tōhoku** earthquake (M9.0, 11 March), the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (GSI) measured a horizontal displacement of about 5.3 m toward the east-southeast and 1.2 m of subsidence at the Oshika Peninsula GEONET station, and offsets exceeding 1 m across most of northeastern Honshu; the seafloor above the rupture moved tens of metres (§39.4). In the **2016 Kaikōura** earthquake in New Zealand (M7.8, 14 November), a cascading rupture of more than a dozen faults produced coastal uplift of up to ~6 m, surface offsets of up to ~10 m on the Kekerengu fault, and displacements of centimetres across the whole of the South Island and lower North Island (Hamling et al. 2017; Clark et al. 2017). The **2010 Maule** earthquake in Chile (M8.8) uplifted the coast by up to ~2.5 m and moved Concepción ~3 m westward; the **2004 Sumatra–Andaman** earthquake (M9.1–9.3) displaced the Andaman Islands by metres, submerging some coastal areas by 1–2 m and uplifting others, and was detectable as a few millimetres of displacement at GNSS stations several thousand kilometres away (Banerjee et al. 2005).

Two properties of the coseismic field matter for elevation work. The first is **reach**: displacement falls off roughly as $1/r^2$ in the far field of a finite dislocation (for a point source; more slowly near a long rupture), so a M9 event with ~30 m of slip produces centimetre-level displacements out to 1000–2000 km — Tōhoku moved stations in Korea and eastern China by 2–5 cm, and Sumatra moved Phuket by ~27 cm. A national datum's "unaffected" area is therefore smaller than intuition suggests: a deformation model must account for far-field displacement wherever it exceeds the accuracy of the control, which for geodetic-grade networks is about a centimetre. The second is the **vertical**: for subduction earthquakes the coast above the locked zone typically subsides (Tōhoku, 1960 Chile, parts of Sumatra) while the outer forearc and seafloor uplift; for thrust ruptures that reach the surface (Kaikōura, 1999 Chi-Chi, 2008 Wenchuan) the hanging wall rises by metres. These vertical steps are permanent changes in the relationship between land and sea: after Tōhoku, towns along the Sanriku coast flooded at high tide for years until seawalls and ground were raised; after Kaikōura, the harbour uplifted by about a metre and lost its usable depth overnight.

The empirical relationship between magnitude and displacement is the scaling law: seismic moment $M_0 = \mu A \bar{s}$ (rigidity × rupture area × mean slip), with $M_w = \tfrac{2}{3}\log_{10} M_0 - 6.07$ (SI). Typical mean slips are ~1 m for M7, ~5 m for M8, and 10–30 m for M9 (Wells & Coppersmith 1994 give regressions for crustal earthquakes; subduction events depart from them). Surface displacement near the rupture is of the order of the slip; a M6.5 crustal event produces decimetres of surface offset, a M7.5 produces metres. The practical threshold at which a survey organization must assume its control has moved by more than a centimetre is roughly M6 within 50 km and M7+ within several hundred kilometres.

## 39.2 Post-seismic relaxation and afterslip

The ground does not stop moving when the shaking stops. **Afterslip** — continued aseismic slip on the fault and its extensions — and **viscoelastic relaxation** of the lower crust and upper mantle produce displacements that begin at rates of centimetres per day and decay over months to decades, following roughly logarithmic (afterslip) or exponential (viscoelastic) time functions. After Tōhoku, post-seismic motion in northeastern Japan exceeded a metre within the first few years at some stations, with the direction and vertical sense differing from the coseismic step (parts of the coast that subsided coseismically began to rise). After the 2004 Sumatra event, post-seismic displacement at Andaman Islands sites exceeded the coseismic in some directions over the following decade. Even for a M7 crustal event, afterslip of 10–30 % of the coseismic slip over the first year is common.

For a deformation model this is the "patch" problem: a coseismic displacement grid is a step that can be applied cleanly at a known date, but post-seismic motion is a time function that must be represented either as a series of dated patches (LINZ's approach after Canterbury and Kaikōura, with a "reverse patch" convention in which post-event coordinates are the reference and pre-event observations are corrected), as parameterized decay functions attached to each grid node (ITRF2014/2020 post-seismic models for GNSS stations), or as a revised secular velocity field adopted some years later once the rate has decayed to near-linearity. Which is chosen affects every user: a surveyor comparing a 2017 and a 2022 GNSS observation in Kaikōura must apply not only the 2016 step but the intervening post-seismic displacement (decimetres), and a lidar change study spanning the same years must either do the same or report the post-seismic motion as apparent terrain change. The honest approach for DEM work is to state which model version was applied and to retain the capability to re-apply a later one.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Coseismic" means during the rupture (seconds to minutes); "post-seismic" means the decaying motion afterward (months to decades); "interseismic" means the steady strain accumulation between events, which is what the secular velocity field in a deformation model represents. A "static" displacement field in seismology means the permanent coseismic offset (as opposed to the transient shaking), not a field that does not change — and GNSS "static" surveys have nothing to do with either. A deformation model "patch" is a dated displacement grid, not a software fix.

## 39.3 What a national agency does after an earthquake

Within hours of a large event, the agency's CORS network shows the step. The sequence that follows, as practised by GSI Japan after Tōhoku and by LINZ after Canterbury and Kaikōura, has become a template.

**Japan, 2011.** GEONET (~1 300 stations) recorded the coseismic field immediately; GSI published preliminary displacement vectors within a day and a slip model within days. The survey results (coordinates) of control points across eastern Japan were suspended for the affected prefectures, because publishing coordinates known to be wrong by metres is worse than publishing nothing. Over the following months GSI re-surveyed the primary control, derived a correction model combining GEONET displacements with the Okada-based slip model and InSAR, and republished revised coordinates for the affected control points — the realization known as **JGD2011**, formally the same datum with new coordinates for the re-surveyed region — with a published correction tool (**PatchJGD**) so that users could update existing survey data. Benchmark heights, which had changed by up to 1.2 m, were revised by combining GNSS/levelling with the vertical displacement model; the semi-dynamic correction parameters for the region were reset so that post-seismic motion from the new epoch could be handled going forward. The whole process took approximately eight months for the main revision, with further updates as post-seismic motion accumulated.

**New Zealand, 2010–2016.** LINZ's NZGD2000 deformation model already existed when the Darfield (2010) and Christchurch (2011) earthquakes struck; the response was to add dated patches derived from GNSS and InSAR, and to publish a new model version. After Kaikōura, LINZ followed the same pattern on a larger scale — the patch covers most of the northeastern South Island and the Wellington region — and additionally the hydrographic office (LINZ is both) issued notices to mariners for Kaikōura harbour and the adjacent coast, where uplift of ~1 m had made charted depths dangerously wrong, and commissioned re-surveys ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)). Because NZGD2000 coordinates are defined at epoch 2000.0 via the model, the *published* coordinates of unaffected marks did not change; only the model did. For marks within the deforming zone, LINZ re-observed and republished.

**Elsewhere.** NGS maintains earthquake models within HTDP for U.S. events back to 1934 and re-surveys CORS and benchmarks after major West Coast and Alaska events; after the 2010 El Mayor–Cucapah earthquake (M7.2, Baja California), coordinates across the Imperial Valley shifted by decimetres and NAD83(2011) incorporated the displacements. Hydrographic offices respond with notices to mariners, preliminary chart corrections, and Navigation Response Team surveys of ports (NOAA's NRTs after Hurricane Katrina and the 2011 Japan tsunami debris are the model); IHO S-44 requires that surveys be repeated after events known to have changed the seafloor. The common principles: suspend what is known to be wrong, measure before modelling, publish the model with a version, and give users a tool to update their own data.

## 39.4 Measuring coseismic deformation

**GNSS** gives the most accurate point displacements (millimetres for continuous stations, centimetres for campaign marks), in three dimensions and absolute in the ITRF, but only where stations exist. Dense networks (GEONET, PBO/Network of the Americas, GeoNet NZ) resolve the field at 20–30 km spacing; elsewhere the field between stations is interpolated or modelled.

**InSAR** maps the line-of-sight component of displacement over the whole rupture area at tens-of-metres resolution, within days of the post-event acquisition — Sentinel-1's systematic 6–12 day repeat means a coseismic interferogram is almost always available, and it has become the primary input to slip models worldwide ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)). Its limits near the rupture are intrinsic: where the displacement gradient exceeds one fringe (half a wavelength, 2.8 cm for C-band) per pixel, the interferogram decorrelates, so the metres-of-offset zone within a kilometre or so of a surface rupture is a hole, and the phase must be unwrapped across it from the far field. Pixel-offset tracking on the SAR amplitude fills the hole with decimetre precision.

**Optical image correlation** (COSI-Corr, Leprince et al. 2007; MicMac; autoRIFT) measures horizontal displacement by sub-pixel cross-correlation of pre- and post-event orthoimages — aerial, SPOT, Pléiades, Sentinel-2, Landsat — with precision of 1/10 to 1/20 of a pixel, i.e. decimetres for metre-class imagery. It works exactly where InSAR fails, in the near field, and resolves the distributed deformation on either side of a fault trace that field mapping of the discrete offset misses; its weaknesses are sensitivity to orthorectification errors (which require a good DEM and accurate orientation; [Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)), and the absence of vertical information from a single image pair.

**Lidar differencing** provides the full 3D near-field displacement when pre-event lidar exists. After El Mayor–Cucapah, Oskin et al. (2012) differenced 2006 INEGI 5 m lidar against post-event NCALM lidar to map the rupture's vertical deformation over 300 km², resolving a complex distribution of slip that surface mapping had not; Nissen et al. (2012) showed that applying the **iterative closest point (ICP)** algorithm to windowed point-cloud pairs recovers the 3D displacement vector *and* rotation in each window — the key advance over vertical DoD, which cannot separate horizontal motion on a slope from vertical change. The method requires that the windows be large enough to contain stable topographic texture (tens of metres) and small enough that displacement is uniform within them, and its precision is set by point density and surface roughness: decimetres for the El Mayor case with 0.1–0.5 pt/m² pre-event data, centimetres with modern 10+ pt/m² data (Scott et al. 2018 on the 2016 Kumamoto earthquake).

**Repeat multibeam bathymetry** is the only way to measure seafloor displacement over wide areas. Fujiwara et al. (2011) compared a 1999 multibeam survey of the Japan Trench with a post-Tōhoku survey and found that the seafloor near the trench axis had moved approximately 50 m horizontally toward the trench and risen by 7–10 m — the largest coseismic displacement ever measured, and evidence that slip reached the trench and drove the tsunami. The comparison was feasible only because the features (the trench slope, a prominent ridge) were large relative to the horizontal uncertainty of the older survey, which with 1999 navigation was tens of metres; the result was obtained by matching bathymetric profiles rather than differencing grids. GNSS-Acoustic seafloor geodesy (Sato et al. 2011) measured 24 m of horizontal and 3 m of vertical motion at discrete seafloor stations ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)). The datum consequence is one that charting agencies had rarely considered: the seabed moved, so pre-event bathymetry across the entire rupture zone is horizontally displaced by metres to tens of metres and vertically by metres, and depths near the coast changed by the coastal subsidence as well.

## 39.5 Volcanic deformation and new terrain

Volcanoes deform the surface in two ways: elastically, as magma pressurizes or drains a reservoir (inflation and deflation, reversible, centimetres to a few metres over the edifice, measured by GNSS, tiltmeters, and InSAR and modelled with Mogi point-source or Okada sill/dyke sources); and constructively or destructively, as eruptions deposit lava, tephra, and domes or remove the summit in collapses. The second kind makes DEMs obsolete in days.

The **2018 Kīlauea** eruption (May–August) is the best-documented example (Neal et al. 2019). On the lower East Rift Zone, fissure 8 produced lava flows that covered about 35 km² — burying 700 homes — and added approximately 3.5 km² of new land to the island, while the summit caldera collapsed incrementally in 62 discrete events, the floor dropping more than 500 m and the caldera volume increasing by ~0.8 km³. Pre-eruption DEMs of the lower Puna district became wrong by tens of metres over 35 km²; the summit DEM became wrong by hundreds of metres. USGS/HVO mapped the flows with daily UAS and helicopter photogrammetry and produced interim DSMs within days; the final post-eruption lidar came in 2019. The **2021 La Palma** eruption (Cumbre Vieja/Tajogaite, 19 September–13 December) covered about 12 km² with lava averaging tens of metres thick, destroyed ~1 600 buildings, and built two lava deltas totalling roughly 0.5 km² of new coastline; Copernicus Emergency Management Service and Spanish agencies issued updated DSMs from Pléiades and drone surveys throughout, each labelled with the acquisition date because the surface changed between flights.

Other volcanic terrain changes are equally decisive: lava-dome growth and collapse (Soufrière Hills, Montserrat, 1995 onward; Mount St. Helens 1980–86 and 2004–08) changes summit heights by hundreds of metres; tephra fall buries terrain under decimetres to metres (Pinatubo 1991 added up to several metres of ash near the vent and lahars continued to rework it for years); caldera-forming collapses (Pinatubo, Bárðarbunga 2014–15, Kīlauea 2018) remove or lower the summit; and new islands appear and vanish (Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, built in 2015, largely destroyed in the January 2022 eruption, whose seafloor crater and surrounding bathymetry changed by hundreds of metres over tens of square kilometres). In each case the pre-event DEM is not degraded but *replaced*, and the correct metadata action is withdrawal of the old product for the affected extent, not a revised accuracy statement.

> **Case file.** *Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, January 2022.* Pre-eruption bathymetry from 2015–2016 showed the volcano's summit near sea level with a 2015 island; post-eruption surveys in 2022 (NIWA and others) found a caldera about 850 m deep where the island had been and at least 6–7 km³ of material removed. Charts for the area were invalid, submarine cables were cut, and the tsunami modelling that depended on the old bathymetry had to be redone with the new. For the bathymetric DEM practitioner the lesson is that global compilations (GEBCO) silently retain the pre-event surface until a contributor supplies new data and the compilation is rebuilt — potentially years later ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).

## 39.6 Landslides and mass movements

A landslide moves a volume of material from a source zone to a deposit zone; the DEM of difference between pre- and post-event surfaces shows depletion (negative) in the source and accumulation (positive) in the deposit, and the two volumes — corrected for bulking of fragmented rock (typically 15–30 % volume increase) — should approximately balance for a confined slide. In practice the estimate is dominated by the uncertainty of the pre-event DEM and by co-registration ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md), [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)): a 10 cm vertical uncertainty over a 1 km² slide is 10⁵ m³ of volume uncertainty before correlation is considered, and if the errors are spatially correlated over 100 m (as DEM errors usually are) the effective number of independent cells is a few hundred rather than a million and the volume uncertainty is several times larger than the naive $\sigma_{\Delta z}\,A/\sqrt{n}$ (Mathematics).

The **Oso landslide** of 22 March 2014 (Washington State) killed 43 people when approximately 8 × 10⁶ m³ of glacial sediment failed from a 180 m-high terrace scarp above the North Fork Stillaguamish River, liquefied, and ran out more than a kilometre across the valley floor (Iverson et al. 2015). Two lessons concern elevation data. First, pre-event lidar (2003, 2013) and post-event lidar (2014) allowed the volume, the runout, and the mobility to be quantified with uncertainties stated; the 2013 pre-event survey, flown months before the event, was a fortunate baseline. Second — and this is the lesson most often drawn — the pre-event lidar bare-earth DTM showed, unmistakably, the hummocky deposits of multiple prehistoric and historic slides from the same scarp across the valley floor, including the 2006 event; the landslide hazard was legible in the DTM in a way it was not in aerial photographs or on the ground under forest. Lidar-based landslide inventories, now standard in Washington, Oregon, and elsewhere, follow directly.

**Rock avalanches** and large rockslides (Val Pola 1987, Mount Cook 1991, Bingham Canyon 2013 — the latter ~65 × 10⁶ m³ into an open-pit mine, mapped with pre- and post-event photogrammetry) move tens of millions of cubic metres in minutes and are among the few episodic processes in which both volume and velocity can be reconstructed from DEMs and seismic records together. **Slow-moving landslides** (earthflows, deep-seated gravitational slope deformations) creep at centimetres to metres per year; they are measured by the same tools as coseismic deformation — InSAR time series, optical correlation of repeat orthoimages, repeat lidar with ICP — and are among the most common sources of apparent "erosion" in DoD maps that are actually horizontal translation on a slope ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)).

**Submarine landslides** are the largest mass movements on Earth (Storegga, ~8 150 years ago, moved ~3 000 km³) and the least observed. The **1929 Grand Banks** event ⟨H⟩ (18 November; a M7.2 earthquake on the continental slope south of Newfoundland) triggered a slope failure and turbidity current that broke twelve transatlantic telegraph cables in sequence over the following thirteen hours; Heezen and Ewing (1952) used the timed breaks to infer a current that travelled more than 600 km at speeds they estimated near 100 km/h on the slope, slowing to ~20 km/h on the abyssal plain, which established turbidity currents as the agent of submarine canyon erosion and deep-sea sand deposition ([Chapter 72](ch72-history.md)). Modern repeat multibeam surveys of canyons (Monterey Canyon's coordinated sensor experiment, 2015–17) show bed changes of metres per event; the cable-break record remains the primary archive of submarine landslide timing, and cable route surveys are the main economic driver for repeat bathymetry on continental slopes ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md), [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

> **Worked example.** *Landslide volume with correlated error.* Post-minus-pre DoD over a 0.6 km² slide, 1 m cells ($n$ = 600 000), each epoch σ = 0.15 m after co-registration, so $\sigma_{\Delta z}$ = 0.21 m. Measured net volume: −1.9 × 10⁶ m³ (source) and +1.6 × 10⁶ m³ (deposit). *Naive* volume uncertainty treating cells as independent: $\sigma_V = \sigma_{\Delta z}\,a\,\sqrt{n}$ = 0.21 × 1 × 775 ≈ 160 m³ — absurdly small. With a variogram showing correlation range $L$ ≈ 120 m, the effective number of independent cells is roughly $n_{\text{eff}} \approx A / (\pi L^2)$ = 600 000 / 45 000 ≈ 13, and the uncertainty of the *mean* change is $\sigma_{\Delta z}/\sqrt{13}$ ≈ 0.058 m, giving $\sigma_V$ ≈ 0.058 × 600 000 ≈ 35 000 m³ — about 2 % of the volume. Adding a possible residual co-registration bias of 0.03 m across the whole area contributes a further 18 000 m³ systematically. Report: source volume 1.9 × 10⁶ ± 0.04 × 10⁶ m³ (1σ, including correlation and registration), and note that the apparent 0.3 × 10⁶ m³ source–deposit imbalance is consistent with 15–20 % bulking plus material lost to the river.

## 39.7 Tsunami and surge as terrain change

Tsunamis and storm surges are hydrodynamic events, but they leave geomorphic change behind: scour around structures, breaching of dunes and barrier islands, deposition of sand sheets inland (the sedimentary record by which paleotsunamis are dated), and removal of entire built landscapes. The 2011 Tōhoku tsunami stripped soil and structures from coastal plains, deposited sand sheets up to tens of centimetres thick kilometres inland, and — combined with coseismic subsidence of up to 1.2 m — permanently changed the land–sea boundary; the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami breached and reshaped coastlines in Aceh by hundreds of metres. Hurricane storm surges do the same on barrier islands: the USGS pre- and post-storm lidar program (since Hurricane Bonnie, 1998) has documented dune lowering of several metres, overwash deposits, and island breaches after nearly every major Atlantic and Gulf landfall, with the 2008 Ike breaches on the Bolivar Peninsula and the 2012 Sandy changes at Fire Island as well-studied cases.

Post-event survey priorities follow from the perishability of the evidence. Water marks, wrack lines, and debris fields that record inundation extent and run-up height are destroyed by cleanup within days; sand sheets are reworked within weeks; scour holes fill. The International Tsunami Survey Team protocol and the USGS post-storm protocol therefore prioritize rapid field measurement of high-water marks and run-up with GNSS, aerial imagery and lidar within days, and bathymetric survey of ports and channels for navigation safety before scientific objectives — all of which produce DEM-relevant data that must carry the acquisition date and the vertical datum *as realized after the event* (coseismic subsidence shifts the tidal datums too; §39.1).

## 39.8 Chart and DEM invalidation policy

A product is invalidated when the surface it describes no longer exists to within its stated accuracy over a non-trivial area. The policy question is operational: who decides, how quickly, and what replaces it.

For nautical charts the machinery exists. Hydrographic offices issue **Notices to Mariners** (temporary, preliminary, or permanent corrections) within days of an event known to have changed depths; S-44 (ed. 6.1.0, 2022) and national guidance require re-survey of affected areas, prioritized by traffic and hazard; and ports commission their own surveys because liability and insurance require it. After Kaikōura, LINZ issued notices for the uplifted coast within days and new survey data within months; after Tōhoku, the Japan Coast Guard re-surveyed major ports within weeks because debris and subsidence had changed controlling depths. The principle is that a chart with a known, unquantified error is withdrawn or caveated rather than left in service ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)).

For topographic DEMs the machinery mostly does not exist. National elevation programs (3DEP, the UK Environment Agency, national lidar programs in Europe) update on acquisition schedules, not event schedules, and few publish an "invalidated extent" layer when an earthquake, eruption, or landslide changes the surface. The responsibility devolves to the user, who must check an event catalog against the acquisition date — an argument for the per-pixel date rasters of [Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md) and for a formal invalidation flag in metadata ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)). Where agencies do respond, the pattern is: a rapid-response product from whatever source is fastest — commercial stereo (Pléiades, WorldView, with DSMs within days), ArcticDEM/REMA-style strips from Maxar imagery produced on demand, drone photogrammetry over small areas, airborne lidar within weeks where it can be tasked (USGS post-event acquisitions under 3DEP, NCALM rapid-response flights) — explicitly labelled provisional, with stated accuracy derived from the sparse control available, and superseded by a validated product later. The provisional label matters: rapid-response DSMs are usually unvalidated, referenced to the pre-event control (which moved), and built from imagery with cloud and shadow gaps, and if ingested into a national compilation without the label they contaminate it with unknown error ([Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md)).

> **Rule of thumb.** After a M7+ earthquake within 100 km, treat every pre-event control point and every pre-event DEM in the area as unverified until (a) the agency publishes a deformation model or revised coordinates, or (b) you have re-observed enough control to estimate the displacement yourself. The rule extends to ~300 km for M8 and to the whole plate boundary region for M9. It does not apply to purely relative uses (slope, local drainage) within areas where deformation is smooth at the scale of the analysis.

## Then & now

The idea that earthquakes *displace* the ground, rather than merely shaking it, was established geodetically. After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, Harry Fielding Reid compared the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey's triangulation of 1851–65 and 1874–92 with the re-survey of 1906–07 and found that points on either side of the San Andreas Fault had been displaced in opposite senses, with the offset decaying away from the fault — from which he formulated the **elastic rebound** theory (Reid 1910): strain accumulates slowly across a locked fault and is released suddenly in slip. Triangulation was the only tool; the measurement took years and the precision was decimetres over tens of kilometres. The 1964 Alaska earthquake (M9.2) was mapped by George Plafker largely from biological evidence — the upper limit of dead barnacles and other intertidal organisms on uplifted shorelines gave vertical displacements of up to ~11 m on Montague Island, and drowned forests gave subsidence of ~2 m around Turnagain Arm — supplemented by re-levelling and tide-gauge records (Plafker 1965); the result was the first complete picture of a subduction earthquake's vertical deformation field and a key confirmation of plate tectonics.

GPS in the 1990s (Landers 1992, Northridge 1994) gave displacement vectors within weeks; the Landers interferogram from ERS-1 (Massonnet et al. 1993) put the coseismic field on a single image and began InSAR. By the 2010s, continuous GNSS gave displacements within minutes (and now feed earthquake early warning and tsunami models), Sentinel-1 made a coseismic interferogram available within days for essentially every significant event on land, and lidar differencing resolved near-field 3D deformation. Deformation models absorbed the results within months. The remaining lags are institutional: revising published coordinates, heights, charts, and DEMs still takes months to years, and most topographic products have no invalidation mechanism at all.

## Mathematics

**Okada (1985) elastic dislocation.** For a rectangular fault of length $L$ and width $W$ in a homogeneous elastic half-space (Poisson's ratio ν, usually 0.25), with top edge at depth $d$, dip δ, and uniform slip components $U_1$ (strike-slip), $U_2$ (dip-slip), and $U_3$ (tensile), Okada gives closed-form expressions for the surface displacement $(u_x, u_y, u_z)$ at any point. In Chinnery's notation, with $\xi$ along strike and $\eta$ down dip, each displacement component is a combination $f(\xi,\eta)\big\|$ evaluated at the four corners of the rectangle:

$$
f(\xi,\eta)\Big\| = f(x,p) - f(x,p-W) - f(x-L,p) + f(x-L,p-W),
$$

where $p = y\cos\delta + d\sin\delta$ and $q = y\sin\delta - d\cos\delta$. For strike-slip, the vertical component is

$$
u_z = -\frac{U_1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\tilde d\, q}{R(R+\eta)} + \frac{q\sin\delta}{R+\eta} + I_4 \sin\delta\right]\Bigg\|,
$$

with $R^2 = \xi^2 + \eta^2 + q^2$, $\tilde d = \eta\sin\delta - q\cos\delta$, and $I_4$ one of five auxiliary terms involving $\mu/(\lambda+\mu)$ that encode the Poisson-ratio dependence; the full set of expressions for all three slip types runs to a page and is reproduced in Okada's Table 6 and in every implementation. The practical points are: displacements scale linearly with slip and are fixed in shape by geometry; the far field decays as $M_0/r^2$ (point-source limit, $M_0 = \mu L W U$); a slip distribution is modelled as a sum of rectangular patches, each with its own Okada contribution, and inverted from GNSS/InSAR data by linear least squares with smoothing; and a deformation-model "patch" grid is simply the forward Okada field from the inverted slip distribution, evaluated on a regular grid and corrected to observed GNSS where they differ. Implementations: `okada_wrapper` (Python), `dc3d` in GMT-based and MATLAB codes, Coulomb 3.

**ICP for 3D displacement.** Given pre-event points $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}$ and post-event points $\{\mathbf{q}_j\}$ in a window, iterative closest point finds the rigid transformation $(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t})$ minimizing $\sum_i \|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{t} - \mathbf{q}_{c(i)}\|^2$ over correspondences $c(i)$ (nearest point, or point-to-plane using local normals), alternating between correspondence and the closed-form Procrustes solution. Nissen et al. (2012) apply it in overlapping windows (e.g. 50–200 m) and report $\mathbf{t}$ as the local 3D displacement and $\mathbf{R}$ as rotation; the window must contain non-planar texture (otherwise the along-plane components are unconstrained) and be small relative to the deformation gradient. Precision scales roughly as $\sigma_{\text{pt}}/\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}}$ with the point-cloud noise and effective point count, but systematic errors from differing point densities, vegetation, and the pre-event survey's own distortions (flight-line offsets, [Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)) usually dominate; stable far-field windows calibrate them.

**Volume with correlated error (preview of [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)).** For a DoD $\Delta z_i$ over $n$ cells of area $a$, $V = a\sum_i \Delta z_i$. With per-cell uncertainty $\sigma_{\Delta z}$ and a spatial correlation function $\rho(h)$, $\sigma_V^2 = a^2 \sigma_{\Delta z}^2 \sum_i\sum_j \rho(h_{ij})$. If $\rho$ has correlation range $L$ (exponential or spherical variogram) and the area $A = na \gg L^2$, the double sum is approximately $n \cdot (\pi L^2 / a) \cdot c$ with $c$ a constant of order 1 depending on the variogram shape, so $\sigma_V \approx \sigma_{\Delta z}\,\sqrt{A\,\pi L^2\, c}$ — independent of cell size, growing with $\sqrt{A}$ rather than $\sqrt{n}$, and larger than the naive estimate by $\sqrt{\pi L^2 c / a}$. A fully correlated component (bias $b$) adds $b A$ linearly. Hugonnet et al. (2022) treat multi-range correlation rigorously.

## Validation & uncertainty

**Sources of error peculiar to sudden events.** (1) *Displaced control*: a post-event survey tied to pre-event benchmark coordinates inherits the coseismic displacement as a bias — uniform if the control is in the far field, spatially variable if near the rupture — and a post-minus-pre DEM difference then shows a planar or warped offset that is the displacement of the control, not of the terrain. (2) *Horizontal displacement on slopes*: a purely horizontal offset $\delta$ produces apparent vertical change $\delta\tan\alpha$ on a slope α; 2 m of horizontal coseismic motion on a 20° hillside is 0.73 m of apparent elevation change. Vertical DoD cannot distinguish this from real erosion; ICP or 3D methods can. (3) *Temporal mixing*: a post-event DEM built from imagery spanning the event, or a pre-event DEM that already includes earlier events in a sequence (Christchurch 2010 vs 2011), attributes change to the wrong event. (4) *Provisional-product error*: rapid-response DSMs typically have vertical errors of metres (uncontrolled stereo) and unknown horizontal registration. (5) *Post-seismic drift*: comparisons made years after the event include decimetres of post-seismic motion that the coseismic patch does not cover.

**Tests.** Compute the DoD over areas known to be stable far from the rupture and verify it is zero within the LoD; if it shows a plane or ramp, the control or the registration is at fault. Decompose the near-field DoD by aspect: a horizontal displacement produces a $\cos(\text{aspect} - \text{azimuth})$ pattern in $\Delta z / \tan\alpha$ (the Nuth–Kääb test, [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)), and its amplitude is the horizontal offset. Compare lidar-ICP displacements with GNSS at every station in the area; the RMS difference is the method's uncertainty and the mean is its bias. For volumes, compute $n_{\text{eff}}$ from the variogram of the DoD over stable terrain, not from the cell count. For any product, record the deformation-model version and the epoch of every control coordinate used.

**What to report.** The event(s) spanned by the comparison; the deformation model and version applied, or an explicit statement that none was; control epochs; stable-area residual statistics; the method's 3D vs vertical nature; volume uncertainties with correlation treatment; and, for rapid-response products, the word "provisional" in the title and the metadata.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Post-event lidar DoD, M7 crustal earthquake, near-field hillslopes, 2 m of horizontal coseismic motion.
>
> | Component | Vertical effect | Treatment |
> |---|---|---|
> | Pre-event lidar σ (legacy, 1 pt/m²) | 0.20 m | Random; variogram range ~80 m |
> | Post-event lidar σ (10 pt/m²) | 0.07 m | Random |
> | Horizontal coseismic motion, 2 m, on 15° slopes | 0.54 m apparent | Systematic, aspect-dependent; remove with ICP/3D or Nuth–Kääb |
> | Control displacement if pre-event coordinates used | 0.1–2 m plane/warp | Systematic; re-observe control |
> | Vegetation/season difference | 0.1–0.3 m | Systematic by land cover; mask |
> | Post-seismic drift (if comparison delayed 2 yr) | 0.05–0.2 m | Systematic; apply post-seismic model |
>
> The random terms sum to ~0.21 m; the systematic terms, if untreated, are 2–10 times larger. Most "coseismic vertical deformation" maps from vertical DoD alone are maps of horizontal motion times slope.

- **NOAA Office of Coast Survey** — Navigation Response Team procedures and emergency survey priorities (Hydrographic Surveys Specifications and Deliverables, current ed.).
- **UNESCO/IOC Post-Tsunami Survey Field Guide** (2nd ed., 2014) — perishable-data priorities and run-up measurement protocols.
- **Copernicus Emergency Management Service** — rapid-mapping product specifications (grading and delineation maps; DSMs where produced), which define how provisional products are labelled.

## Pitfalls

- **Using pre-event control after a M7+ event** → datasheets and base-station files are not withdrawn automatically → check the event catalog and deformation-model status before every survey in a seismic region; re-observe.
- **"Finding" uniform offsets between post- and pre-event DEMs** → the control moved, not the terrain → test over stable far-field terrain; apply the deformation model to the control first.
- **Mapping horizontal motion as vertical change on slopes** → vertical DoD cannot separate them → use ICP/3D methods or the Nuth–Kääb aspect test; report horizontal displacement separately.
- **Ignoring post-seismic drift years later** → the coseismic patch is a step, the ground keeps moving → apply the post-seismic model or re-observe; state the epoch.
- **Volume estimates without error bars** → cell-count statistics give absurd precision → compute $n_{\text{eff}}$ from a variogram; include registration bias.
- **Forgetting that the seafloor moved too** → charts and bathymetric DEMs are assumed static → after subduction events, treat offshore bathymetry as displaced by the slip model; re-survey ports.
- **Ingesting provisional rapid-response DSMs into compilations** → urgency overrides labelling → keep a provisional flag that propagates into any derived product; replace when validated.
- **Mixing imagery from before and after the event in one DSM** → long acquisition windows → check per-image dates against the event time; split.
- **Treating lava-covered or landslide areas as "degraded accuracy"** → the surface no longer exists → withdraw the extent; do not revise an accuracy statement for terrain that is gone.
- **Assuming a national compilation (GEBCO, national DEM) has been updated after an event** → compilations lag by years → check the source and date layers.
- **Using an Okada model with the wrong sign or dip convention** → several conventions in circulation → verify against a published test case before using the field as a correction.

## Key takeaways

- After a large event, control is a hypothesis to be re-tested, not a given; suspend what is known to be wrong, measure, then model.
- Coseismic deformation is a datum event: metres near the rupture, centimetres to 1000+ km away, vertical steps that permanently change land–sea relationships — and it continues post-seismically for years.
- Agencies now revise coordinates within months (GSI 2011, LINZ 2016); users must track deformation-model versions and apply them to their own data.
- Measure with the tool that matches the near-field/far-field problem: GNSS for absolute points, InSAR for the regional field, optical correlation and lidar ICP for the near field in 3D, repeat MBES for the seafloor.
- Vertical DoD across an earthquake mostly shows horizontal motion times slope; use 3D methods.
- Eruptions and landslides replace terrain rather than degrade it; the metadata action is withdrawal of the extent and a provisional replacement, dated.
- Landslide and lava volumes need correlated-error treatment; naive cell-count statistics are wrong by one to two orders of magnitude.
- Rapid-response DEMs are valuable but must be labelled provisional and kept out of compilations until validated.
