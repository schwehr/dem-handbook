# Chapter 15 — SLAM: solving the map and the trajectory together (and mapping innerspace)

> **Part IV — Positioning and orientation.** The previous chapters positioned a sensor first and mapped second; this chapter closes Part IV with the formulation in which the map and the trajectory are unknowns of one estimation problem — the only practical approach where GNSS is absent and the method that increasingly underlies trajectory refinement everywhere.

**In this chapter.** Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) estimates a sensor's trajectory and a map of its environment from the sensor's own observations, with no external positioning. It is the technology inside handheld and backpack scanners, autonomous vehicles, drones in tunnels and under canopy, and the robots that map mines, caves, sewers, ship hulls, and buildings — the **innerspace** that no satellite can see. This chapter traces SLAM from its 1986 origins through filter- and graph-based formulations to today's lidar-inertial and visual-inertial systems; explains loop closure, ICP and NDT registration, and the factor-graph estimate that unifies them; shows that SLAM is the same least squares as bundle adjustment and survey network adjustment, with different priors; describes the handheld and backpack instruments and the environments they map; and treats georeferencing SLAM maps, evaluating trajectories (APE/RPE and the benchmark datasets), and the drift that turns a relatively accurate map into an absolutely wrong elevation product. You should finish able to say what a SLAM-derived DTM's accuracy is relative to, how to tie it to control, and why its covariances are almost always too small.

## 15.1 Origins and formulations

The problem was named and posed probabilistically by Smith & Cheeseman (1986 ⟨H⟩), who showed how to represent the uncertainty of a chain of relative spatial relationships (robot moves, robot observes landmark) as a joint Gaussian and propagate it consistently — the insight being that the robot's pose and the landmark positions are *correlated* through the observations, so that re-observing one landmark improves the estimates of all the others and of the trajectory. Smith, Self & Cheeseman (1990) and the mid-1990s work surveyed by Durrant-Whyte & Bailey (2006) made this **EKF-SLAM**: a Kalman filter whose state is the current pose plus every landmark, with a covariance matrix that grows quadratically with the number of landmarks and whose linearization errors accumulate over long trajectories. **Particle-filter SLAM** (FastSLAM, Montemerlo et al. 2002; grid-based Rao-Blackwellized filters) sampled trajectories and attached a map to each sample, handling non-Gaussian uncertainty and data association at the price of particle depletion in large loops.

The formulation that won is **graph SLAM** (Lu & Milios 1997; Thrun & Montemerlo 2006; Grisetti et al. 2010 tutorial): the unknowns are the poses along the trajectory (and optionally landmarks), each measurement is a **constraint** between two or more of them (odometry between consecutive poses, a scan registration between two poses, a landmark observation, a GNSS fix on one pose, a loop closure between distant poses), and the solution is the set of poses that minimizes the sum of squared, covariance-weighted constraint residuals. In the language of **factor graphs** (Dellaert & Kaess 2017), variables are poses and landmarks, factors are measurement likelihoods, and the maximum-a-posteriori estimate is a sparse nonlinear least-squares problem solved by Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt, exploiting the sparsity of the information matrix (each factor touches few variables) through sparse Cholesky or QR factorization — which is what **g2o** (Kümmerle et al. 2011), **GTSAM** (with its incremental iSAM2 solver, Kaess et al. 2012), and **Ceres Solver** do. The filter is a special case of the graph that marginalizes old poses; the graph keeps them, so that a loop closure found late can correct the whole trajectory — the capability that defines modern SLAM.

Two more pieces complete the vocabulary. The **front end** processes raw sensor data into constraints: feature extraction and matching, scan registration, IMU preintegration, place recognition. The **back end** optimizes the graph. Front-end mistakes — a wrong loop closure, a mis-registered scan — are the dominant failure mode, because the back end will cheerfully distort the whole map to satisfy a false constraint; robust kernels (Huber, Cauchy, switchable constraints, dynamic covariance scaling) and consistency checks on loop closures exist to limit the damage.

## 15.2 Lidar, visual, and visual-inertial SLAM; loop closure

**Lidar SLAM** registers successive scans (or scans against a local map) to estimate motion, then builds the global map and graph. **ICP** (iterative closest point; Besl & McKay 1992; Chen & Medioni 1992 for point-to-plane) alternates between finding nearest-neighbour correspondences and solving for the rigid transform that minimizes their distances; **GICP** (Segal, Haehnel & Thrun 2009) uses local surface covariances for a plane-to-plane metric; **NDT** (normal distributions transform; Biber & Straßer 2003; Magnusson 2009) models the reference scan as a grid of Gaussians and maximizes the likelihood of the new scan's points, which is smoother and faster for large scans. **LOAM** (Zhang & Singh 2014) extracted edge and planar features from spinning-lidar scans and ran a fast odometry and a slower mapping thread, establishing the architecture that **LeGO-LOAM** (ground-optimized), **LIO-SAM** (Shan et al. 2020; tightly coupled lidar-inertial odometry via smoothing and mapping, with IMU preintegration factors, loop closures, and GNSS factors in a GTSAM graph) and **FAST-LIO/FAST-LIO2** (Xu et al. 2022; an iterated error-state Kalman filter fusing raw lidar points and IMU at high rate with an incremental k-d tree map) refined. Google's **Cartographer** (Hess et al. 2016) combines local submap matching with a sparse pose graph and branch-and-bound loop closure, in 2D and 3D. **KISS-ICP** (Vizzo et al. 2023) showed that a carefully engineered point-to-point ICP with adaptive thresholds and constant-velocity prediction matches the accuracy of far more complex systems across datasets — a useful corrective to feature-heavy complexity. **Lidar-inertial** systems are the workhorses of mapping-grade handheld and mobile scanners because the IMU bridges fast rotations and featureless intervals where scan matching alone fails and provides the gravity direction that keeps the map level.

**Visual SLAM** uses cameras: monocular (scale unobservable, drift in scale as well as pose), stereo (metric scale from the baseline), or RGB-D (depth from structured light or time of flight, indoors). **ORB-SLAM** through **ORB-SLAM3** (Campos et al. 2021) is the reference feature-based system — ORB features, a covisibility graph, local bundle adjustment, a bag-of-words place recognizer for loop closure and relocalization, and in version 3 tight visual-inertial fusion and multi-map ("Atlas") handling. Direct methods (LSD-SLAM, DSO) minimize photometric error rather than reprojection error. **Visual-inertial** fusion (VINS-Mono, OpenVINS, ORB-SLAM3 VI, and the estimators inside ARKit/ARCore and drone autopilots) makes scale and gravity observable and tolerates brief visual loss; it drifts at roughly 0.5–2 % of distance in good conditions ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md), §14.3).

**Loop closure** is what distinguishes SLAM from odometry: recognizing that the sensor has returned to a previously mapped place and adding a constraint between the two poses, after which the back end distributes the accumulated drift around the loop. **Place recognition** proposes candidates — bag-of-visual-words descriptors (DBoW2) for images; Scan Context, global descriptors, or learned descriptors for lidar — and a geometric verification (ICP or feature matching with RANSAC) confirms them. False positives in repetitive environments (identical corridors, rows of warehouse racking, tunnel segments) are the classic catastrophe, folding the map onto itself; conservative thresholds and consistency checks (does the proposed closure agree with the odometry uncertainty?) are mandatory. Without loop closure, drift is unbounded and grows roughly linearly with distance for good lidar-inertial systems (0.1–1 % typical in structured environments) and faster in rotation-heavy or featureless ones.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Accuracy" in a SLAM paper is almost always **relative**: absolute trajectory error after aligning the estimated trajectory to ground truth by a best-fit rigid (or similarity) transform, or relative pose error over fixed sub-trajectory lengths. A handheld scanner whose brochure says "accuracy 1–3 cm" means (at best) that a point's position relative to nearby points, or the trajectory after alignment, is that good; it says nothing about where the map sits in a geodetic frame, which is undefined until control is added. A "SLAM DTM" has no absolute vertical accuracy until it is georeferenced — and the georeferencing's uncertainty, not the SLAM's, then dominates.

## 15.3 Relationship to bundle adjustment and survey network adjustment

Strip the robotics vocabulary and graph SLAM is **least-squares adjustment of a network of observations**, the same problem Gauss solved for Hanover ([Chapter 11](ch11-history-of-positioning.md)). A survey network adjustment has unknown station coordinates, observations (angles, distances, height differences, GNSS vectors) with weights, and a datum defined by fixing or weighting control; a photogrammetric **bundle adjustment** ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) has unknown camera poses and 3D points, observations (image coordinates) with weights, and a datum from ground control or GNSS camera positions; a SLAM back end has unknown poses (and points or surfels), observations (relative poses from scan matching, IMU preintegrations, landmark bearings), and a datum from — nothing, unless GNSS or control factors are added, in which case the graph is "gauge-fixed" by them and otherwise by fixing the first pose arbitrarily.

The differences are in the priors and the operating regime. Survey adjustments have few, carefully observed, redundant measurements with well-known stochastic models and expect residual analysis by a human; bundle adjustments have millions of automatically matched observations with outlier rejection; SLAM back ends have the same but must run online, marginalize or sparsify to stay bounded, and rely on a motion model (the IMU, constant velocity) as a strong prior between frames. The vocabulary maps directly: a loop closure is a *tie* between non-adjacent stations; IMU preintegration is a *dead-reckoning observation*; GNSS factors are *absolute position observations*; a robust kernel is *data snooping*. Lidar strip adjustment ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.5) and MB-System's `mbnavadjust` for multibeam are SLAM back ends applied to survey data after the fact; SfM is visual SLAM run offline with global optimization. Recognizing the identity lets you apply the survey discipline to SLAM outputs — redundancy, residual inspection, independent checks, honest covariances — and lets you borrow SLAM's robust estimation and sparse solvers for survey problems. [Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md) and [Appendix B](../appendices/appendix-b-math-reference.md) give the least-squares formalism.

## 15.4 Handheld and backpack scanners; mapping innerspace

The commercial expression of lidar-inertial SLAM is the **mobile handheld or backpack scanner**: a spinning multi-beam lidar (commonly a Velodyne/Ouster/Hesai 16–128-channel unit or a Livox solid-state unit), a MEMS or tactical IMU, cameras for colour and sometimes for visual odometry, and an onboard computer, carried by a walking operator. **GeoSLAM** ZEB (from 2013, the ZEB1 with a swinging 2D lidar on a spring, then ZEB Horizon and successors; now part of FARO), **Leica BLK2GO** (2019) and BLK2FLY (drone), **NavVis** VLX (wearable) and M6 (trolley) with their indoor-mapping heritage, **Emesent Hovermap** (handheld, vehicle-, or drone-mounted, with autonomy for beyond-line-of-sight flight in mines), **Kaarta** Stencil, and lidar backpacks from Greenvalley, Viametris, and others. Published and independently tested accuracies, relative, in built environments are typically 1–3 cm point noise and 1–3 cm relative accuracy over a room, degrading to decimetres over hundreds of metres without closures; drift figures quoted by vendors of 0.1–0.5 % of distance should be read as best-case structured-environment performance. The devices acquire 300 000–1 000 000+ points per second while walking at 1 m/s, covering a building floor in minutes; the trade for that speed is the error structure of this chapter.

Their domain is **innerspace**: environments that are enclosed, GNSS-denied, often dark, and often unsafe or impractical to survey conventionally. **Mines**, where handheld or drone-borne SLAM maps stopes, drives, and ore passes for volume reconciliation, convergence monitoring, and safety ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)); **caves and lava tubes**, mapped for science and for planetary analogues; **tunnels and sewers**, for condition assessment and as-built records; **ship interiors, tanks, and hulls** in dry dock; **buildings**, for BIM, floor plans, facility management, and the indoor layer of digital twins ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)); **forests under canopy**, where backpack and handheld lidar capture stems and the ground beneath them for under-canopy DTMs that airborne lidar cannot resolve in dense stands, and for forest inventory ([Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md)); **construction sites**, for progress and earthwork volumes; and **disaster sites**, where a robot or a drone enters a damaged structure (the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, 2018–2021, drove much of the current lidar-inertial state of the art in exactly these conditions).

The output is a point cloud in an arbitrary local frame, oriented with gravity down (the IMU supplies that) and with the starting pose at the origin, from which floor surfaces, walls, and — outdoors or under canopy — a ground surface can be extracted by the methods of [Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md). Its "elevations" are heights above wherever the scan started. In a mine, "elevation" in the mine grid is a surveyed quantity tied to control stations in the drives; a SLAM scan becomes a mine-grid product only by registering it to those stations, and drift between stations is the error.

## 15.5 Georeferencing SLAM maps and evaluating trajectories

A SLAM map becomes an elevation product only when it is tied to a geodetic frame and vertical datum ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md), [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). Three approaches, usually combined:

**Control points.** Targets (spheres, checkerboards, reflective markers) or natural features surveyed by total station or GNSS in the project frame, identified in the point cloud, and used either (a) to compute a rigid or similarity transform of the finished map — which corrects the datum but not the drift, so residuals at the control grow with distance from the centroid — or (b) as **prior factors in the graph** before optimization, which lets the back end redistribute drift between control points and is the correct approach for anything longer than a room. Vendors' software (GeoSLAM Connect, Emesent Aura, NavVis IVION, Leica Cyclone REGISTER 360) offers both; prefer (b) and demand per-control residuals. The spacing of control determines the residual drift: with 0.3 % drift and control every 100 m, the mid-span error is of order 15 cm before adjustment and a few centimetres after, if the drift is smooth.

**GNSS where available.** Outdoor and transitional segments can carry a GNSS receiver whose fixes become position factors (LIO-SAM, Hovermap, and most backpack systems support this); the graph then has absolute constraints wherever sky was visible and interpolates through the indoor or under-canopy segments. The GNSS fixes' quality flags must be respected — an RTK float or multipath-corrupted fix entered as a tight prior distorts the map more than no fix at all — and the lever arm from antenna to lidar must be known ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). **Indoor–outdoor registration** is the specific problem of carrying a frame from the GNSS-rich exterior through a doorway into a building or down a portal into a mine: the transition zone has degraded GNSS and often weak geometry (a doorway is a planar scene); scanning the exterior façade and the entrance in one continuous pass, with targets on both sides of the threshold, gives the graph the constraints to bridge it. The same issue arises for the land–water seam in bathymetry ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md), §9.8) and for airborne-to-terrestrial merges ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).

**Registration to an existing georeferenced dataset.** Aligning a SLAM cloud to an airborne lidar DSM, a terrestrial scan, or a BIM model by ICP or feature matching inherits that dataset's accuracy and datum — the TRN logic of [Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md), with the same caveat that the reference's errors become yours and that ICP on a flat ground surface constrains the vertical and the tilt but not the horizontal.

**Evaluating trajectories.** The SLAM community evaluates against ground truth with two metrics (Sturm et al. 2012; Zhang & Scaramuzza 2018). **Absolute trajectory error (ATE/APE)** aligns the estimated trajectory to the reference by a rigid (SE(3)) or similarity (Sim(3), for monocular) transform in a least-squares sense and reports the RMS of the remaining position differences; it measures global consistency but, by construction, removes any constant offset and (for Sim(3)) scale — so it cannot measure georeferencing accuracy. **Relative pose error (RPE)** compares the relative motion over sub-trajectories of fixed length (e.g., 100–800 m, as in KITTI) or fixed time and reports translational drift as a percentage and rotational drift in degrees per metre; it measures local accuracy independent of loop closures. The standard benchmarks — **KITTI** (car, outdoor, GNSS/INS ground truth; Geiger et al. 2012), **EuRoC MAV** (drone indoors, motion-capture and laser-tracker truth; Burri et al. 2016), **TUM RGB-D** (handheld indoors, motion capture; Sturm et al. 2012), **Newer College** and **Hilti SLAM Challenge** (handheld lidar-inertial in construction and campus settings, with total-station-surveyed targets as truth; Helmberger et al. 2022) — report these metrics, and the Hilti challenge in particular is close to the surveying use case because its ground truth is survey control, not motion capture. Typical leading results: KITTI translational drift 0.5–1 % for lidar odometry; Hilti handheld lidar-inertial errors of 1–5 cm at the control points for the best systems in 2022–2023, with failures (decimetres to metres) on the harder sequences — stairs, long featureless corridors, outdoor-to-indoor transitions. The open-source `evo` package computes APE/RPE from trajectory files and is the tool to use when you evaluate a vendor's scanner against your own total-station truth.

## 15.6 Where SLAM-derived terrain fits, and where it fails

SLAM-derived point clouds become elevation products in three settings where nothing else works as well. **Under-canopy DTMs**: backpack or handheld lidar in dense forest sees the ground between stems from 1–2 m away with thousands of points per square metre, where airborne lidar might deliver a few ground returns per square metre or none; the resulting DTM is locally excellent (centimetres) and absolutely only as good as the GNSS fixes in gaps and the control carried in from the forest edge — often decimetres over a few hundred metres ([Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md), [Chapter 35](ch35-voids-and-overhangs.md)). **Construction and earthworks**: daily or weekly volumes from handheld or vehicle-mounted SLAM scans on sites where GNSS is intermittent among structures and excavations; control is usually abundant (site benchmarks) and the relative accuracy over tens of metres is what volumes need ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)). **Urban canyons and interiors**: street-level and indoor surfaces (floors, ramps, stairs, underpasses, station platforms) for accessibility, flood modelling of basements and subways, and digital twins, where the SLAM map supplies geometry the airborne DSM cannot see ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md), [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)).

It fails, predictably, where its assumptions fail. **Featureless or self-similar geometry** — long straight tunnels, smooth corridors, open flat fields, water surfaces, snowfields — gives scan matching nothing to constrain motion along the degenerate direction; the trajectory slides along the tunnel axis, and the scanner may report confident nonsense. (Vendors add wheel odometry, visual features, or fiducial targets along tunnels for this reason.) **Long loops without closure** — out-and-back traverses, linear corridors, forest transects — accumulate drift that no back end can remove without a tie, and the drift is typically a slow bend and a vertical sag or rise that looks like a gentle slope in the DTM. **Dynamic scenes** — crowds, traffic, moving machinery — inject points that corrupt registration unless filtered ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)). **Fast rotations and poor IMU** — swinging the scanner around a corner faster than the IMU and scan rate can follow — produce smeared or doubled walls. **Reflective and transparent surfaces** — glass façades, wet floors, mirrors, water — return ghost geometry. **Scale drift** in monocular visual SLAM makes a building 2 % larger at one end than the other. And **repetitive structure** invites false loop closures, which fold the map.

> **Case file.** The Hilti SLAM Challenge 2022 (Helmberger et al. 2022) provided handheld lidar-inertial datasets from construction sites and a campus with ground truth from total-station-surveyed control points (millimetre-level) rather than motion capture, and scored submissions by position error at those points. The published analysis shows that systems competitive on KITTI and EuRoC failed on sequences with stairs, narrow corridors, and transitions between floors — environments with little horizontal structure and rapid attitude change — with errors jumping from centimetres to decimetres or metres on specific sequences while remaining small on others. The practical reading: SLAM accuracy is environment-dependent to a degree that no single specification captures; the control-point residuals *in your environment* are the only accuracy figure that applies to your data.

## 15.7 Uncertainty in SLAM outputs

SLAM back ends compute covariances — the inverse of the information matrix at the solution, or its marginals — and most pipelines report them for the trajectory and sometimes per point. They are **almost always optimistic**, for reasons that are structural rather than careless (Cadena et al. 2016 discuss this as an open problem):

1. **Measurement covariances are guesses.** The covariance assigned to a scan-matching result (ICP, NDT) is usually either a fixed constant or derived from the Hessian of the matching cost, which captures the geometric conditioning but not the correspondence errors, the sensor's systematic errors (range bias, beam divergence, intensity-dependent range), or the environment's effect (moving objects, vegetation); Censi (2007) gives a principled ICP covariance that is still optimistic in practice. IMU noise parameters come from datasheets or Allan analysis and ignore vibration and temperature.
2. **Errors are correlated in time and space.** Consecutive scan matches share the same local map and the same systematic errors; treating them as independent factors overstates the information by a large factor, which is why graphs with thousands of factors report millimetre covariances on trajectories that are decimetres wrong.
3. **Linearization and marginalization.** Covariances are computed at the linearization point; after marginalizing old states (filters, sliding windows), the retained covariance is an approximation that can become inconsistent (too small) — the EKF-SLAM inconsistency literature documents this (Julier & Uhlmann 2001; Huang, Mourikis & Roumeliotis 2010).
4. **Outliers and wrong associations are not in the model.** A false loop closure or a mis-registered scan is a gross error with probability zero under the Gaussian model; its effect on the solution is unbounded and its effect on the reported covariance is nil.
5. **Datum uncertainty is excluded.** The covariance is relative to the gauge (the fixed first pose or the control); the georeferencing uncertainty must be added separately and usually is not.

The practical consequence: **treat SLAM covariances as lower bounds on relative uncertainty, and determine the real uncertainty empirically** — by the residuals at control points not used in the solution, by repeat scans of the same space, by comparison with a terrestrial laser scan or total-station survey of a sample of surfaces, and by the APE/RPE against a surveyed trajectory where one can be obtained. For an elevation product, add the georeferencing uncertainty (transform or control-factor residuals) and the vertical-datum uncertainty in quadrature, and report the result as a function of distance from control, since that is how it actually varies.

## Then & now

- **1986 ⟨H⟩ — Smith & Cheeseman** pose the problem of estimating spatial relationships with uncertainty; the term SLAM is coined in the mid-1990s (Durrant-Whyte and colleagues).
- **1990s — EKF-SLAM** with landmark maps; quadratic cost and inconsistency limit it to small environments. Lu & Milios (1997) formulate globally consistent range-scan alignment — the first pose graph.
- **2002–2006 — Particle filters** (FastSLAM, GMapping) make large 2D occupancy-grid maps practical for indoor robots; the DARPA Grand Challenge (2005) and Urban Challenge (2007) put lidar on cars and push 3D.
- **2006–2012 — Graph SLAM matures**: sparse solvers (√SAM, iSAM, g2o, Ceres), the 2010 tutorial by Grisetti et al., and the realization that the problem is sparse least squares. Kinect (2010) makes RGB-D SLAM (KinectFusion 2011) a desktop phenomenon.
- **2013–2016 — Products**: GeoSLAM ZEB1 (2013) commercializes handheld lidar SLAM; ORB-SLAM (2015), LOAM (2014), Cartographer (2016) become the open-source baselines; cars and drones ship visual-inertial odometry (Google Tango 2014, ARKit/ARCore 2017).
- **2016 — Cadena et al.** survey "past, present, and future" and name the open problems: robustness, long-term operation, metric-semantic maps, and honest uncertainty.
- **2018–2021 — DARPA Subterranean Challenge** drives lidar-inertial SLAM in tunnels, caves, and urban underground; LIO-SAM (2020) and FAST-LIO2 (2022) become the standard lidar-inertial baselines; the Hilti SLAM Challenge (2021–2023) evaluates against survey control.
- **2020s — Convergence**: lidar strip adjustment, SfM, and SLAM back ends share solvers; GNSS/INS post-processing adds scan-matching constraints; surveying firms adopt handheld SLAM for interiors and under-canopy work while demanding survey-style QA; learned front ends (place recognition, feature matching) and neural scene representations (NeRF, Gaussian splatting — [Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md)) enter the map representation. The accuracy question has not changed: relative unless tied to control.

## Mathematics

**Pose-graph optimization.** Let $\mathbf{x} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ be poses in SE(3) (or SE(2)), and let each measurement $z_{ij}$ be a relative pose between $x_i$ and $x_j$ with information matrix $\Omega_{ij}$ (inverse covariance). The residual is $e_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) = \log\!\big(z_{ij}^{-1}\,(x_i^{-1} x_j)\big)^\vee$, the 6-vector of the error pose; the objective is

$$\mathbf{x}^\ast = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}} e_{ij}(\mathbf{x})^\mathsf{T}\,\Omega_{ij}\,e_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) \;+\; \sum_{k} e_k^{prior}(\mathbf{x})^\mathsf{T}\Omega_k e_k^{prior}(\mathbf{x}),$$

where prior terms include GNSS fixes, control points, and the gauge constraint. Gauss–Newton linearizes $e_{ij}(\mathbf{x} \boxplus \Delta) \approx e_{ij} + J_{ij}\Delta$ and solves the normal equations $H\Delta = -b$ with $H = \sum J_{ij}^\mathsf{T}\Omega_{ij}J_{ij}$ (sparse; block $(i,j)$ nonzero only for connected poses) and $b = \sum J_{ij}^\mathsf{T}\Omega_{ij}e_{ij}$, then updates $\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} \boxplus \Delta$ on the manifold. A robust kernel $\rho(\cdot)$ replaces the squared norm, $\sum \rho\big(e^\mathsf{T}\Omega e\big)$, down-weighting large residuals (Huber: quadratic to a threshold, linear beyond). The estimated covariance of the poses is $H^{-1}$ at convergence — the quantity §15.7 warns is optimistic.

**Factor-graph MAP estimate.** With variables $\Theta$ and factors $\phi_k(\Theta_k) \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\|h_k(\Theta_k) - z_k\|^2_{\Sigma_k}\big)$, the MAP estimate is $\Theta^\ast = \arg\max \prod_k \phi_k = \arg\min \sum_k \|h_k(\Theta_k) - z_k\|^2_{\Sigma_k}$, where $\|r\|^2_\Sigma = r^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}r$. The pose graph is the special case where $h_k$ is a relative pose; IMU preintegration contributes factors between consecutive poses, velocities, and biases; a GNSS factor is $h(x_i) = t_i + R_i\,\mathbf{l}$ (position of the antenna via the lever arm) with $\Sigma$ from the receiver's (optimistic) covariance; a control-point factor is the observed target position in the map frame.

**ICP objective.** Point-to-point: $\min_{R,t}\sum_i w_i\,\|R\,p_i + t - q_{c(i)}\|^2$ over correspondences $c(i)$ (closed form by SVD for fixed correspondences, iterated). Point-to-plane: $\min_{R,t}\sum_i w_i\,\big((R\,p_i + t - q_{c(i)})\cdot n_{c(i)}\big)^2$, converging faster on smooth surfaces. GICP: $\min_{T}\sum_i d_i^\mathsf{T}\big(C^Q_i + T C^P_i T^\mathsf{T}\big)^{-1} d_i$ with $d_i = q_i - T p_i$ and local covariances $C$. ICP converges to the nearest local minimum; its basin is a few times the scan's structure scale, which is why it needs an initial guess (IMU, constant velocity) and why it drifts along directions the geometry does not constrain — in a corridor, the Hessian $\sum J^\mathsf{T}J$ has a near-zero eigenvalue along the axis, and the solution along that axis is set by the prior alone.

**Trajectory error metrics.** Given estimated poses $P_i$ and ground truth $Q_i$ at matched times, APE first finds the alignment $S = \arg\min_S \sum_i \|\mathrm{trans}(Q_i) - S\,\mathrm{trans}(P_i)\|^2$ over SE(3) (or Sim(3) for monocular), then reports $\mathrm{APE}_{RMSE} = \sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_i\|\mathrm{trans}(Q_i^{-1} S P_i)\|^2}$. RPE over a step $\Delta$ (in frames, time, or distance) is $E_i = (Q_i^{-1}Q_{i+\Delta})^{-1}(P_i^{-1}P_{i+\Delta})$, with $\mathrm{RPE}_{trans} = \sqrt{\tfrac1m\sum_i\|\mathrm{trans}(E_i)\|^2}$ and the rotational part from the angle of $\mathrm{rot}(E_i)$; KITTI reports the translational RPE as a percentage of the segment length averaged over 100–800 m and the rotational RPE in °/m. For elevation work, compute the vertical component of APE separately and *do not* apply the alignment when assessing a georeferenced product — the un-aligned position differences at control points are the absolute accuracy.

**Drift growth.** For odometry with independent per-step errors, position error grows as $\sqrt{n}\,\sigma_{step}$ (random walk) in translation; a heading error $\delta\psi$ accumulated early produces a position error $L\,\delta\psi$ at distance $L$, so rotation drift dominates at long range — 0.1° at 500 m is 0.9 m. A loop closure constrains the end to the start and, for smooth drift, reduces the mid-loop error to roughly a quarter of the open-loop end error.

## Validation & uncertainty

**How errors arise.** From the front end: registration bias (systematic range or incidence-angle errors of the lidar; mis-matched correspondences on vegetation or edges), degenerate geometry (corridors, flat fields), dynamic objects, false loop closures, timing offsets between lidar, IMU, and camera (the same arithmetic as [Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md): 1 ms at 60 °/s handheld rotation is 0.06°, which at 20 m is 2 cm). From the back end: linearization at a poor initial guess, over-confident factor weights, marginalization inconsistency. From georeferencing: control identification error (a sphere centre from a sparse scan is a few millimetres; a natural feature is centimetres), control survey error, lever arms, and the choice of rigid transform versus in-graph priors. From the environment: scale drift (monocular), glass and water, crowds.

**How they propagate into elevation products.** Drift produces smooth, low-frequency surface deformation — a tilt or sag of the extracted floor or ground — that is invisible in the point cloud's local quality and shows up only against control or an independent surface; false loop closures produce step discontinuities and duplicated surfaces; timing and registration noise produce thickened surfaces (a floor 3–5 cm "thick" in cross-section) that bias a gridded DTM by the thickness asymmetry. A SLAM DTM compared to an airborne lidar DTM without co-registration will show an apparent systematic difference that is the sum of the SLAM drift, the georeferencing offset, and the airborne lidar's own error ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md), [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)); co-register on stable hard surfaces first, then compare where the methods should differ (under canopy, indoors).

**How to test.** (1) **Withheld control**: survey more targets than you use; constrain the graph with a subset; report residuals at the rest as a function of distance from the used control. (2) **Repeat scans**: scan the same space twice (different paths, different directions) and difference the extracted surfaces; the spread is the precision, and a consistent pattern (always sagging mid-corridor) is the drift signature. (3) **Reference surfaces**: compare floors, roads, and walls against a terrestrial laser scan or total-station profile over a sample; report bias and spread per surface. (4) **Trajectory metrics** against a surveyed trajectory (robotic total station tracking a prism on the scanner gives a 1–2 cm truth at 10–20 Hz) using `evo`: APE after alignment for relative quality, un-aligned error for absolute. (5) **Cross-section inspection**: cut 10 cm slabs through walls and floors; surface thickness and doubling diagnose timing, registration, and false closures. (6) **Closure consistency**: for every loop closure, record the pre-closure drift it corrected; a closure that moves the trajectory by far more than the accumulated odometry uncertainty predicts is suspect.

**What to report.** Scanner, lidar, and IMU models; SLAM software and version with the parameter set; whether loop closures occurred and where; control: number, survey method and accuracy, which were used as constraints and which withheld, with residuals; georeferencing method (rigid transform vs in-graph) and frame/datum/geoid; repeat-scan or reference-surface statistics; and an explicit statement of the relative accuracy (over what distance) and the absolute accuracy (at what confidence, relative to what control). Appendix G includes a SLAM-survey checklist ([Appendix G](../appendices/appendix-g-checklists.md)).

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical uncertainty (1σ) of a floor or ground surface from a handheld lidar-inertial scanner in three scenarios, order-of-magnitude values from vendor tests and the benchmark literature cited.
>
> | Component | Single room, 20 m, 1 loop | Building floor, 300 m path, closures, 6 control | Forest transect, 400 m out-and-back, GNSS at ends only |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Point noise (lidar range + registration) | 1–2 cm | 1–2 cm | 2–3 cm (vegetation returns) |
> | Drift between closures/control | < 1 cm | 2–5 cm | 20–60 cm mid-transect |
> | Georeferencing (control/GNSS residual) | 1 cm (targets) | 1–2 cm | 3–5 cm (PPK at ends) |
> | Timing / attitude smear | < 1 cm | 1 cm | 1–2 cm (fast rotations) |
> | Vertical datum (geoid, control height) | 1–2 cm | 1–2 cm | 2–3 cm |
> | Total (RSS) | ≈ 2–3 cm | ≈ 3–6 cm | ≈ 20–60 cm |
>
> The third column is why under-canopy SLAM DTMs need control or GNSS fixes *along* the transect, not just at the ends, and why "1–3 cm accuracy" on the brochure is the first column.

> **Try it.** Evaluate a SLAM trajectory against a total-station-tracked truth with `evo`, reporting both aligned (relative) and un-aligned (absolute) errors — the distinction this chapter insists on.
>
> ```bash
> pip install evo --upgrade
> # est.tum and truth.tum: "timestamp tx ty tz qx qy qz qw" per line, truth in the project frame.
> # 1. Absolute (no alignment): how far is the georeferenced trajectory from the truth?
> evo_ape tum truth.tum est.tum --pose_relation trans_part --plot_mode xz --save_results ape_abs.zip
> # 2. Relative (SE(3) alignment removes the datum offset): how good is the SLAM itself?
> evo_ape tum truth.tum est.tum --align --pose_relation trans_part --save_results ape_rel.zip
> # 3. Drift: translational RPE over 10 m segments, as a percentage
> evo_rpe tum truth.tum est.tum --delta 10 --delta_unit m --pose_relation trans_part --save_results rpe10.zip
> evo_res ape_abs.zip ape_rel.zip rpe10.zip --use_filenames
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: for a well-georeferenced handheld scan, un-aligned APE of a few centimetres and aligned APE slightly lower; a large gap between them (e.g., 25 cm vs 2 cm) means the SLAM is fine and the georeferencing is not — fix the control, not the algorithm. RPE over 10 m at 0.2–0.5 % is typical; RPE that jumps in specific segments locates the corridors or stairwells where the front end struggled. Inspect the `--plot_mode xz` output for the vertical component specifically; it is often the worst axis for handheld scans because the IMU's gravity reference is good but the scan geometry of floors is weak along the vertical.

## Software

**Open source:** **ROS 2** — the integration framework for most research and many commercial systems; **Cartographer** (Google; 2D/3D lidar SLAM with submaps and pose graph; maintained by the community since 2021 — check status); **LIO-SAM** (lidar-inertial with GTSAM back end, GNSS factors supported; the most common starting point for mapping-grade lidar-inertial work); **FAST-LIO2** (very fast iterated-EKF lidar-inertial odometry; no loop closure by itself — pair with a pose-graph layer); **KISS-ICP** (minimal, robust lidar odometry; a good baseline to understand before adding complexity); **ORB-SLAM3** (visual, visual-inertial, RGB-D, multi-map; research licence — GPL); **OpenVINS** (visual-inertial filter with calibration); **GTSAM**, **g2o**, **Ceres Solver** (back ends); **Open3D** and **PCL** (ICP, GICP, registration, point-cloud processing); **evo** (trajectory evaluation); **PDAL** for turning the resulting clouds into gridded products ([Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)). Caveat: open-source SLAM systems are tuned for their benchmark datasets; expect to retune for your scanner and environment, and to add loop closure, control, and QA yourself. **Free but closed:** vendor viewers (e.g., Emesent Commander/Aura viewer tiers, GeoSLAM Draw viewers) — read-only. **Commercial:** **GeoSLAM Connect / FARO** (processing and georeferencing of ZEB data, control-point adjustment); **Leica Cyclone REGISTER 360 / Cyclone 3DR** (registration, control, and BLK2GO processing); **NavVis IVION** (indoor spatial data platform, control-point alignment); **Emesent Aura** (Hovermap processing with GNSS and control, mine-grid registration); **Kaarta Engine**; **Trimble RealWorks** and **Autodesk ReCap** for downstream registration; and mine-survey packages (Deswik, Maptek PointStudio) that import SLAM clouds against mine-grid control. Caveat: commercial packages differ in whether control is applied as a post-hoc transform or as constraints in the optimization — ask, because the residual drift differs by an order of magnitude.

## Standards & guides

- ASPRS, *Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data*, Edition 2 (2023) — relative and absolute accuracy definitions and checkpoint requirements that apply to SLAM-derived products once georeferenced.
- U.S. Institute of Building Documentation (USIBD), *Level of Accuracy (LOA) Specification for Building Documentation*, Version 3.0 (2019) — LOA10–LOA50 ranges (5 cm to 1 mm) for as-built capture, the de facto accuracy language for indoor SLAM deliverables.
- ISO 17123-9:2018 (optics and optical instruments — field procedures for testing geodetic and surveying instruments — terrestrial laser scanners) and the DIN/VDI 2634 series — test procedures adaptable to handheld scanners (verify).
- BSI PAS 128:2022 (underground utility detection, verification and location) and PAS 1192/ISO 19650 series — survey and information-management requirements where SLAM scans feed BIM.
- IEEE P1873 / IEEE Std 1873-2015, *Standard for Robot Map Data Representation for Navigation* — map data interchange (limited uptake).
- Mine surveying regulations (e.g., Australian state mine survey codes; U.S. MSHA 30 CFR Part 75.1200 map requirements) — the accuracy and control requirements that SLAM mine scans must meet to be accepted as survey records (verify for the applicable jurisdiction).
- Benchmark protocols: KITTI odometry evaluation (Geiger et al. 2012), TUM RGB-D (Sturm et al. 2012), EuRoC (Burri et al. 2016), Hilti SLAM Challenge (Helmberger et al. 2022) — the de facto standards for APE/RPE reporting.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating SLAM output as surveyed** → the brochure's "1–3 cm" is relative; without loop closure drift is 0.1–1 % of distance (decimetres per few hundred metres), and monocular systems drift in scale → tie to control, withhold some, report residuals versus distance.
- **Comparing a SLAM DTM to a lidar DTM without co-registration** → the difference mixes SLAM drift, georeferencing offset, and the reference's own error → co-register on stable hard surfaces, then compare only where the methods should agree; treat residual low-frequency patterns as drift.
- **Indoor "elevations" with no vertical-datum tie** → the scan's z is height above the starting pose; floor levels in a building model become datum-less numbers → survey at least one benchmark tie per building (levelled from an outdoor benchmark) and state the datum.
- **Control applied as a post-hoc rigid transform on a long traverse** → the drift remains; residuals grow with distance from the centroid → use control as constraints in the graph optimization.
- **Accepting every loop closure** → false closures in repetitive environments fold the map → require geometric verification and odometry-consistency checks; inspect closures that move the trajectory more than the accumulated uncertainty predicts.
- **Scanning corridors and tunnels without added structure** → degenerate geometry lets the trajectory slide along the axis → place targets or objects, add wheel odometry or visual features, walk with lateral motion, and close loops.
- **Trusting the reported covariance** → correlated, mis-weighted, and outlier-blind factors make it optimistic by large factors → determine uncertainty empirically (withheld control, repeat scans, reference surfaces).
- **Fast rotations around corners and on stairs** → attitude smear doubles walls and thickens floors → move the scanner slowly through turns; check cross-sections for surface thickness.
- **Entering a building without bridging constraints** → GNSS degrades in the doorway; the indoor map floats relative to the outdoor frame → scan façade and entrance continuously with targets on both sides of the threshold.
- **Dynamic objects left in the registration** → people and vehicles bias scan matching → filter moving points (range-image consistency, dynamic-object removal) before or during registration ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)).
- **Vegetation returns in under-canopy ground extraction** → low vegetation and litter thicken the ground surface by centimetres to decimetres → classify conservatively ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)); validate against total-station ground shots.
- **Benchmark numbers as project expectations** → KITTI/EuRoC performance does not transfer to your mine or forest → run a pilot with control in the actual environment.

## Key takeaways

- SLAM is least squares over a graph of poses and observations with a map as part of the unknowns — the same mathematics as bundle adjustment and survey network adjustment, with different priors and an online operating regime.
- Its accuracy is **relative** unless tied to control or GNSS; drift of 0.1–1 % of distance without loop closure is normal, and the vertical is often the worst axis for handheld scans.
- Loop closure bounds drift and false loop closure destroys maps; place recognition needs geometric verification and consistency checks.
- Georeference by putting control and GNSS fixes *into* the optimization, not by transforming the finished map; carry the frame through indoor–outdoor transitions with targets on both sides.
- Evaluate with APE (aligned for relative quality, un-aligned for absolute) and RPE (drift), against survey-grade truth in your own environment; the Hilti-style control-point residual is the figure that matters for elevation products.
- SLAM covariances are lower bounds; determine real uncertainty from withheld control, repeat scans, and reference surfaces, and report it as a function of distance from control.
- SLAM is the only practical option in GNSS-denied innerspace — mines, caves, tunnels, buildings, under canopy — and produces locally excellent surfaces whose absolute elevation is exactly as good as the tie you give them.
- Report the scanner, software, closures, control (used and withheld, with residuals), georeferencing method, datum, and both relative and absolute accuracy statements with every SLAM-derived product.
