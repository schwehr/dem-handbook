# Chapter 59 — Vector data and DEMs: points, lines, polygons, breaklines, contours, topology

> **Part XIII — Vectors, grids, and location codes.** Elevation data are usually thought of as rasters and point clouds; this chapter is about the vector geometry that feeds them (breaklines, shorelines, control points), that is produced from them (contours, footprints), and that silently corrupts them when its third dimension is undefined.

**In this chapter.** Every DEM project is also a vector project. Breaklines and hydro-flattening polygons decide where the surface bends and where it is flat; ground control and checkpoints arrive as point tables; contours and building footprints leave as lines and polygons; and shorelines, obstacle files, and road centrelines are draped on the DEM to make the products people actually use. Yet the vector formats and APIs that carry this geometry treat Z as an afterthought — GeoJSON names a vertical reference few producers honour, the shapefile has none, CAD has no CRS at all — and the topological rules that would catch a contour crossing or a river flowing uphill are rarely run. This chapter covers geometry types and their 3D semantics across the common formats; breaklines and mass points; contours as data and the losses of a DEM → contour → DEM round trip; shoreline and water polygons and their tidal references; footprints, obstacles, and the question of *which* point carries the height; GCP and checkpoint schemas; topology checks as DEM quality control; draping, 3D length and surface area, rasterization rules; generalization; and 3D vector delivery on the web. You should finish able to specify Z unambiguously, to use vectors to constrain and test a DEM, and to know how much error each vector operation adds.

## 59.1 Geometry types and their 3D semantics

The **OGC Simple Features** model (ISO 19125) defines the geometry vocabulary nearly every GIS uses: **Point**, **LineString**, **Polygon** (an exterior ring with zero or more interior rings — holes), their **Multi** collections, and **GeometryCollection**. Rings must be closed and simple, holes must lie inside the exterior, and ring orientation is a convention in Simple Features (GeoJSON, by contrast, mandates counter-clockwise exteriors in RFC 7946). Each coordinate may carry a **Z** (elevation) and an **M** (measure), giving `PointZ`, `LineStringZM`, and so on; WKT/WKB encode these, and GEOS, JTS, Shapely, PostGIS, and GDAL/OGR all implement them.

The model is **2.5D**, not 3D, and the distinction matters for elevation work. A Simple Features geometry is a planar object with a Z attached to each vertex: predicates (intersects, contains, touches) and operations (buffer, union, intersection) are computed in the XY plane, and Z is carried along, not reasoned about. Two `LineStringZ` features that cross in plan "intersect" even if one is a bridge 20 m above the other; a `PolygonZ` cannot be vertical, cannot have two Z values at one XY position, and cannot represent an overhang. Genuinely **3D** models — solids with faces and volumes, as in CityGML/CityJSON, IFC, and mesh formats — belong to a different family with different validity rules ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)). PostGIS's `ST_3DIntersects` and SFCGAL bridge the gap for a few operations, but assume any vector tool is computing in plan unless its documentation says otherwise.

What Z *means* is left to the format and, too often, to the producer's memory. The common formats differ in what they can say about it:

| Format | Z supported | Vertical CRS recorded | Horizontal CRS recorded | Other limits relevant to DEM work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shapefile (1998) | Yes (PointZ, PolyLineZ, PolygonZ, MultiPointZ; M optional) | No — `.prj` is a WKT1 horizontal CRS only | Yes (`.prj`, ESRI WKT) | 2 GB per component file; 10-character field names; no null vs empty geometry; multipatch is the only "3D" |
| GeoJSON (RFC 7946, 2016) | Yes (third position element) | Specified as metres above/below the WGS 84 ellipsoid — but see box | Fixed: WGS 84 lon/lat; `crs` member removed | No topology; precision by decimal places; antimeridian splitting rule |
| GeoPackage (OGC, 1.0 2014 → 1.4 2024) | Yes (Z and M flags in the geometry header) | Yes — `gpkg_spatial_ref_sys` may hold a compound CRS (WKT2) | Yes | SQLite container; 3D extension for compound CRS is optional and often unused by writers |
| GeoParquet (OGC, 1.0 2023, 1.1 2024) | Yes (WKB with Z) | Yes — column metadata `crs` as PROJJSON, may be compound | Yes | Columnar; bounding boxes 2D; Z semantics depend on the writer populating the CRS |
| FlatGeobuf | Yes (hasZ, hasM) | CRS as code/WKT in header; vertical if compound | Yes | Streaming, spatially indexed; CRS commonly written as 2D EPSG code |
| LandXML 1.2 (2008) | Yes (surfaces as TIN points/faces; alignments; profiles) | Only as free-text `CoordinateSystem` metadata | Same free-text element; frequently absent | Civil-engineering exchange; local site grids and surface units are conventions, not fields |
| DXF/DWG | Yes (3D polylines, 3DFACE, meshes) | No | No — drawing units only | Local CAD origins, rotations, and scale factors; "Z" may be a drawing elevation unrelated to any datum |
| KML | Yes (`altitudeMode`: clampToGround, relativeToGround, absolute) | "absolute" means EGM96 orthometric per spec | Fixed: WGS 84 | Altitude mode is per-feature and often left at clampToGround, discarding Z |

> **Definitions that bite.** *What is a GeoJSON altitude?* RFC 7946 §4 says the optional third element "SHALL be the height in meters above or below the WGS 84 reference ellipsoid" — an ellipsoidal height $h$. The 2008 GeoJSON specification it replaced said only "altitude or elevation," with no reference surface, and that is how almost all producers still behave: the Z in a GeoJSON file is whatever came out of the originating dataset — an orthometric $H$ in NAVD88, a depth below MLLW with sign flipped, a barometric altitude, or a lidar $h$ — and nothing in the file says which. The difference between $h$ and $H$ is the geoid undulation $N$, roughly −30 m across much of the conterminous United States and up to ±100 m globally ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)). A consumer who takes the RFC at its word will misplace every orthometric Z by that amount; a consumer who assumes orthometric will misplace every RFC-compliant Z by the same. The only defence is an explicit, out-of-band statement of the vertical datum, and a schema field for it when you control the data.

The shapefile deserves a specific warning because it is still the lingua franca of exchange. Its `.prj` carries a horizontal CRS in ESRI WKT1 and nothing else — no vertical CRS, epoch, or geoid model — so Z-type shapefiles arrive with heights whose datum lives, at best, in a README. The 2 GB limit per component is reached by a few million contour vertices; field names truncate at 10 characters; and the DBF stores numbers as fixed-width text, so an elevation written with two decimals cannot later carry three. Prefer GeoPackage or GeoParquet with a compound CRS; if a shapefile must be delivered, ship a sidecar that states the vertical datum and the meaning of Z.

CAD formats are worse. A DXF from a site survey has coordinates in drawing units on a local grid — State Plane scaled to ground, a site datum with an arbitrary origin, or rotated by an angle recorded only on the title block — and elevations that may be orthometric or relative to a project benchmark assigned 100.000. LandXML carries TIN surfaces with a free-text coordinate-system element that is frequently empty. Ingesting CAD requires a documented transformation — origin, rotation, scale, vertical offset — validated against at least three surveyed points before a single breakline is used.

## 59.2 Breaklines and mass points

Interpolators are smooth by default; terrain is not. A **breakline** is a 3D polyline along which the surface is allowed — required — to change slope abruptly: the top and toe of a cut, a curb, a ridge crest, a stream thalweg, the edge of a water body. When a Delaunay triangulation is constrained to include breakline segments as edges (a **constrained Delaunay triangulation**), no triangle crosses the line, so the surface bends along it rather than across it. **Hard breaklines** enforce a slope discontinuity; **soft breaklines** enforce the Z values along the line but allow smooth slope across it (the edge of a road crown or water body). **Mass points** are irregularly spaced spot elevations with no connectivity — the photogrammetric era's name for what lidar now supplies by the billion — and the classical DTM of the 1980s–2000s was mass points plus breaklines triangulated into a TIN ([Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md)).

Lidar did not abolish breaklines; it changed which ones matter. Dense ground points capture ridges and most slope breaks adequately at 1 m posting, but three classes of feature still require explicit lines. **Hydrographic breaklines** make water flat and streams monotonic: the USGS **Lidar Base Specification (LBS)** requires hydro-flattening of inland ponds and lakes of 2 acres (≈ 0.8 ha) or more, double-line streams of nominal width 100 ft (≈ 30 m) or more, and tidal waters, each delivered as 3D breaklines with the water-surface elevation, plus optional **hydro-enforcement** lines that cut through road embankments at culverts for drainage modelling ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md), [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)). **Engineered edges** — curbs, retaining walls, bridge decks, dam crests — are sharper than the lidar footprint resolves and are smeared by any gridding; a breakline restores them for design-grade DTMs. **Breaklines in occluded ground** — under dense canopy or in steep shadowed gullies — supply shape where there are no returns. Collection cost is the obstacle: hydro breaklines are digitized by analysts on stereo imagery or on lidar intensity and hillshade, at a cost that can be a significant fraction of the acquisition itself, which is why they are specified only above a size threshold.

Where the breakline comes from determines its errors. Breaklines **from stereo imagery** have the photogrammetric vertical accuracy of the imagery (often several times worse than the lidar) and an epoch that may differ from the lidar flight by months or years — a shoreline digitized on leaf-off imagery from the previous spring sits at a different water level than the lidar observed. Breaklines **from the lidar itself** (digitized on intensity, hillshade, or automatically extracted from the point cloud) are coherent with the surface they will constrain but cannot see what the lidar missed. Either way the breakline's Z must be reconciled with the ground points: a stream breakline 0.3 m above the adjacent lidar ground produces a levee; one 0.3 m below produces a trench. The LBS requires water-surface breaklines to be at or below the adjacent ground and streams to be monotonically decreasing downstream, both of which are testable (§59.7).

The effect on the product is large and local. Omitting a ridge breakline flattens the crest across one triangle; omitting a stream breakline lets triangles span the channel and creates a chain of false dams; in a grid the same omissions appear as rounded crests and blocked drainage. Breaklines of the wrong epoch or with bad Z create artefacts sharper and more plausible-looking than the ones they fix — a straight-edged step along a lake shore reads as a real wall. Version breaklines with the DEM they constrained, and record their source, epoch, and vertical reference as carefully as the lidar's.

## 59.3 Contours as data

For most of the twentieth century the contour *was* the elevation dataset. The first national DEMs — the USGS 1:250,000 DEMs of the 1970s–80s, the 30 m DEMs of the 1990s, and their counterparts in most countries — were interpolated from digitized contours, and many survive as "legacy" layers inside composites such as SRTM void fills ([Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)). Understanding contour-derived DEMs is therefore not antiquarian.

Interpolating a surface from contours is a hard problem because the data are dense along lines and absent between them, and naive methods produce a recognisable pathology: **terraces** at each contour elevation, where the interpolator, finding many points at the same height, returns that height for the whole band; and **stair-steps** between terraces. The elevation histogram of such a DEM has spikes at the contour values (10, 20, 30 m…) and troughs between, and slope maps show rings of zero slope separated by rings of high slope that follow the contours exactly — "contour ghosts." **ANUDEM** (Hutchinson 1989; the algorithm behind ArcGIS *Topo to Raster*) was designed to defeat this: a thin-plate spline with a roughness penalty, fitted from coarse to fine grids, with **drainage enforcement** that removes spurious sinks and uses contour curvature to infer streamlines and ridges. ANUDEM DEMs still show residual contour ghosting on gentle slopes, but far less than linear or IDW interpolation, and their drainage connectivity is often better than a raw lidar DTM's.

The accuracy relationship runs both ways. A contour's vertical accuracy is bounded by the DEM or stereo model it came from, and its *horizontal* position carries that vertical error divided by slope ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.3). A DEM interpolated from contours has, between the contours, an error that depends on the interval and the terrain: on uniform slopes the interpolation is nearly exact, while on convex or concave slopes, benches, and small features that fall between contours it can approach half the interval. A rule of thumb for a well-interpolated contour DEM is RMSE ≈ 0.2–0.3 × interval in moderate terrain, which for a 20 m interval is 4–6 m — consistent with the USGS Level 2 DEM standard, which permitted an RMSE of up to one-half the source contour interval. Contours cannot carry anything smaller than the interval, so a contour-derived DEM is also *smoother* than the terrain in a way its RMSE does not express; its effective resolution ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)) is set by the contour spacing, not the grid cell.

The **round trip** DEM → contours → DEM is lossy in a measurable way: tracing discards the elevation everywhere except on the isolines, and re-interpolation recovers the original only where the surface between contours happened to match the interpolator's idea of smooth. Everything smaller than the interval — ditches, terraces, hummocks, small sinkholes — vanishes; ridgelines and thalwegs are rounded; flat areas acquire the interpolator's drift. A simple experiment makes the magnitude concrete.

> **Worked example.** Take a 1 m lidar DTM of rolling farmland (relief 60 m, mean slope 5°, assessed RMSE 0.10 m). Contour it at 2 m, re-interpolate with ANUDEM at 1 m, and difference against the original. Typical results for this terrain type: RMSE of the difference ≈ 0.45 m overall; ≈ 0.25 m on slopes above 5°, ≈ 0.7 m on slopes below 1°; maximum errors of 1.5–2 m at drainage-ditch bottoms and field terraces (features 0.5–1.5 m high that fell between contours). The round-tripped histogram shows weak spikes at even metres, and its slope map has a mean within 0.2° of the original but a standard deviation about 20 % lower — the micro-relief is gone. Vertical accuracy fell from 0.10 to 0.45 m (4.5×), and the information loss is larger than that number suggests. Values are illustrative; run the test on your own data before accepting a contour-derived product as equivalent to a DEM.

Two further points. In several jurisdictions contours are a **legal product** — the contour plan is what a planning authority approves, what a flood ordinance references, or what a cadastral survey records — so the contour layer must be archived as delivered, with its DEM, generation parameters, and smoothing recorded, rather than regenerated on demand. And when you receive historical contours to build a DEM, treat their attributes with suspicion: index contours were often digitized more carefully than intermediates, elevation attributes contain transposition errors (a 1,200 m contour labelled 2,100) that create cliffs, and sheet edges contain mismatches where adjacent maps had different intervals or datums. Checking that every contour's elevation differs from its neighbours' by exactly one interval, and that no contour crosses another, finds most attribute errors before interpolation ([§59.7](#597-topology-and-consistency)).

## 59.4 Shorelines and water polygons

A shoreline is a contour whose elevation is a tidal datum, and its reference is the first thing to establish. Four widely used sources, four different definitions:

- **NOAA CUSP** (Continually Updated Shoreline Product) is the US national shoreline, compiled from NOAA shoreline-mapping surveys, lidar, and imagery; its primary reference is **mean high water (MHW)**, with some segments at other tidal datums as attributed. It is the shoreline on NOAA nautical charts and the one that defines the coast for many legal purposes.
- The **National Hydrography Dataset (NHD)** and **NHDPlus High Resolution** carry inland water bodies and streams as polygons and flowlines at 1:24,000 or better; their water extent is whatever the source imagery or topographic sheet showed on its date — a hydrologic snapshot, not a tidal datum. NHD is being succeeded by the 3D Hydrography Program, which derives hydrography from 3DEP lidar so that water edges and the DEM are at last coherent ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)).
- **OpenStreetMap coastlines** are a volunteer-digitized global line, generally traced from imagery at an unknown tide stage, with no datum attribute; quality is high in populated areas and variable elsewhere.
- **GSHHG** (Global Self-consistent, Hierarchical, High-resolution Geography; Wessel & Smith 1996) is the global shoreline used by GMT and many models, assembled from the World Vector Shoreline and World Data Bank II with a nominal full-resolution scale of about 1:250,000 and no tidal reference; it is a cartographic product, not a survey.

For DEM production the shoreline is a **contract**: it defines where the land model ends, where bathymetry begins, which cells are hydro-flattened, and to what elevation. If the DTM is on NAVD88 and the shoreline is MHW, the flattened cells get the local MHW height in NAVD88 — which varies by a metre or more along a coast and must come from a datum-conversion model (VDatum or equivalent; [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). A shoreline at an unknown tide stage sets the land–water edge at whatever the lidar saw there, which may be a wet beach at low tide. Carry water polygons as vectors until the final product, because the vector preserves the edge at better than cell resolution and carries attributes (datum, water-surface elevation, source, date) that a mask cannot; rasterize under a stated rule for analysis ([§59.8](#598-operations-draping-profiles-3d-length-rasterization)).

Shoreline *change* work has its own trap: comparing two shorelines digitized at different tidal stages or by different rules measures the rule difference, not the coast. Li, Ma & Di (2002) formalized the **tide-coordinated shoreline** — the intersection of a DEM of the beach with a specified tidal datum surface — as the reproducible alternative to image-traced lines, and that is the method to use when a DEM exists: extract the MHW (or MLLW, or whichever) contour from the DEM after transforming it to the tidal datum, and version the result with the DEM and datum model used.

## 59.5 Footprints and obstacles

Building footprints are now available globally: the **Microsoft Building Footprints** (over a billion polygons extracted from imagery by deep learning, released in regional batches since 2018), **Google Open Buildings** (polygons across Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean, with a confidence score per polygon), and **OpenStreetMap** (hand-digitized, with attributes where mappers added them). Microsoft's and Google's releases are 2D polygons, with heights added for subsets (Microsoft's GlobalML release includes model-estimated heights for a subset of its footprints), and OSM carries `height` or `building:levels` tags on a minority of buildings. National cadastres and city models (CityGML LoD1–2) have footprints with surveyed heights for many developed-world cities ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

The question that matters to the DEM practitioner is deceptively simple: **which point of the footprint has the height?** A footprint is a polygon and a height is a scalar; connecting them requires a convention, and at least five are in use: the *roof maximum* (what aviation and line-of-sight analysis want), the *roof median or mean* (what a LoD1 block model uses), the *eave height* (building codes and shadow studies), the *ground elevation at the footprint* — itself ambiguous on a slope, where the DTM under a 30 m building can vary by 3 m — and an *absolute roof elevation* in a stated vertical datum (what a 3D city model stores). A footprint dataset that says "height: 12.4" without saying which of these it is, and relative to what, cannot be combined with a DEM. When you derive heights yourself, compute them as zonal statistics of DSM − DTM inside a footprint eroded by one cell (to avoid edge mixing) and store the statistic's name, the DEM sources, and the datum as attributes.

**Obstacles** are the aviation world's point and line features: the FAA **Digital Obstacle File (DOF)** lists man-made obstacles — towers, buildings, stacks, wind turbines, cranes — with MSL elevation, AGL height, and letter-coded horizontal and vertical accuracy classes (vertical classes from A, ±3 ft, through H, ±1,000 ft, with I meaning unknown), and ICAO Annex 15 eTOD defines equivalent national datasets ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.5, [Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)). The obstacle's position is nominally the structure's centre, its elevation the top. Matching obstacle points to a DSM is a validation exercise in both directions — a DSM can be checked for whether it resolves each listed tower, and the DOF for whether the DSM shows obstacles it does not list — but the matching must allow for the DOF's horizontal accuracy class, which for older entries can be hundreds of metres.

**Wires** are the hardest vector object: a conductor between two towers is a catenary whose lowest point can be 10 m or more below the straight line joining the attachment points, and whose sag changes with temperature and load by metres over a day ([Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)). A 2D polyline with a single height attribute cannot represent it; a 3D polyline sampled every few metres along the catenary, with the sag conditions recorded, can. Utility models (PLS-CADD and similar) store conductors parametrically; the GIS export is a sampled polyline, and the sampling conditions are the attribute that matters.

## 59.6 Ground control points and checkpoints as vectors

A ground control point is the smallest vector dataset in the project and the one most often delivered without a CRS. The minimum schema for a GCP or checkpoint table is:

| Field | Content | Why it is not optional |
|---|---|---|
| `id` | Stable identifier | Links to photos, field notes, and the adjustment report |
| `x`, `y`, `z` | Coordinates in the stated CRS | — |
| `h_crs`, `v_crs` | Horizontal and vertical CRS by EPSG or WKT2; geoid model if orthometric | Without these, Z is a number |
| `epoch` | Decimal year of the coordinates' reference frame epoch | Plate motion and subsidence move points by cm/yr ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)) |
| `sigma_x`, `sigma_y`, `sigma_z` | 1σ uncertainties from the survey adjustment | Needed to weight GCPs and to assess checkpoints honestly |
| `method` | RTK, static, levelled, total station, PPP | Determines plausible σ and correlation |
| `survey_date` | ISO date | The point may have moved or been destroyed |
| `type` | Control (used in adjustment) or check (withheld) | Mixing them invalidates the accuracy assessment |
| `land_cover`, `description` | Open/vegetated class; what the point is | ASPRS NVA/VVA classes; finding it again |
| `photo` | Filename or URL | Resolves "which corner of the manhole" |

Deliver this as a GeoPackage (which can carry the compound CRS) or as CSV *with* a CRS statement in the header or a sidecar; a bare `lat, lon, elev` CSV has no vertical datum, no epoch, and often no indication of whether `lon` is positive west. Record both $h$ and $H$ with the geoid model name if space allows, so the conversion can be checked later.

In SfM photogrammetry ([Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) the GCP is also an *image* measurement: the marked pixel in each photograph where the target centre appears. Marking error of 0.3–1 pixel at a GSD of 2 cm is 0.6–2 cm of positional noise per observation, comparable to the RTK survey's σ; sloppy marking (1–3 pixels) dominates. Target design governs marking precision — high-contrast coded targets at least 5–10 GSD across, flat, visible in at least five images — and any GCP whose image residual exceeds about 1 pixel in the processing report usually means a mis-marked image rather than a bad survey. Checkpoints must be withheld from the bundle adjustment entirely, marked and surveyed by the same standards, and reported separately ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

## 59.7 Topology and consistency

Topology is the set of relationships — adjacency, containment, connectivity — that a geometry dataset is supposed to maintain, and checking it is one of the cheapest DEM quality controls available, because elevation data and the vectors that constrain or derive from them must agree with each other and with physics.

The classical relationship vocabulary is Egenhofer & Franzosa's (1991) **nine-intersection model**, which classifies how two regions relate by the intersections of their interiors, boundaries, and exteriors (disjoint, meet, overlap, covers, contains, equal, and their converses); Simple Features' `ST_Intersects`, `ST_Touches`, `ST_Within`, and `ST_Relate` with a DE-9IM pattern implement it. Validity rules per geometry (`ST_IsValid`) are the first filter; most topology failures in elevation-related vectors are invalid polygons from naive raster-to-vector conversion or editing.

The checks that act as DEM QC, with the test for each:

- **Contours do not cross or touch.** Self-join contour lines with `ST_Crosses` (or `ST_Intersects` excluding shared endpoints) between features of different elevation; any hit is an attribute error or a marching-squares saddle inconsistency. Also test that adjacent contours differ by exactly one interval along any transect.
- **Closed contours enclose consistent elevation.** For each closed ring, sample the DEM inside and outside; the inside must be uniformly higher (hill) or lower (depression, to be hachured). A ring whose inside is mixed indicates a DEM artefact or a bad contour value.
- **Rivers flow downhill.** Sample the DEM along each flowline at regular spacing and flag any rise greater than the DEM's vertical uncertainty ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.10 code). Rises at bridges and culverts are expected in a DSM and are a hydro-enforcement failure in a hydro-conditioned DTM; rises elsewhere are DEM artefacts or misaligned hydrography.
- **Water bodies are flat and at or below their banks.** For each hydro-flattening polygon, the DEM's standard deviation inside should be at the noise level (< 0.05 m for lidar) and the mean should be ≤ the 5th percentile of ground elevation in a 2–5 m buffer outside. Levee-like rims around lakes are the signature of a breakline above the ground.
- **Streams cross contours once, at the apex.** A flowline intersecting a single contour twice within a short distance marks either a flattened reach or a contour that wanders across the channel.
- **Polygons tile without gaps or overlaps** where they are supposed to (depth areas on charts, land-cover, administrative units used for zonal statistics): union the set and compare its area to the sum of parts; the difference is the overlap, and `ST_Difference` against the frame finds gaps.
- **Overlapping footprints** — two buildings sharing area — are usually duplicate extractions and will double-count volume in any DSM-based building statistic.
- **Roads under buildings** and **bridges as lines over polygons** are legitimate in a 2.5D world only if the dataset says so (a `layer` or `bridge` attribute, or a Z); a road centreline crossing a water polygon without a bridge attribute will be draped onto the water surface, and a road passing through a building footprint without a tunnel attribute is a digitizing error or a missing level ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

> **Try it.** Topology checks as DEM QC in PostGIS. Expected result: counts of crossing contours (should be 0), closed contours with mixed interior (a handful on a noisy DEM — inspect them), and flowline reaches rising more than 0.3 m (a few per cent on a raw DSM; near zero on a hydro-enforced DTM).
>
> ```sql
> -- 1. Contours of different elevation that cross
> SELECT count(*) AS crossings
> FROM contours a JOIN contours b
>   ON a.id < b.id AND a.elev <> b.elev
>  AND ST_Crosses(a.geom, b.geom);
>
> -- 2. Closed contours: inside vs outside DEM elevation (raster table dem)
> WITH rings AS (
>   SELECT id, elev, ST_MakePolygon(geom) AS poly
>   FROM contours WHERE ST_IsClosed(geom) AND ST_NPoints(geom) > 3)
> SELECT r.id, r.elev,
>        (ST_SummaryStatsAgg(ST_Clip(d.rast, r.poly), 1, true)).mean AS z_in,
>        (ST_SummaryStatsAgg(ST_Clip(d.rast,
>            ST_Difference(ST_Buffer(r.poly, 5), r.poly)), 1, true)).mean AS z_out
> FROM rings r JOIN dem d ON ST_Intersects(d.rast, ST_Buffer(r.poly, 5))
> GROUP BY r.id, r.elev, r.poly
> HAVING sign((ST_SummaryStatsAgg(ST_Clip(d.rast, r.poly), 1, true)).mean - r.elev)
>      = sign((ST_SummaryStatsAgg(ST_Clip(d.rast,
>            ST_Difference(ST_Buffer(r.poly, 5), r.poly)), 1, true)).mean - r.elev);
>
> -- 3. Flowlines that climb: sample every 10 m, compare to running minimum
> WITH pts AS (
>   SELECT f.id, s.i,
>          ST_Value(d.rast, ST_LineInterpolatePoint(f.geom, s.i / ST_Length(f.geom))) AS z
>   FROM flowlines f
>   CROSS JOIN LATERAL generate_series(0, floor(ST_Length(f.geom))::int, 10) AS s(i)
>   JOIN dem d ON ST_Intersects(d.rast, ST_LineInterpolatePoint(f.geom, s.i / ST_Length(f.geom))))
> SELECT id, max(z - running_min) AS max_rise
> FROM (SELECT id, i, z, min(z) OVER (PARTITION BY id ORDER BY i) AS running_min FROM pts) q
> GROUP BY id HAVING max(z - running_min) > 0.3
> ORDER BY max_rise DESC;
> ```

## 59.8 Operations: draping, profiles, 3D length, rasterization

**Draping** assigns elevations from a DEM to a 2D vector — a road, a trail, a pipeline, a boundary — by sampling the raster at the vertices and, if done properly, at every raster-cell crossing between them. Sampling at vertices alone gives a profile whose resolution is the vertex spacing — hundreds of metres for a simplified road centreline — so a 3 m ditch or a 10 m embankment between vertices vanishes. Densifying the line to the DEM cell size and sampling bilinearly honours the DEM's resolution and avoids the staircase that nearest-neighbour produces along diagonal lines. And **drape onto the right surface**: a road draped on a DSM climbs every tree crown and bridge; draped on a DTM it dives under every bridge into the river ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md)). The honest profile of a road requires a surface in which the road itself is modelled — a DTM with bridges retained (the LBS "bridge deck" class handling varies by specification) or a dedicated road-surface model.

**Profiles** are a one-dimensional drape and the most powerful QC tool in this book ([Chapter 57](ch57-visualizing-dems.md) §57.7): a transect across a lake shore, a road embankment, or a tile boundary shows levees, steps, and seams that no plan view reveals. Extract them with densified bilinear sampling and plot with the vertical exaggeration declared.

**3D length and surface area.** The length of a draped line is its 3D length $L_{3D} = \sum_i \sqrt{\Delta x_i^2 + \Delta y_i^2 + \Delta z_i^2}$, which exceeds the planimetric length by a factor of $\sec\beta$ on a uniform slope $\beta$ — 1.5 % at 10°, 15 % at 30°, and much more on a trail with constant small undulations, where the result depends strongly on the sampling interval (a fractal-like effect: finer sampling adds length until the DEM's noise dominates). State the DEM and sampling interval with any 3D length. **Surface area** is the analogous quantity for polygons: Jenness (2004) sums the 3D areas of eight triangles connecting each cell centre to its neighbours, which for a planar slope $\beta$ gives the planimetric area times $\sec\beta$ and for rough terrain a value that increases with resolution and noise. It is useful as a relative roughness or habitat index and a poor absolute quantity, because it has no resolution-independent limit on real terrain.

**Slope-aware buffering.** A buffer of 100 m along the ground on a 30° slope is only 87 m in plan; a planimetric buffer therefore under-represents ground distance on slopes. Where this matters (riparian setbacks measured along the ground, line-of-sight corridors), buffer in 3D by iterating a cost-distance over the slope-corrected cell size, or buffer in plan with a width divided by $\cos\beta$ of the local slope.

**Rasterization** of vectors — a water polygon to a mask, footprints to a building raster, a parcel to a zone — requires a rule for the cells along the boundary, and the rules give different answers:

| Rule | A cell is "in" if… | Area bias | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell centre (default in GDAL `gdal_rasterize`, rasterio) | its centre lies inside the polygon | Unbiased in expectation; high variance for small or thin features; thin features can vanish entirely | Masks, zonal statistics on large zones |
| All-touched (`-at`, `all_touched=True`) | any part of it intersects the polygon | Overestimates area by about one cell width along the perimeter — up to tens of percent for small features | Making sure thin features (streams, roads) are represented; conservative masks |
| Area-weighted (fractional coverage; `exactextract`, `rasterstats` with coverage) | fraction of its area inside, stored as a weight | Unbiased; exact for area sums | Zonal statistics of DEM values, accurate area and volume |

> **Worked example.** A square building footprint 11 m on a side (area 121 m²) rasterized onto a 2 m grid whose lines do not align with the walls. Cell-centre rule: 5 or 6 cell centres fall inside along each axis, so 25–36 cells, i.e. 100–144 m² — a swing of −17 % to +19 % for one building, though unbiased over many. All-touched: 6 or 7 cells per axis, 36–49 cells (144–196 m²), a systematic 19–62 % overestimate. Area-weighted: exactly 121 m² as the sum of fractions. For a zonal mean of building height from a DSM, the cell-centre rule is acceptable when footprints are large relative to cells; for building *volume* (height × area), only the area-weighted rule gives a result that does not depend on grid alignment.

**Raster-to-vector** conversion (`gdal_polygonize`, `r.to.vect`) creates polygons with cell-edge staircases whose perimeter is inflated by up to $4/\pi \approx 1.27$ relative to a smooth boundary, with slivers along diagonal edges, and with the DEM's noise expressed as thousands of one-cell polygons wherever a threshold is crossed; smooth with a tolerance below the cell size and remove polygons smaller than a few cells, and record both choices.

## 59.9 Generalization

Line simplification removes vertices and, in doing so, moves the line. **Douglas–Peucker** (1973) bounds that movement: every original vertex lies within the tolerance $\epsilon$ of the simplified line, so the positional error introduced is at most $\epsilon$ (though the algorithm does not preserve topology — two simplified lines that did not cross may now cross, and a ring may self-intersect). **Visvalingam–Whyatt** (1993) removes vertices in order of the area of the triangle they form with their neighbours, producing more natural shapes at aggressive levels but without a simple distance bound; the effective-area threshold maps approximately to a distance tolerance of $\sqrt{2A/\ell}$ for segment length $\ell$. **Topology-preserving** simplification (GEOS `TopologyPreservingSimplifier`, PostGIS `ST_SimplifyPreserveTopology`, GRASS `v.generalize` with the topology option, mapshaper's shared-boundary handling) prevents self-intersections and, in mapshaper's case, keeps adjacent polygons' shared edges coincident so that generalization does not open gaps between parcels or depth areas.

Generalization changes derived quantities predictably: simplifying a line shortens it (every removed vertex replaces two segments by a chord), typically by 1–5 % at a cell-size tolerance and more on sinuous lines; simplifying a polygon changes its area by up to the tolerance times the perimeter. For elevation work, two consequences follow. A simplified contour has a positional error of up to $\epsilon$ added to its DEM-inherited error, so keep $\epsilon$ below the DEM cell size ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.3). And a simplified breakline or shoreline, re-used to constrain a DEM, moves the constraint: a stream breakline simplified by 2 m before TIN construction will put the channel 2 m from where the lidar saw it, which in a 1 m DTM is a visible artefact. Generalize for *display*; constrain and compute with the full-resolution geometry; and store the tolerance as an attribute of any simplified layer so that nobody downstream mistakes it for a survey.

Isobaths add the shoal-bias constraint: the generalized line may move only seaward ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.4). No general simplification algorithm enforces this, so chart-production systems post-process by testing each simplified segment against the source line and pushing it offshore of any inshore crossing, or generalize the *grid* shoal-biased (taking the minimum depth in each coarser cell) and contour the result.

## 59.10 Vector tiles and 3D vector formats

Web delivery of vectors is by **vector tiles** (Mapbox Vector Tile specification; tiles produced by tippecanoe, PostGIS `ST_AsMVT`, or Martin/Tegola servers), which encode geometry as integer coordinates within a tile's extent — typically 4,096 units across — in Web Mercator at each zoom level. Two losses are built in. Coordinates are quantized: at zoom 14 a tile is about 2.4 km across at the equator, so one unit is ≈ 0.6 m; at zoom 10 it is ≈ 9.5 m, so a shoreline delivered at zoom 10 has had nearly 10 m of positional error added. And the specification has no Z: elevation survives only as an attribute (`ele`, `height`), with no vertical CRS field in the tile or in TileJSON, so contour layers in vector-tile basemaps carry heights whose datum is stated nowhere the user can see ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md) §58.9).

Three-dimensional vector content goes to the web through **3D Tiles** (OGC Community Standard, 1.0 in 2019, 1.1 in 2023), whose tiles contain **glTF 2.0** meshes with instancing and metadata; the "vector data" tile format proposed for 3D Tiles was never finalized, so vector features are delivered as meshes (extruded footprints, draped lines) or as separate 2D layers. 3D Tiles places tiles in an Earth-centred Cartesian frame (EPSG:4978) with a per-tileset transform, which means heights are ellipsoidal by construction — a CityGML model in orthometric heights that is converted without applying the geoid will float or sink by $N$. **CityJSON** (2.0, 2023; the JSON encoding of CityGML 3.0) records a CRS by EPSG code and can carry a compound CRS with a vertical component — one of the few web-oriented 3D formats that can state what its Z means. **I3S** (Esri's Indexed 3D Scene Layers, also an OGC Community Standard) and bare **glTF** have no geodetic CRS; georeferencing lives in the wrapping service.

The practical rule is the same as for GeoJSON and shapefiles: **vertical CRS is lost in web delivery unless you put it somewhere a human and a program will find it.** Carry it as an attribute on every feature or in a documented metadata object; transform to ellipsoidal heights explicitly when the target frame (3D Tiles, Cesium, any globe) is ellipsoidal; and test the result by placing a feature of known height on the terrain and measuring where it lands.

## Then & now

The first GIS vector models were topological by necessity. **ARC/INFO** (Esri, 1982) ⟨H⟩ stored geometry as a *coverage*: arcs with from- and to-nodes, polygons defined by the arcs that bound them, left and right polygon identifiers on every arc. The structure guaranteed shared edges and no slivers, and let a contour plate be checked for crossings by a table query — at the cost of a build/clean step after every edit and a data model few other programs could read. Elevation lived in attributes and in separate TIN and lattice models; Z on coordinates was not part of the picture.

The **shapefile** (Esri technical description, 1998) ⟨H⟩ threw topology away for simplicity: each feature carries its own geometry, polygons may overlap freely, and shared boundaries are stored twice. It gained Z and M types and universal readability and became the exchange format of the field for 25 years; it never gained a vertical CRS, a 64-bit size limit, or long field names. The **OGC Simple Features** specification (1999; ISO 19125 in 2004) standardized the shapefile's geometry philosophy — simple, non-topological, 2.5D — as WKT/WKB and as the SQL functions that PostGIS, Oracle Spatial, and SQL Server implement, and defined the predicates (DE-9IM) that let topology be *checked* even when it is not *stored*.

**GeoJSON** emerged from the web (community specification 2008; IETF RFC 7946 in 2016) and made two decisions that simplified web mapping and complicated geodesy: one CRS only (WGS 84 lon/lat), and an optional altitude whose reference the RFC fixed to the ellipsoid and almost nobody honours. **GeoPackage** (OGC, 2014) put Simple Features in SQLite with a proper spatial-reference table that *can* hold a compound CRS; **GeoParquet** (OGC, 1.0 in 2023 after 0.x drafts from 2022) did the same for columnar cloud storage with PROJJSON CRS metadata; **FlatGeobuf** (2018–) added streaming and a spatial index. Each successive format could carry the vertical reference, and each is routinely written without it. Across four decades the pattern held: Z was added to the coordinate tuple early and to the *metadata* late, and the gap between the two is where elevation semantics have been lost.

The present direction is toward **topology as validation rather than storage** (GEOS/JTS validity and relate predicates, PostGIS topology extension, mapshaper's shared-edge model) and toward **3D as a separate family** (CityGML/CityJSON solids, 3D Tiles meshes) rather than as Z on 2D geometry. For DEM work this is healthy — the checks of §59.7 are cheap to run on any format — provided the vertical reference travels with the data.

## Mathematics

**Point-in-polygon.** The ray-casting rule counts crossings of a horizontal ray from the point with polygon edges; odd means inside. For edge from $(x_i, y_i)$ to $(x_j, y_j)$ and point $(x, y)$, the edge is crossed if $(y_i > y) \ne (y_j > y)$ and $x < x_i + (y - y_i)(x_j - x_i)/(y_j - y_i)$. Points exactly on an edge are a convention (GEOS treats the boundary as not interior for `ST_Contains`, interior for `ST_Covers`); for rasterization by cell centre this convention decides which of two adjacent polygons owns a cell whose centre lies on their shared edge, and must be applied consistently or cells will be double-counted or dropped.

**Simplification tolerance and positional error.** Douglas–Peucker with tolerance $\epsilon$ guarantees $d_H(L, L') \le \epsilon$ in the Hausdorff metric from the original to the simplified line. If the original line already has positional uncertainty $\sigma_p$ (for a contour, $\sigma_p \approx \sigma_z/\tan\beta$), the simplified line's uncertainty is bounded by $\sigma_p + \epsilon$ in the worst case and approximately $\sqrt{\sigma_p^2 + \epsilon^2/3}$ in RMS if the simplification displacement is treated as uniform on $[-\epsilon, \epsilon]$. Goodchild & Hunter (1997) measure the positional accuracy of a line against a reference line by the fraction of its length within a buffer of width $d$ of the reference, as a function of $d$ — the right statistic for comparing two shorelines or two contour sets, because it does not require vertex correspondence.

**Constrained Delaunay triangulation.** Given points $P$ and segments $S$ (breaklines), the CDT is the triangulation containing every segment of $S$ as an edge and otherwise as close to Delaunay as possible: each triangle's circumcircle contains no point of $P$ *visible* from the triangle's interior (visibility blocked by segments). Inserting a segment that crosses existing edges removes those triangles and re-triangulates the two resulting polygons. Breakline Z values are interpolated linearly along each segment where it is split by intersections; where a breakline's Z disagrees with a nearby point by more than the data's noise, the CDT reproduces the disagreement as a near-vertical facet — the levee or trench of §59.2.

**3D length and surface area.** For a draped line sampled at points $(x_i, y_i, z_i)$,
$$L_{3D} = \sum_{i} \sqrt{(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2 + (y_{i+1}-y_i)^2 + (z_{i+1}-z_i)^2},$$
and on a uniform slope $\beta$, $L_{3D} = L_{2D}\sec\beta$. Jenness's surface-area estimate for a cell of side $\Delta$ centred at $c$ with neighbours $n_k$ ($k = 1\ldots8$, in cyclic order) sums eight triangles $(c, n_k, n_{k+1})$ each with half-length edges: $A_{cell} = \sum_k \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_k \times \mathbf{u}_{k+1}\|$ where $\mathbf{u}_k$ is the 3D vector from $c$ to the midpoint of the segment to $n_k$. For a plane of slope $\beta$ this yields $\Delta^2 \sec\beta$ exactly; for noisy data with per-cell vertical noise $\sigma_z$ it adds a positive bias of order $(\sigma_z/\Delta)^2$ relative to the true area, which for $\sigma_z = 0.1$ m at $\Delta = 1$ m is about 1 % and at $\Delta = 0.25$ m about 16 % — the resolution dependence noted in §59.8.

**Rasterization sampling bias.** For a polygon of area $A$ and perimeter $p$ rasterized at cell size $\Delta$, the expected count under the cell-centre rule is $A/\Delta^2$ (unbiased) with a standard deviation of order $\sqrt{p/\Delta}$ cells — the boundary cells are each a coin flip — so the relative error scales as $\Delta\sqrt{p/\Delta}/A \propto \sqrt{\Delta p}/A$. The all-touched rule counts approximately $(A + p\,\Delta\,c)/\Delta^2$ cells with $c \approx 0.5$–$1$ depending on edge orientation, a systematic overestimate of $c\,p\,\Delta/A$: for the 11 m building on a 2 m grid, $p\Delta/A = 44 \times 2/121 \approx 0.73$, which brackets the 19–62 % range of the worked example.

## Validation & uncertainty

Vector geometry contributes to a DEM's error budget in four ways, and the vector products derived from the DEM inherit its errors plus their own. Each is testable.

**Errors that vectors introduce into the DEM.** A breakline with the wrong Z is the dominant one: a hydro-flattening polygon 0.3 m above the lidar ground produces a levee of exactly that height along its entire perimeter — a coherent systematic error no checkpoint statistic will catch, because checkpoints are not placed on lake shores. Test every breakline's Z against the DEM it will constrain *before* constraining: the water-surface elevation should be at or below the 5th-percentile ground elevation within a 2–5 m outside buffer, and stream breaklines should be monotonic. A breakline from a different epoch introduces a time error of the feature's own change rate — a river bank migrating 2 m/yr, a reservoir drawn down 5 m — which the metadata must expose. A shoreline at an unknown tide stage mis-sets the land–water boundary by the tide range times the beach slope: on a 1:50 beach with a 2 m range, 100 m horizontally.

**Errors in vectors derived from the DEM.** Contours carry $\sigma_z/\tan\beta$ plus generalization ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md)); footprint heights carry the DSM's and DTM's errors plus the choice of statistic (roof maximum versus median can differ by the roof pitch, 2–4 m on a gabled house); draped profiles carry the DEM's error plus the sampling method's (nearest-neighbour at sparse vertices can miss 100 % of a feature narrower than the vertex spacing); 3D lengths and surface areas carry a resolution-dependent positive bias from noise.

**Errors in control and checkpoints.** Mis-specified CRS (a NAD83(2011) point labelled WGS 84 is off by ~1–2 m in the conterminous US plus plate motion since epoch — [Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)), mis-specified vertical datum ($N$, tens of metres, caught immediately; GEOID12B versus GEOID18, a few centimetres, not caught), lon/lat swaps, and sign conventions. Plot every GCP table against a basemap and the DEM before use: a point 30 m off the surface is a vertical-datum error; a cluster offset by a constant is a horizontal-datum or epoch error.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Height of a building from footprints and a lidar DSM/DTM, per building, 1σ.
>
> | Component | Source | Magnitude |
> |---|---|---|
> | DSM roof elevation | lidar σ_z on hard roof | 0.05–0.10 m |
> | DTM ground elevation | lidar σ_z on open ground | 0.05–0.10 m |
> | Ground reference ambiguity | which ground: footprint mean vs lowest vs street side; slope 3° × 20 m building | up to 1 m (systematic per site) |
> | Roof statistic choice | max vs median vs mean on pitched roof | 1–3 m (definitional, not random) |
> | Footprint positional error | 1–3 m offset puts edge cells on the ground or neighbours | 0.2–1 m after 1-cell erosion |
> | Footprint–lidar epoch mismatch | building altered/demolished between | gross errors in a few percent of buildings |
> | Rasterization rule | cell-centre vs area-weighted on 11 m building, 2 m grid | 0 m on height, roughly ±20 % on area/volume |
>
> The measurement noise (first two rows) is centimetres; the budget is dominated by definitions and matching, which is why "height" in a footprint file is meaningless without the convention stated.

**Tests to run, and what to report.** (1) Validity: `ST_IsValid` on every vector layer; fix before anything else. (2) Topology as DEM QC: the §59.7 checks, with counts reported as pass/fail against stated thresholds (zero crossing contours; climbing flowlines below x %; lake rims below the lidar noise). (3) Breakline–DEM agreement: histogram of breakline Z minus DEM Z at breakline vertices, reported as mean and 95th percentile; the mean should be within the lidar's σ_z and the sign consistent with the breakline type. (4) Round-trip tests for any contour-derived input: contour → DEM → contour, compare with the Goodchild–Hunter buffer statistic at 0.5 and 1.0 cell widths. (5) Control-point audit: plot, check against DEM, confirm CRS and epoch with the surveyor, and report the GCP/checkpoint split and the σ values used. (6) Rasterization: state the rule and cell size for every mask, and for area or volume statistics use area-weighted coverage. Report the vertical CRS of every vector layer in the deliverable, by name, even when the format cannot hold it.

## Pitfalls

- **GeoJSON Z values assumed orthometric (or assumed ellipsoidal)** → the RFC says ellipsoid, producers write whatever they had → never trust a GeoJSON Z without an out-of-band datum statement; difference a few features against a DEM of known datum and look for a constant ≈ N.
- **Shapefile with no vertical datum** → the format cannot hold one → ship a sidecar or switch to GeoPackage with a compound CRS; on receipt, demand the datum in writing.
- **Breaklines from a different epoch than the lidar** → stereo-digitized hydro lines reused across campaigns → check breakline Z against the new DEM before constraining; expect reservoirs, rivers, and coasts to have moved.
- **Water breakline above the bank** → water-surface elevation taken from a gauge, a different datum, or a high-water image → the lake acquires a rim; test with the outside-buffer percentile rule.
- **Contour intervals finer than the data** → the software draws them → interval ≥ 3 × RMSE; see [Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md).
- **Draping a 2D road onto a DSM and reporting a bridge as a hill** (or onto a DTM and reporting it as a dip) → the surface does not model the road → use a surface that does, or flag bridge/tunnel attributes and interpolate across them.
- **Rasterization rule changing a footprint's area by 20 % or more** → all-touched used for small features → use area-weighted coverage for area and volume; state the rule in metadata.
- **Historical contour attribute errors** (transposed digits, wrong interval at sheet edges) → digitizing era → check that neighbouring contours differ by exactly one interval and that no contours cross before interpolating.
- **Round-tripping DEM → contours → DEM and treating the result as equivalent** → contours look authoritative → measure the loss (RMSE several times the original, micro-relief gone) and keep the original grid.
- **Mixing control and checkpoints** → convenience → separate tables or a `type` field honoured by the processing; report them separately.
- **GCP CSV without CRS or epoch** → field software exports lat/lon/elev → require the schema of §59.6; plot every table against the DEM before use.
- **Simplified geometry used as a constraint** → the display copy was handed to the TIN tool → keep full-resolution geometry for computation; store the tolerance as an attribute of simplified layers.
- **Footprint height with no convention** → "height" means five different things → store the statistic name, surfaces, and datum as attributes; erode footprints one cell before zonal statistics.
- **3D Tiles or globe content sinking or floating by tens of metres** → orthometric heights placed in an ellipsoidal frame → apply the geoid explicitly; test with a known-height feature.

## Key takeaways

- Vectors carry elevation semantics too: define what Z is (h or H, which datum and geoid model, which epoch) in a field or sidecar, because no common format does it for you — and the one that tries (RFC 7946) is honoured by almost no one.
- Simple Features are 2.5D: predicates and operations are planar, so bridges, overhangs, and vertical faces need explicit attributes or a true 3D model.
- Breaklines and shorelines shape the DEM; version them with it, record source and epoch, and test their Z against the ground before constraining.
- Contours are a lossy encoding of a surface; a contour-derived DEM has RMSE of roughly 0.2–0.3 × interval and no information finer than the interval, and the DEM → contour → DEM round trip is measurably destructive.
- Topology checks — crossing contours, climbing rivers, rimmed lakes, overlapping footprints — are DEM checks; run them as SQL and report counts.
- Draping, 3D length, and surface area depend on sampling method and resolution; state both, and expect noise to bias lengths and areas upward.
- Rasterization needs a stated rule; use area-weighted coverage whenever area or volume is the answer.
- Generalize for display, compute with full resolution, and move isobaths only seaward.
- Web delivery (vector tiles, 3D Tiles, glTF) drops the vertical CRS; put it back as attributes and transform to the target frame deliberately.
