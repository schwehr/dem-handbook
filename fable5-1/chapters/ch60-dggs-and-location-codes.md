# Chapter 60 — Discrete global grids and location codes: S2, H3, geohash, plus codes, what3words, MGRS

> **Part XIII — Vectors, grids, and location codes.** Having treated vector geometry, the book turns to the other way of naming places — by cell identifiers and short codes — and to what happens when elevation data are aggregated, indexed, or communicated on them.

**In this chapter.** A discrete global grid system (DGGS) partitions the Earth into cells at a hierarchy of resolutions and gives each cell a stable identifier; a location code does something similar for humans, trading precision for pronounceability. Both are indexes, not measurements, and both carry geometric quirks that matter as soon as elevation is aggregated onto them or differenced across them. This chapter explains why DGGS exist and what OGC Topic 21 requires of one; the geometry of S2 (cube faces, Hilbert curves, exact hierarchy) and H3 (icosahedron, aperture-7 hexagons, pentagons, approximate hierarchy); the other grids you will meet — geohash, quadkeys and XYZ tiles, rHEALPix, ISEA, GeoSOT, A5; the human codes — MGRS/USNG, plus codes, what3words, Maidenhead; how to put elevation statistics, slope, and hydrology on hexagons without inventing precision; the losses in converting raster ↔ DGGS ↔ raster; the ambiguities, datum assumptions, and ownership problems of coding schemes; and how to choose among them for indexing, analysis, communication, and distribution. A Python Try-it compares H3 and S2 aggregation of a DEM and demonstrates H3's parent–child leakage numerically.

## 60.1 Why DGGS

Latitude–longitude is a coordinate system, not a partition: "the cell containing this point at resolution r" requires a grid, and the default grids — degree squares, UTM tiles, Web Mercator tiles — have cells whose area and shape vary with latitude and whose hierarchies (if any) were never designed for analysis. A **discrete global grid system (DGGS)** is a deliberate alternative: a family of partitions of the globe, one per resolution, in which every cell has a unique identifier, cells at each resolution cover the Earth without gaps or overlaps, and finer resolutions refine coarser ones in a defined way. The motivations are four.

**Consistent-area analysis.** Per-cell counts, means, and sums are comparable across cells only if the cells have the same area or the variation is known and small. Equal-area DGGS (ISEA, rHEALPix, HEALPix) achieve equality exactly; S2 and H3 approximately (§§60.2–60.3); Web Mercator tiles not at all (a zoom-10 tile at 60° latitude has one quarter the ground area of one at the equator).

**Hierarchical aggregation.** If a fine cell lies in exactly one parent at every coarser resolution, statistics computed once at the finest level can be rolled up without re-reading the data. S2 and quadtree grids have this property exactly; H3 and other hexagon grids only approximately, which is the single most important thing to know about H3 (§60.3).

**Indexing and joins at scale.** A 64-bit cell identifier is a sortable integer, so data indexed by cell ID can be joined, filtered, and partitioned with ordinary database machinery instead of geometric predicates. This is why S2 underlies Google's spatial storage, H3 Uber's, and both are extensions in DuckDB, BigQuery, Snowflake, and PostgreSQL; for point clouds and DEM tiles the cell ID is a natural partition key ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md), [Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md)).

**Tiling for cloud-native data.** A DGGS cell can be the unit of storage and request, with the hierarchy giving overviews for free. In practice most cloud-native rasters still tile in their native projection and the DGGS serves as an *index over* those tiles rather than as their geometry (§60.9).

The **OGC Abstract Specification Topic 21** (Purss et al., 2017; version 2.0, 2021) states what a DGGS must have to be called one: a reference frame (a polyhedron or other base and a projection to the sphere/ellipsoid), a cell structure with defined refinement, global coverage at every resolution, unique cell identifiers (with a defined addressing scheme), a spatial reference for each cell (its centroid and boundary), and defined operations for quantization (assigning data to cells), algebra (combining cells), and interoperability (converting to and from conventional coordinates). Topic 21 is agnostic about equal-area versus equal-shape and about the polyhedron, so a conforming DGGS can still be a poor choice for a given analysis; it tells you what to look for, not which to pick. The companion **OGC API – DGGS** (draft) defines web endpoints for listing a server's DGGS, querying cells, and retrieving data per zone.

> **Definitions that bite.** *Resolution* in DGGS literature means the level of the hierarchy (H3 resolution 9, S2 level 14), not a cell size in metres; two systems' resolution numbers are unrelated (H3 resolution 9 cells average 0.105 km², S2 level 14 cells average about 0.32 km²), and neither corresponds to a DEM's posting. *Aperture* is the ratio of parent cell area to child cell area — 4 for quadtrees and S2, 7 for H3, 3 or 4 for ISEA variants — and determines how quickly cells shrink per level and whether children can nest inside parents (they can for aperture 4 on a square grid; they cannot for any hexagon aperture). *Equal area* means every cell at a resolution has the same area on the reference ellipsoid or sphere; *equal shape* means cells have the same shape, which no global grid of more than a few cells can achieve exactly, since a sphere cannot be tiled by congruent regular polygons beyond the Platonic solids.

## 60.2 S2

**S2** (Google; developed internally from the late 2000s, open-sourced as `s2geometry` in 2017 ⟨H⟩) projects the sphere onto the six faces of a cube and subdivides each face as a quadtree. Three ideas make it useful. First, the face projection is not the raw gnomonic projection, whose cells vary in area by a factor of 5.2 across a face, but a **quadratic transform** of the gnomonic coordinates that reduces the variation to about **2.08** between the largest and smallest cells at a level (a tangent transform does better but slower). Second, each face's quadtree is traversed by a **Hilbert curve** ⟨H⟩, a space-filling curve in which consecutive cells are always adjacent, so that a one-dimensional range of cell IDs corresponds to a compact region on the sphere and spatial queries become integer range scans. Third, the **cell ID** is a 64-bit integer: 3 bits for the face, 2 bits per level for the quadtree position along the Hilbert curve (up to 30 levels), and a trailing 1 bit marking the level, so that the ID of any cell sorts between the IDs of its children and a parent is obtained by masking.

Levels run from 0 (one cell per face, about 85 million km²) to 30 (about 0.74 cm² on average; range roughly 0.48–0.93 cm²). Useful intermediate scales: level 10 cells average about 81 km² (≈ 9 km across), level 14 about 0.32 km² (≈ 560 m), level 20 about 77 m² (≈ 9 m), level 24 about 0.3 m² (≈ 55 cm). Because the subdivision is a true quadtree, the **hierarchy is exact**: every level-$k$ cell is the union of exactly four level-$(k+1)$ cells and is contained in exactly one level-$(k-1)$ cell. Statistics can be rolled up without leakage, a point of contrast with H3. Cells are spherical quadrilaterals bounded by great-circle arcs (S2 computes on the unit sphere, not the ellipsoid; the mapping from WGS 84 latitude is direct, so the "sphere" is only a computational device, and areas reported by the library are spherical areas scaled by a nominal Earth radius, which differ from ellipsoidal areas by a few tenths of a percent depending on latitude).

S2's other workhorse is the **covering**: an approximation of an arbitrary region (polygon, cap, rectangle) by a small set of cells, possibly at mixed levels, that contains the region (an exterior covering) or lies inside it (an interior covering). Coverings turn geometric predicates into ID range queries — "which point clouds intersect this watershed" becomes "which stored cell IDs fall in these ranges" — with a controllable trade-off between cell count and boundary slop. For elevation data the pattern is to give each lidar tile or DEM chunk a covering at a level near its extent (level 12–16 for 1 km tiles) and catalogue the IDs; the point cloud itself can be sorted by level-20 to level-24 cell ID so that spatial reads are contiguous ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md) describes COPC's octree alternative). S2's weaknesses are the cube edges and corners, where cells are most distorted and neighbour computation crosses faces, and the quadrilateral cell itself: four edge-neighbours and four corner-neighbours at different distances complicate isotropic analyses such as flow routing.

## 60.3 H3

**H3** (Uber; open-sourced 2018; Brodsky 2018) starts from an icosahedron oriented so that its twelve vertices fall in the ocean (a specific orientation chosen to keep the vertex distortions away from land), projects each triangular face gnomonically, and tiles the result with hexagons. Hexagons are attractive for analysis because every neighbour shares an edge at the same centre-to-centre distance — no corner neighbours — so neighbourhood operations are isotropic in a way square grids cannot be. The cost is twofold, and both costs are structural.

First, **a sphere cannot be tiled with hexagons alone**: Euler's formula requires exactly twelve pentagons in any such tiling, and H3 has twelve pentagons at every resolution, at the icosahedron vertices. Pentagons have five neighbours, roughly half the area of a hexagon, and distorted geometry; H3 places them in the ocean, but ocean-mapping and global analyses meet them, and code that assumes six neighbours or uniform area must special-case them.

Second, hexagons do not nest. H3 uses **aperture 7**: each resolution's cells are about one seventh the area of the previous resolution's, and the finer grid is rotated by about 19.1° relative to the coarser (alternating direction each level, the "Class II/Class III" alternation). A child hexagon is assigned to the parent whose centre is nearest, and seven children are associated with each parent — the central one entirely inside the parent and six around it, each straddling the parent's boundary. The union of the seven children is a flower-shaped region that approximates the parent but is not equal to it. In the planar idealization (regular hexagons, aperture 7, 19.1° rotation) the geometry is exact: each of the six peripheral children has **1/12 of its area outside the parent**, so the flower spills **1/14 ≈ 7.1 %** of the parent's area over its boundary, and the same 7.1 % of the parent is covered by children that belong to neighbouring parents; on the sphere the figures vary slightly with local distortion. The H3 documentation calls this "approximate containment." The practical consequence is that **summing children does not reproduce the parent**: a sum aggregated at resolution 10 and rolled up by `cell_to_parent` to resolution 9 is a sum over the flower, not the hexagon, and any analysis that compares a parent's own statistic with the sum of its children incurs boundary errors of up to about 7 % of the parent's area — concentrated wherever the summed quantity varies sharply across the boundary. H3's answer is to compute at the resolution you report and treat `cell_to_parent` as an index operation rather than a geometric one.

H3 has sixteen resolutions, 0–15. Resolution 0 has 122 cells (110 hexagons and 12 pentagons) averaging 4.36 million km²; resolution 15 cells average 0.895 m² (about 1 m across). Intermediate scales: resolution 7 averages 5.16 km² (edge ≈ 1.2 km), resolution 9 averages 0.105 km² (edge ≈ 174 m), resolution 11 averages 2,150 m² (edge ≈ 25 m), resolution 13 averages 43.9 m² (edge ≈ 3.6 m). Cell area is **not uniform**: the gnomonic face projection enlarges cells toward face edges and vertices, so that at a given resolution the largest hexagons exceed the smallest by a factor of up to roughly 2 (the H3 documentation tabulates minimum and maximum areas per resolution, and Kmoch et al. 2022 map the distortion); the variation is smooth, so cells within a few hundred kilometres of each other are nearly equal. The 64-bit index encodes the resolution, the base cell (0–121), and a 3-bit digit per resolution, with unused digits set to 7; it does not use a space-filling curve, so sorted H3 indexes are only loosely spatially coherent.

H3's strengths for elevation work are the isotropic neighbourhood (slope, aspect, and flow computed on hexagons have no diagonal-versus-orthogonal asymmetry; §60.6), the fine resolutions reaching 1 m, and the breadth of bindings (C, Python, Java, JavaScript, R, DuckDB, PostgreSQL, BigQuery). Its weaknesses are the approximate hierarchy, the non-equal area, the pentagons, and the fact that polygon-to-cells ("polyfill") uses a cell-centre containment rule by default — a polygon is represented by the cells whose centres fall inside it — which has the same boundary bias as cell-centre rasterization ([Chapter 59](ch59-vector-data.md) §59.8) and which H3 v4 extends with overlap-based modes.

## 60.4 Other DGGS and tiling schemes

Several other systems appear in elevation work, each with a different balance of equal area, equal shape, hierarchy, and convenience.

**HEALPix** (Górski et al. 2005) was built for cosmic-microwave-background sky maps: twelve equal-area base diamonds, each subdivided as a quadtree, giving exactly equal-area cells at every level and rings of constant latitude that make spherical-harmonic analysis efficient. **rHEALPix** (Gibb 2016) rearranges the base cells so that cells are squares in the plane (triangles at the poles) with an exact aperture-9 hierarchy; it underlies Australian and New Zealand DGGS experiments and has an open Python implementation. Equal area makes both ideal for aggregating extensive quantities — area, volume, counts — without weights.

**ISEA** grids (Icosahedral Snyder Equal Area; Sahr, White & Kimerling 2003) project the icosahedron's faces with Snyder's equal-area polyhedral projection and tile them with hexagons at aperture 3 (**ISEA3H**), 4 (**ISEA4H**), or 7, or with triangles (**ISEA4T**). The reference implementation is **DGGRID** (Sahr), which also generates the grids as vector files for use in a GIS. ISEA3H is the system most often used in academic DGGS work and in the OGC DGGS testbeds; equal area is exact, shape distortion is modest, and hierarchy is approximate as with any hexagon grid. **OpenEAGGR** (Riskaware, 2017) implements ISEA4T and ISEA3H with a public API. **GeoSOT** is a Chinese national standard (GB/T 40087-2021 (verify)) that subdivides latitude–longitude space in a degree–minute–second-aligned quadtree with 32 levels, used in Chinese geospatial infrastructure. **A5** (Palmer, 2025) is a recent open pentagonal equal-area DGGS based on a dodecahedron, designed to minimize shape distortion; it is new enough that its adoption and stability are unproven.

Two schemes are not DGGS in the Topic 21 sense but are so common that they must be understood as if they were. **Geohash** (Niemeyer 2008) interleaves the bits of latitude and longitude (a Z-order or Morton curve ⟨H⟩ on the equirectangular plane) and encodes the result in base 32, five bits per character: one character is a cell about 5,000 km × 5,000 km, six characters about 1.2 km × 0.6 km, eight about 38 m × 19 m, twelve about 3.7 cm × 1.9 cm (at the equator; cells narrow with latitude as $\cos\varphi$). Geohash is hierarchical by string prefix, which makes it convenient in key–value stores, but cells are latitude–longitude rectangles that shrink toward the poles; the Z-order curve has large jumps, so points 1 m apart across the Greenwich meridian or the equator share no prefix at all; and the alternating lat/lon bits make cells twice as wide as tall at odd lengths. **Quadkeys** and **XYZ tiles** (the Bing Maps and OpenStreetMap tile schemes) are quadtree addresses on the Web Mercator plane: zoom $z$ divides the world into $2^z \times 2^z$ square tiles of 256 or 512 pixels, and a quadkey is the base-4 string of the path. Tiles are exact in hierarchy and trivially cached, but Web Mercator stops at ±85.05° latitude and its area scale grows as $\sec^2\varphi$, so a tile at 60° covers a quarter of the ground area of an equatorial tile and a tile at 80° about one thirty-third. Per-tile statistics are comparable across latitude only after area normalization; analyse a DEM in a projection suited to the region and only *serve* it as tiles.

## 60.5 Location codes for humans

A cell identifier is for machines; a **location code** is a cell identifier a person can read aloud, write on a form, or key into a radio. The design trade-offs are legibility (short, unambiguous characters), hierarchy (does truncation give a coarser location?), offline computability (can it be decoded without a server?), and openness (who owns the algorithm?).

**MGRS and USNG.** The **Military Grid Reference System** (NATO, 1940s–) ⟨H⟩ and its civilian twin the **United States National Grid** (FGDC-STD-011-2001) are alphanumeric encodings of UTM (and UPS at the poles): the UTM zone number and latitude band letter (`10T`), a two-letter 100 km square identifier (`ES`), and an even number of digits giving easting and northing within the square truncated to the stated precision — two digits each for 1 km (`10T ES 12 34`), five each for 1 m (`10T ES 12345 34567`). Truncation is meaningful: dropping digits gives the containing coarser square, and precision is explicit in the length. The letters I and O are excluded to avoid confusion with 1 and 0. The traps are inherited from UTM: the 100 km square letters repeat every 2,000 km north and every few zones, so a reference without zone and band is ambiguous; zone boundaries cut through regions (a search area straddling 10/11 needs two sets of coordinates), with the Norway and Svalbard exceptions widening zones 31V, 32V, and 31X–37X; latitude bands (C–X, omitting I and O) are 8° tall except X at 12°; and a position written in a neighbouring zone's grid decodes differently in software that does not expect it. MGRS is on WGS 84 by convention, but legacy military maps used local datums (ED50, NAD27) with the same letter scheme — a 1960s reference decoded as WGS 84 is off by 100–200 m. USNG is the standard for US emergency response, printed on US Topo and many state maps, precisely because it is readable, truncatable, and offline.

**Plus codes (Open Location Code).** Developed at Google's Zurich office in 2014 and released as an open specification and open-source library ⟨H⟩, a **plus code** encodes a latitude–longitude cell in a base-20 alphabet (`23456789CFGHJMPQRVWX`, chosen to exclude vowels — avoiding accidental words — and the characters most often confused). The first ten characters, with a `+` after the eighth, name a cell of 1/8000° × 1/8000°, about 13.9 m × 13.9 m at the equator (narrower in longitude with latitude); an optional eleventh character subdivides it 4 × 5 into cells of about 3.5 m × 2.8 m, and further characters continue the refinement. Codes are hierarchical by prefix (each character pair refines 20 × 20), computable offline in a few lines of code, licence-free, and usable where streets have no names — the design target. The **short code** form drops the first four or six characters when a locality is given (`8FVC9G8F+6X` becomes `9G8F+6X Zurich`), which introduces a dependency on resolving the locality correctly. Plus codes assume WGS 84 and carry no elevation, datum realization, or epoch.

**what3words.** Launched in 2013, **what3words** divides the world into a fixed grid of 3 m × 3 m squares (approximately 57 trillion) and assigns each a triple of dictionary words, in each of some fifty languages, via a proprietary algorithm; decoding requires the company's software or API under licence. The appeal — three memorable words — has made it popular in consumer apps and with some emergency services. The criticisms are specific. There is **no hierarchy**: adjacent squares have unrelated words, so a one-word error produces a location anywhere on Earth, and truncation is meaningless. Word lists contain **homophones, plurals, and near-spellings** (`sale`/`sail`, `fork`/`forks`), and independent analyses (notably Tierney 2021) found many confusable pairs within a few kilometres of each other — exactly the distance at which a rescue team would go to the wrong place and not notice; the company disputes the frequency but not the existence of such pairs. The scheme is **closed**: the mapping cannot be verified, archived, or computed offline without the licensed library. Mountain-rescue organizations in several countries advise cross-checking what3words locations against coordinates or a grid reference — sound practice for any spoken location code.

**Others.** The **Maidenhead Locator System** (amateur radio, 1980) encodes latitude–longitude in alternating letter and digit pairs (`JN47`, `FN31pr`), hierarchical, offline, and coarse by design (six characters ≈ 4.6 km × 2.3 km at mid-latitudes). **Natural Area Coding** (1994) is a base-30 encoding with an optional altitude component whose reference is unspecified. **Geohash** strings also serve as human codes, with the Z-order discontinuity as their main defect. For emergency services a code should be sayable over a noisy radio, tolerant of a single character error, decodable offline, and anchored to a published datum; MGRS/USNG and plus codes meet these, what3words meets the first only, and none says anything about height — the dimension that matters on a multi-storey car park, a cliff, or a mine ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

## 60.6 Elevation in DGGS

Putting a DEM onto a DGGS is a resampling, and everything from [Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md) and [Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md) applies — with the added feature that the target cells are not aligned with, and usually not the same size as, the source grid.

**Cell statistics, not cell values.** The honest representation of a DEM in a cell larger than the DEM's posting is a set of **statistics**: mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, count of source cells, and ideally a few percentiles. An H3 resolution-9 cell (0.105 km²) over a 1 m DEM contains about 105,000 source cells; its range may be hundreds of metres in mountains, and a single "elevation" for the cell is a fiction. Store the statistics with the source DEM's identity, posting, and vertical datum, and store the count so that partial cells at edges and voids can be recognized. The minimum is what hydrology wants, the maximum what aviation wants, and the mean what neither wants.

**Match the cell size to the DEM.** A DGGS resolution finer than the DEM's effective resolution manufactures precision: a 30 m DEM on H3 resolution 13 (3.6 m cells) yields about seventy cells per source pixel, all holding the same value or, worse, interpolated values that look like information. Choose the resolution whose cell area is at least several source pixels — H3 resolution 10 (edge ≈ 66 m, area ≈ 15,000 m²) or S2 level 17 (≈ 1,200 m²) for a 30 m DEM; resolution 12–13 or level 20–21 for a 1 m DEM — and record the ratio. The effective resolution of the result is the coarser of the DEM's and the cell's, never the cell's alone.

**Area weighting.** Because DGGS cell boundaries cut source pixels, the statistics should be computed with fractional coverage (the exactextract approach of [Chapter 59](ch59-vector-data.md) §59.8) when source pixels are large relative to cells, or by point-in-cell assignment of pixel centres when they are small (a 1 m DEM into 174 m hexagons has negligible boundary bias; a 30 m DEM into 66 m hexagons does not). In S2 and rHEALPix the aggregation can be done at the finest level once and rolled up exactly; in H3 and ISEA3H it must be recomputed from the source at each resolution you intend to report, or the 7 % leakage of §60.3 enters every level.

**Slope and aspect on hexagons.** A hexagonal cell has six equidistant neighbours, and the gradient can be estimated by a least-squares plane fit through the seven centre elevations or by the discrete analogue of Horn's operator on the six neighbours ([Chapter 57](ch57-visualizing-dems.md) §57.2): $\partial z/\partial x \approx \frac{1}{3d}\sum_{k} z_k \cos\theta_k$ and $\partial z/\partial y \approx \frac{1}{3d}\sum_{k} z_k \sin\theta_k$ for the six neighbours at angles $\theta_k$ and centre spacing $d$. The result is more isotropic than square-grid slope — there is no diagonal/orthogonal asymmetry — but it is slope of the *aggregated* surface at the cell scale, which is systematically lower than slope from the source DEM (aggregation smooths; [Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)). Report the scale. The orientation of H3's hexagons rotates with resolution and position, so "aspect" must be computed in geographic terms from the neighbour bearings, not in cell-index terms.

**Hydrology on hexagons.** Single-flow-direction routing on a hexagonal grid has six choices instead of D8's eight, and the choices are equidistant, which removes the D8 bias toward the cardinal and diagonal directions ([Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)); several experimental implementations exist (hexagonal flow accumulation on H3 and ISEA3H in research code), but production hydrologic tools remain square-grid. For hydrology the cell statistic must be the *minimum* (or a hydro-conditioned mean), pits must be filled at the cell scale, and streams narrower than the cell vanish exactly as they do when coarsening a raster.

**3D and time.** Topic 21 is two-dimensional; research systems extend DGGS with vertical subdivisions (prism-stacked cells, exponential radial schemes) for atmospheric, subsurface, and bathymetric volumes, without a dominant standard. For DEM work the question is simpler: a cell's elevation statistics must state their vertical datum and epoch exactly as a raster must, and a DGGS table with a `mean_elev` column and no `v_datum` column is a shapefile problem in a new container. Time enters as a dimension of the table (one row per cell per epoch), and the DGGS's stability — S2 and H3 cell IDs are fixed for all time; the Earth under them moves at plate rates ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)) — means that a cell at resolution 15 (1 m) in Australia is traversed by the land in about 15 years at 7 cm/yr. Below about 10 m cell size, the DGGS is a fixed frame over a moving surface, and the ITRF epoch of the coordinates used for assignment belongs in the metadata.

> **Try it.** Aggregate a DEM onto H3 and S2 cells and demonstrate the H3 parent–child leakage numerically. Expected result: H3 resolution-9 means computed directly from the DEM versus means obtained by averaging the seven resolution-10 children differ by up to a few metres in rugged terrain (zero in flat terrain), with the count of source pixels in the children's union exceeding the parent's own count by about 7 %; S2 level-14 means computed directly and by rolling up level-16 children agree to floating-point precision.
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np, rasterio, h3, s2sphere, pandas as pd
> from rasterio.warp import transform
>
> src = rasterio.open("dem_30m_utm.tif")           # any projected DEM
> z = src.read(1, masked=True)
> rows, cols = np.where(~z.mask)
> xs, ys = rasterio.transform.xy(src.transform, rows, cols)  # pixel centres
> lons, lats = transform(src.crs, "EPSG:4326", xs, ys)
> df = pd.DataFrame({"lon": lons, "lat": lats, "z": z[rows, cols].data})
>
> # --- H3: assign at res 10, aggregate, then compare parent roll-up vs direct ---
> df["h10"] = [h3.latlng_to_cell(la, lo, 10) for la, lo in zip(df.lat, df.lon)]
> df["h9"]  = [h3.latlng_to_cell(la, lo, 9)  for la, lo in zip(df.lat, df.lon)]
> direct9 = df.groupby("h9").z.agg(["mean", "count"])
> c10 = df.groupby("h10").z.agg(["sum", "count"])
> c10["parent"] = [h3.cell_to_parent(c, 9) for c in c10.index]
> rolled9 = c10.groupby("parent").agg(sum=("sum", "sum"), count=("count", "sum"))
> rolled9["mean"] = rolled9["sum"] / rolled9["count"]
> cmp = direct9.join(rolled9, lsuffix="_direct", rsuffix="_rolled", how="inner")
> print("H3 res 9: |mean_direct - mean_rolled| 95th pct =",
>       np.percentile(np.abs(cmp.mean_direct - cmp.mean_rolled), 95).round(2), "m")
> print("H3 res 9: median(count_rolled / count_direct) =",
>       (cmp.count_rolled / cmp.count_direct).median().round(3))   # ≈ 1.07
>
> # --- S2: assign at level 16, roll up to 14, compare with direct ---
> def s2id(la, lo, lvl):
>     return s2sphere.CellId.from_lat_lng(s2sphere.LatLng.from_degrees(la, lo)).parent(lvl).id()
> df["s16"] = [s2id(la, lo, 16) for la, lo in zip(df.lat, df.lon)]
> df["s14"] = [s2id(la, lo, 14) for la, lo in zip(df.lat, df.lon)]
> direct14 = df.groupby("s14").z.agg(["mean", "count"])
> c16 = df.groupby("s16").z.agg(["sum", "count"])
> c16["parent"] = [s2sphere.CellId(i).parent(14).id() for i in c16.index]
> rolled14 = c16.groupby("parent").agg(sum=("sum", "sum"), count=("count", "sum"))
> rolled14["mean"] = rolled14["sum"] / rolled14["count"]
> cmp2 = direct14.join(rolled14, lsuffix="_direct", rsuffix="_rolled", how="inner")
> print("S2 L14: max |mean_direct - mean_rolled| =",
>       np.abs(cmp2.mean_direct - cmp2.mean_rolled).max())          # ~1e-12
> ```

## 60.7 Converting between systems

Every conversion between a raster and a DGGS, or between two DGGS, is a resampling and is **lossy in both directions**. Raster → DGGS → raster does not recover the raster: the first step averages over cells that do not align with pixels, the second assigns each output pixel the value of the cell containing its centre, and the round trip has the smoothing of the first plus the blockiness of the second. With cells several times the pixel size the result has the cells' effective resolution; with cells near the pixel size it shows moiré-like **cell-boundary artefacts** where the hexagonal tiling beats against the pixel lattice — a honeycomb texture in a hillshade, periodic stripes in a slope map. These describe the grids, not the terrain; treat slope or curvature derived from a DEM resampled onto hexagons and back as contaminated at wavelengths near the cell size.

**Area-weighted reprojection** is the correct way to move an extensive quantity (volume, counts, area of a class) between grids: compute the intersection area of each source cell with each target cell and distribute values proportionally. For intensive quantities (elevation, slope) area weighting gives the area-weighted mean, which is right for a "mean elevation" and wrong for a minimum or a maximum; those must be taken over the source cells intersecting the target, not weighted. Most DGGS libraries do point assignment (pixel centre → cell) rather than area intersection, because it is fast; it is adequate when source pixels are much smaller than target cells and biased otherwise ([Chapter 59](ch59-vector-data.md) §59.8).

**H3 non-containment** produces a specific conversion error: if a quantity is computed per resolution-$r$ cell by intersecting polygons or rasters with the cell's true boundary, and then children are summed to parents, boundary areas are counted in both the parent's own value and in the neighbouring parent's children — **double counting** of up to 7 % of each cell's area at every level of a hierarchical roll-up, compounding across levels. The remedy is to compute every reported resolution from the source, or to accept the H3 "parent" as an indexing abstraction and label the rolled-up value as a flower-shaped neighbourhood statistic rather than a cell statistic.

**Between DGGS** (S2 to H3, H3 to ISEA3H) the only exact path is through the source data; converting cell statistics directly from one tiling to another is a second resampling with compounded loss. When a pipeline needs both (S2 for storage, H3 for an analyst's dashboard), compute both from the source and keep them as sibling products.

## 60.8 Issues with coding schemes

Several problems are common to all the schemes in this chapter and are worth stating together.

**Ambiguity of characters.** Spoken and handwritten codes suffer the usual confusions — 0/O, 1/I/l, 2/Z, 5/S, 8/B. The plus-code alphabet designs most of these out; MGRS omits I and O but is mostly digits. Neither has a check character, so a single error is undetectable from the code alone, and emergency services rely on read-back and a map cross-check. what3words' designers argued that word errors are more detectable than digit errors, which is true for random nonsense words and false for homophones.

**Truncation semantics.** Truncating MGRS, plus codes, geohash, or S2/H3 IDs gives a coarser cell *containing* (or, for H3, approximately containing) the original; truncating what3words is undefined; and truncating decimal latitude–longitude gives a corner, not a containing cell, which is why "rounded coordinates" systematically bias toward the south-west. A truncated code should be read as "somewhere in this cell," never as "the centre of this cell"; the implied precision is the cell size, and reporting a plus-code cell centre to seven decimals is false precision.

**Datum and epoch.** Every scheme assumes WGS 84 and says nothing about realization or epoch. At metre-scale precision (MGRS at 1 m, plus codes and what3words at 3 m, H3 resolution 15, S2 beyond level 23), the differences between WGS 84 realizations (decimetres), between WGS 84 and a national datum such as NAD83(2011) or GDA2020 (1–2 m), and between epochs on a moving plate (7 cm/yr in Australia) are comparable to or larger than the cell ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)). A code at these precisions is attached to coordinates uncertain at the cell scale unless datum and epoch are stated alongside it — which no scheme provides for.

**Vertical absent.** No widely used scheme encodes height. For most uses this is sensible (the code names a place on the ground), but it means that DGGS tables of elevation statistics must carry the vertical datum as a column, that location codes given for a point on a bridge, a cliff, or a building floor are ambiguous in the dimension that matters, and that 3D DGGS extensions remain research systems.

**Ownership and longevity.** S2 and H3 are open-source under permissive licences and embedded in enough infrastructure that their cell definitions are effectively frozen; plus codes and geohash can be reimplemented from their specifications in an afternoon; MGRS/USNG is a government standard. what3words is a private product with a licensed decoder: a dataset tagged only with what3words addresses cannot be decoded without that company's continued existence and permission, a risk to be weighed explicitly for archival, legal, or safety-critical use ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md), [Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)). The legal status of any proprietary code as an *address* is unsettled.

> **Case file.** In 2021, a security researcher (Andrew Tierney, writing as Cybergibbons) published an analysis of the what3words English word list showing numerous pairs of addresses differing by one plural or homophone that resolve to locations within a few kilometres of each other, and reported examples where emergency callers' spoken addresses were mis-transcribed to a plausible nearby square; UK mountain-rescue teams had already reported cases of being sent to wrong locations. what3words responded that confusable pairs are deliberately placed far apart in most cases and that the system's overall error rate is low. The lesson for this book is not about one product: any location code used operationally should be cross-checked against an independent representation (a grid reference, coordinates, or a map), and its failure mode on a single error — local or global — should be known to the people using it. (Sources: Tierney 2021; UK press coverage of mountain-rescue reports, 2021.)

## 60.9 Choosing a scheme

The choice depends on what the cell or code is *for*.

**Indexing and joins.** S2 if you need exact hierarchy, coverings, and spatially coherent ID ranges (point clouds, tile catalogues, large joins); H3 if your warehouse already speaks it and neighbourhood queries matter more than roll-ups; geohash only where a string prefix index is the only tool available. In all cases the index is a key, not a geometry: keep the real coordinates and CRS in the record.

**Analysis.** For any extensive quantity that will be summed, compared across regions, or rolled up — area of a land-cover class, volume of change, number of points — use an **equal-area** DGGS (ISEA3H via DGGRID, rHEALPix, HEALPix) or area-normalize explicitly. H3 is acceptable for intensive quantities and neighbourhood operations within regions small enough that its area variation is negligible (a few percent over a few hundred kilometres), with per-resolution recomputation rather than roll-ups. Never do area statistics on Web Mercator tiles.

**Human communication.** MGRS/USNG where a national grid is printed on maps and the audience is trained (emergency response, military, search and rescue); plus codes for addressing where streets have no names and the audience has smartphones; always with a read-back and a datum assumption understood. Proprietary word-based codes should be accompanied by coordinates.

**Distribution and tiling.** For serving DEMs on the web, projected square tiles (XYZ/quadkey, or better a regional TileMatrixSet) and cloud-optimized formats (COG, Zarr, COPC) remain the practical choice, because every client understands them and analysis should happen in the native grid anyway; use a DGGS as the *catalogue index* over those tiles, unless an equal-area global analysis is the product itself — in which case choose the DGGS first and derive tiles from it.

Whatever the choice, record it the way you would record a projection: the scheme and its version, the resolution or level, the assignment rule (centre, area-weighted, minimum), the source DEM with its posting and vertical datum, and the horizontal datum and epoch of the coordinates used to assign cells. A DGGS table without that provenance is a grid without a georeference.

## Then & now

Grids for naming places are older than computing. Military grids appeared on First World War artillery maps and were systematized after the Second World War with **UTM (1947) and MGRS** ⟨H⟩, which tied a readable alphanumeric reference to a projection rather than a local sheet; the civilian US National Grid (2001) is the same idea with an FGDC standard behind it. **Quadtree indexing** ⟨H⟩ (Finkel & Bentley 1974; Morton's Z-order key for IBM's Canadian geographic files, 1966) made hierarchical spatial subdivision a data-structure technique, and the quadtree on a projected plane is the ancestor of XYZ tiles, quadkeys, and geohash. The idea of a *global* polyhedral grid for science came from Fuller's Dymaxion projection (1943) and was formalized by Sahr, White & Kimerling's "Geodesic Discrete Global Grid Systems" (2003), which defined the design space — base polyhedron, orientation, cell shape, aperture, projection, addressing — that every later system occupies.

**Geohash** (Niemeyer, 2008) brought hierarchical cell codes to the web as URL-friendly strings. **S2** was developed inside Google for its spatial storage and services during the late 2000s and 2010s and open-sourced in 2017 ⟨H⟩; **H3** was open-sourced by Uber in 2018, motivated by neighbourhood analysis of ride demand. Both became de facto standards in cloud data warehouses within a few years. **Plus codes** (2014) ⟨H⟩ addressed the human side — an open, offline, hierarchical code for places without addresses — while **what3words** (2013) took the proprietary, word-based route.

Standardization followed practice: **OGC Topic 21** (2017; version 2.0, 2021) defined what a DGGS is, and the **OGC API – DGGS** draft defines how to query one over the web. The present frontier is DGGS as the native geometry of analysis-ready data cubes — which would make the aggregation issues of §§60.6–60.7 first-class metadata — and 3D and temporal extensions; for elevation data the dominant storage geometry remains the projected raster, with DGGS as the index above it.

## Mathematics

**Morton (Z-order) and Hilbert curves.** The Morton key of integer cell coordinates $(i, j)$ interleaves their bits: $m = \sum_k \left(i_k 2^{2k} + j_k 2^{2k+1}\right)$, where $i_k$ is bit $k$ of $i$. Geohash is a Morton key on quantized longitude and latitude, encoded base 32. Z-order preserves locality on average but has discontinuities: the keys of $(2^n - 1, y)$ and $(2^n, y)$ differ in their highest bits. The **Hilbert curve** maps the unit square to the unit interval so that consecutive cells are always edge-adjacent; its recursive construction rotates and reflects the four sub-squares at each level, and the mapping from $(i,j)$ to the curve index $d$ is computed in $O(\text{levels})$ bit operations. S2 uses the Hilbert order within each face so that a contiguous range of cell IDs is a compact region — the property that turns region queries into range scans.

**S2 face projection.** A point on the unit sphere $\mathbf{p}$ is assigned to the cube face with the largest $|p_x|, |p_y|, |p_z|$ and projected gnomonically to face coordinates $(u, v) \in [-1, 1]^2$ (ratios of the other two components to the dominant one). The gnomonic projection has area distortion of up to 5.2× across a face; S2 applies the quadratic transform $s = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + 3u}$ for $u \ge 0$ (and symmetrically for $u < 0$) to obtain $(s, t) \in [0, 1]^2$, which reduces the max/min cell-area ratio at a level to about 2.08 and the edge-length ratio to about 1.4. Cell $(s, t)$ coordinates at level $k$ are the leading $k$ bits of $s$ and $t$ scaled to $2^{30}$; the Hilbert position follows.

**H3 aperture 7.** In a hexagonal lattice with child centre spacing $d$, parent centres lie at lattice vectors of squared length $7d^2$ — e.g. $2\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2$ for basis vectors $\mathbf{a}_1 = (d, 0)$, $\mathbf{a}_2 = (d/2, d\sqrt{3}/2)$ — giving parent spacing $d\sqrt{7}$ and a rotation of $\arctan(\sqrt{3}/5) \approx 19.11°$ between the parent and child lattices. Parent inradius is $\sqrt{7}$ times the child inradius; the six peripheral children, centred at distance $d$ from the parent centre, each have exactly $1/12$ of their area outside the parent in the planar case (§60.3), so the children's union exceeds the parent by $6 \times \tfrac{1}{12} \times \tfrac{1}{7} = \tfrac{1}{14}$ of the parent's area. The twelve pentagons follow from Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ applied to a tiling by hexagons and pentagons: with every vertex of degree 3, $\sum_f (6 - n_f) = 12$, so exactly twelve pentagons are needed (and no other polygon mix with only five- and six-sided faces works).

**Equal-area polyhedral projections.** Snyder's (1992) equal-area projection for polyhedral globes maps each face of the icosahedron (or other solid) to the plane with area exactly preserved by construction, at the cost of angular distortion that grows toward face corners; ISEA grids inherit exact equal area and the shape distortion. HEALPix achieves equal area with a Lambert cylindrical-equal-area band at low latitudes and a Collignon-like polar cap; rHEALPix rearranges HEALPix's polar triangles into squares. For any equal-area DGGS, cell area at resolution $r$ is simply $A_\oplus / N_r$ with $N_r$ the cell count — for ISEA3H, $N_r = 10 \cdot 3^r + 2$.

**Web Mercator area distortion.** Mercator's scale factor is $k = \sec\varphi$ in both directions, so area is inflated by $\sec^2\varphi$: at 45° by 2.0, at 60° by 4.0, at 80° by 33.2. A per-tile count $n$ at latitude $\varphi$ becomes a density $n \sec^2\varphi / A_0$ with $A_0$ the equatorial tile area. The same $\sec\varphi$ enters geohash cells' east–west width.

**Precision per code length.** For a base-$b$ hierarchical code with $k$ characters covering a lat/lon extent of $\Delta_0$ degrees, the cell extent is $\Delta_0 / b^{k/2}$ per axis when characters alternate axes (geohash: $b = 32$, five bits split 3/2 or 2/3) or $\Delta_0 / b^{k/2}$ per axis when character pairs encode both axes (plus codes: $b = 20$, the first pair names a 20° cell and each further pair refines both axes by 20, so after five pairs the cell is $20°/20^4 = 1/8000°$). Converting degrees to metres requires $111.32\,\text{km}/° \times \cos\varphi$ in longitude, which is why all lat/lon-based codes name cells that narrow toward the poles.

## Validation & uncertainty

DGGS and location codes introduce no measurement error of their own — they are deterministic functions of coordinates — but they add **representation error** in three ways, and they inherit the coordinate uncertainty of whatever was encoded.

**Quantization error.** A code or cell replaces a point by a region; the implied uncertainty is the cell's extent. For a uniformly distributed true position within a square cell of side $s$, the RMS distance from the cell centre is $s/\sqrt{6} \approx 0.41 s$ and the maximum is $0.71 s$; for a plus code's 13.9 m cell, 5.7 m RMS and 9.8 m max; for H3 resolution 9 (edge 174 m, inradius ≈ 151 m), about 90 m RMS. State it. Never report a decoded cell centre with more precision than the cell.

**Aggregation error.** A cell statistic's uncertainty has two parts: the source DEM's error propagated through the statistic, and the terrain's variability within the cell. For a mean over $n_{eff}$ effectively independent source pixels the DEM contribution is $\sigma_z/\sqrt{n_{eff}}$ — negligible for lidar in large cells; for 30 m global DEMs with correlated errors ([Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)) the correlation length can exceed the cell and the mean inherits nearly the full $\sigma_z$. Terrain variability is not an error of the mean but is the uncertainty of using the mean for any point in the cell; always report the per-cell standard deviation and range alongside it. Minimum and maximum statistics are biased by DEM noise (the minimum of $n$ noisy values is lower than the true minimum by roughly $\sigma_z \sqrt{2 \ln n}$ for Gaussian noise — about 3σ for $n = 100$, 4σ for $n = 10^4$), so a "cell minimum elevation" from a noisy DEM is systematically too low, and a cell maximum too high, by amounts that depend on $n$; use percentiles (1st/99th) where robustness matters.

**Hierarchy error.** In S2, rHEALPix, HEALPix, and quadtrees, roll-ups are exact and introduce nothing. In H3 and ISEA3H the roll-up of children to parents mixes in 1/14 of the neighbours' area (planar value); the resulting error in a parent mean is bounded by $\tfrac{1}{14}$ of the difference between the parent's own mean and its neighbours' — small on gentle terrain, metres to tens of metres at escarpments and coasts — and the error in a parent *sum* or *count* is a systematic +7 %. Test it directly, as the Try-it in §60.6 does: compute the statistic both ways and difference them.

> **Worked example.** A 30 m DEM (σ_z = 4 m, error correlation length ≈ 300 m) is aggregated to H3 resolution 8 (area 0.737 km², ≈ 820 source pixels, inradius ≈ 400 m). DEM contribution to the cell mean: with correlation length comparable to the cell, $n_{eff} \approx (820 \text{ m}/300 \text{ m})^2 \approx 7$ (cell diameter over correlation length, squared — approximate), so $\sigma_{\bar z} \approx 4/\sqrt{7} \approx 1.5$ m. Terrain variability in a dissected plateau: per-cell σ ≈ 60 m, range ≈ 250 m. Quantization of a point query: 0.41 × 820 m ≈ 340 m RMS. Cell minimum bias: $4\sqrt{2 \ln 820} \approx 4 \times 3.7 \approx 15$ m too low. Roll-up of resolution-9 children to resolution 8 at a 200 m escarpment crossing the cell: neighbour mean differs by ≈ 200 m, leakage ≈ 1/14, so the rolled-up mean is biased by up to ≈ 14 m relative to the direct mean. Of these numbers the one most often reported — the mean's 1.5 m — is the least informative; the per-cell σ of 60 m is the one a user needs.

**Code-transcription error.** For human codes the error model is discrete: a single-character substitution in MGRS's last digits displaces by up to the precision step times 9 (a 1 m reference with one wrong digit is at most 9 m, 90 m, 900 m… away depending on position); in a plus code, an error in the last pair displaces within the 20 × 20 parent (≈ 280 m), in the penultimate pair within ≈ 5.6 km; in what3words, anywhere. **Test transcription channels** the way hydrographic offices test chart reading: have the code read over the intended medium and decoded independently, and count failures. **Datum/epoch error** applies to all: a code at metre precision generated from GDA94 coordinates and decoded assuming GDA2020 is 1.8 m off; generated from 2010 NAD83(2011) coordinates and used in 2026 on a plate moving 2 cm/yr is 0.3 m off — within a 3 m cell for now, not for ever.

**What to report** with any DGGS elevation product: scheme and version; resolution and cell area (nominal and, for non-equal-area systems, the range over the product's extent); assignment rule and whether area-weighted; source DEM identity, posting, vertical datum, and accuracy; per-cell count, mean, σ, min/max or percentiles; whether statistics were computed directly or rolled up (and for H3, the leakage caveat); horizontal datum and epoch of the coordinates used to assign cells. With any location code: the scheme, the assumed datum, and the cell size as the positional uncertainty.

## Pitfalls

- **Summing H3 children and comparing to parents** → aperture-7 hexagons do not nest → compute each reported resolution from the source; treat `cell_to_parent` as an index, not a geometry; expect +7 % in counts and boundary bias in means.
- **Web Mercator tiles used for area statistics** → tiles look equal on screen → normalize by sec²φ or use an equal-area DGGS; never compute densities per XYZ tile without latitude correction.
- **MGRS zone-boundary mistakes** → a search area or dataset straddles two zones, or 100 km square letters repeat → always carry the zone and band; test software on points within 1 km of a zone boundary and in the Norway/Svalbard exceptions.
- **A DEM resampled to hexagons and then differentiated** → honeycomb beat pattern between the hex tiling and the pixel lattice → derive slope/curvature from the native raster; use the DGGS for aggregated statistics only.
- **Emergency responders handed a what3words address with a transposed or homophone word** → no hierarchy, so the error is global and silent → cross-check with coordinates or a grid reference; use read-back; prefer schemes whose single-character errors are local.
- **Assuming a code encodes a datum or epoch** → all schemes assume "WGS 84" and none record the realization → state datum and epoch with any code at metre precision; expect 1–2 m national-datum offsets and plate motion.
- **DGGS resolution finer than the DEM's effective resolution** → libraries will happily make 1 m hexagons from 30 m data → choose cells at least several source pixels in area; store the source pixel count per cell.
- **Reporting the cell mean as "the elevation"** → convenience → report σ, range, and count per cell; mean, minimum, and maximum serve different users and must be labelled.
- **Cell minimum from a noisy DEM used as a hydrologic or safety value** → extreme-value bias of several σ → use low percentiles or a denoised DEM; state the bias.
- **Decoding a truncated code to its cell centre with full precision** → software returns a centroid → report the cell size as the uncertainty; never add decimals a code does not carry.
- **S2 or H3 areas assumed equal across a region** → both vary (≈ 2× for S2 within a level; smoothly for H3) → use the library's per-cell area function and weight by it, or use an equal-area system.
- **Storing only the proprietary code** → the dataset becomes undecodable if the licence or company lapses → store coordinates with CRS alongside any code; archive open codes only.
- **Pentagons breaking neighbourhood code** → twelve per H3 resolution with five neighbours and half the area → test on a pentagon cell explicitly (`h3.is_pentagon`).

## Key takeaways

- DGGS and location codes are indexes and names, not measurements: they add quantization and aggregation error on top of the coordinates' own uncertainty, and encode no datum, epoch, or height.
- S2 gives exact hierarchy and Hilbert-ordered IDs (ideal for indexing and roll-ups) at the cost of ≈ 2× area variation; H3 gives isotropic neighbourhoods and fine resolution at the cost of approximate containment (1/14 leakage per level) and twelve pentagons; equal-area systems (ISEA, rHEALPix, HEALPix) are the right tools when sums and areas matter.
- Match the cell size to the DEM's effective resolution, aggregate with a stated rule, and report count, mean, σ, and extremes per cell — never a single "elevation."
- Compute every reported H3 resolution from the source; roll up only in exact-hierarchy systems.
- Web Mercator tiles are for serving, not for area statistics; their area distortion is sec²φ.
- For human codes, prefer open, offline, hierarchical schemes (MGRS/USNG, plus codes), know each scheme's single-error failure mode, and always cross-check operational locations against coordinates.
- Every scheme assumes WGS 84 without saying which realization or epoch; at metre precision that assumption is already a 1–2 m error.
- Record scheme, version, resolution, assignment rule, source DEM, datums, and epoch with any DGGS product, exactly as you would record a projection.
