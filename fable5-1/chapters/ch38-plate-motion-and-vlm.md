# Chapter 38 — Plate motion, reference-frame dynamics, and vertical land motion

> **Part VIII — The dynamic Earth.** This chapter treats the slow, steady, large-scale motions — plates rotating, continents rebounding from ice, cities sinking into their aquifers — that make every coordinate a function of time and every published height a value with an expiry date.

**In this chapter.** You will learn why a coordinate on Earth's surface has a velocity, how plate motion is modelled (Euler poles, NNR-MORVEL56, the ITRF plate models) and why a global frame such as ITRF cannot be "the same" as a plate-fixed national datum for more than an instant. You will work through the 14-parameter Helmert transformation with rates and the epoch-propagation formula, compute the 1.8 m GDA94 → GDA2020 shift from first principles, and see how deformation models (HTDP, the NZGD2000 model, Japan's semi-dynamic correction, Australia's ATRF, the U.S. IFVM) are built, gridded, and delivered through PROJ. You will survey vertical land motion — glacial isostatic adjustment, tectonics, groundwater and hydrocarbon extraction, sediment compaction, permafrost thaw, mining — with magnitudes for the classic cases, learn the five ways it is measured (CORS, InSAR, levelling, tide gauge–altimetry, absolute gravity) and what each can and cannot resolve, and finish with the consequences for DEMs: benchmark decay, control that moved between campaigns, relative versus absolute sea-level rise, and the New Orleans lesson that a levee built to a decayed benchmark is lower than its drawings say.

## 38.1 Plate tectonics as a coordinate problem

The lithosphere is broken into plates that move relative to one another at 1–10 cm/yr, and relative to any Earth-centred frame at comparable speeds. For a cartographer with metre-level precision this was invisible; for GNSS with millimetre repeatability it is the dominant signal in any multi-year position series. The geodetic response has been to describe the motion rather than deny it: the **International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF)** assigns every station a position at a reference epoch *and* a linear velocity (plus, since ITRF2014, post-seismic deformation models and seasonal terms for some stations), and the frame as a whole is constrained so that there is no net rotation of the lithosphere — the **no-net-rotation (NNR)** condition — which is a mathematical convenience, not a physical anchor ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

To first order a plate moves as a rigid cap on a sphere, and a rigid rotation on a sphere is described by an **Euler pole** (latitude, longitude) and an angular rate ω. The velocity of a point at position vector $\mathbf{r}$ (from the geocentre) is $\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf{r}$ (Mathematics). Plate motion models are tables of Euler vectors: geological models such as **NUVEL-1A** (DeMets et al. 1994) and **MORVEL** (DeMets, Gordon & Argus 2010) are built from seafloor-spreading rates, transform-fault azimuths, and earthquake slip vectors and so average motion over ~3 Myr; **NNR-MORVEL56** (Argus, Gordon & DeMets 2011) extends MORVEL to 56 plates and imposes the no-net-rotation condition so that it can be compared with geodetic frames. Geodetic models — the **ITRF2014 plate motion model** (Altamimi et al. 2017) and its ITRF2020 successor — fit Euler vectors to the velocities of GNSS stations on stable plate interiors and so represent the *present-day* motion over a few decades. The geological and geodetic models agree to within about 1–2 mm/yr for the large plates, a remarkable confirmation that plate motion is steady over millions of years; where they disagree, it is usually because of GIA or because a station is not on rigid interior.

The magnitudes that matter for elevation work: Australia (Indo-Australian plate) ≈ 7 cm/yr toward the north-northeast; the Pacific plate ≈ 7–10 cm/yr toward the northwest; North America ≈ 1.5–2.5 cm/yr toward the southwest (varying across the continent because the Euler pole is nearby, off the Pacific coast of South America); Eurasia ≈ 2.5 cm/yr toward the northeast; Africa ≈ 2 cm/yr; Antarctica ≈ 1 cm/yr. Vertical components of rigid plate motion are zero by construction; vertical station velocities in ITRF come from GIA, tectonics, and local effects (§38.4) and are typically within ±3 mm/yr outside GIA centres and plate boundaries.

## 38.2 Plate-fixed datums versus global dynamic frames

A national mapping agency cannot have its cadastral boundaries and its bridge drawings move 7 cm every year. The solution adopted since the early 1990s is the **plate-fixed datum**: a frame that rotates with the plate, so that coordinates of points on the plate's stable interior are approximately constant. **NAD83** (realizations NAD83(CORS96), NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00, and the Pacific and Mariana variants) is fixed to the North American plate; **ETRS89** is defined to be coincident with ITRS at epoch 1989.0 and co-rotating with the stable part of the Eurasian plate (realized as ETRF2000, ETRF2014, and ETRF2020); **GDA94** was fixed to the Australian plate at 1994.0 and **GDA2020** at 2020.0; **NZGD2000** is fixed at 2000.0 but New Zealand straddles the Australian–Pacific boundary, so it requires a deformation model to be usable at all (§38.3). **WGS 84** is not plate-fixed: it is the U.S. Department of Defense's realization of a global frame, aligned to ITRF at the centimetre level since the G1150 realization (2002), through G2139 (2021) to G2296 (2024), with its own reference epochs; "WGS 84" without a realization and epoch is ambiguous at the metre level, since the original 1987 realization (Transit-based) differed from ITRF by roughly a metre ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

The consequence of fixing a frame to a plate is a growing offset from the global frame. ETRS89 and ITRF coincided in 1989; Eurasia has since moved about 2.5 cm/yr, so by 2024 a point's ETRS89 and ITRF2020 coordinates differ by roughly 0.85 m — the same physical point, two coordinate pairs, both correct in their own frame. NAD83(2011) and ITRF2014 differ by 1–2 m across the conterminous United States, mostly the inherited offset of NAD83's original 1986 realization from the geocentre, with the plate-rotation component growing at 1–2 cm/yr. GDA94 and ITRF differed by nothing in 1994 and by ~1.8 m in 2020: 26 years × ~7 cm/yr. That is why Australia defined GDA2020, a new plate-fixed datum equal to ITRF2014 at 2020.0 — the coordinates jumped by about 1.8 m to the northeast on adoption, and will diverge again from ITRF at 7 cm/yr until the next redefinition. For users who need to work in the global frame continuously, Geoscience Australia also defined the **Australian Terrestrial Reference Frame (ATRF2014)**, a fully dynamic frame in which coordinates carry an epoch and are propagated with the Australian plate motion model.

> **Worked example.** *GDA94 → GDA2020 shift.* Geoscience Australia's Australian Plate Motion Model (GDA2020 Technical Manual) gives the plate's rotation rates in ITRF2014 as $\dot\theta_x = 1.50379$, $\dot\theta_y = 1.18346$, $\dot\theta_z = 1.20716$ mas/yr. Converting to rad/yr (1 mas = 4.848 × 10⁻⁹ rad), forming $\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf{r}$ at Perth (−31.95°, 115.86°, $|\mathbf{r}|$ ≈ 6371 km), and rotating into local north/east gives $v_N \approx 0.058$ m/yr, $v_E \approx 0.038$ m/yr — about 6.9 cm/yr toward azimuth 34°. Over the 26.0 years from 1994.0 to 2020.0 that is 1.50 m north and 1.00 m east, a total of approximately 1.80 m: the famous shift. The same calculation at Canberra (−35.3°, 149.1°) gives $v_N \approx 0.055$, $v_E \approx 0.018$ m/yr (5.8 cm/yr, azimuth 18°) and a 26-year shift of about 1.51 m — the shift varies across the continent because the Euler pole is finite distance away. The GDA94 → GDA2020 7-parameter similarity transformation published by Geoscience Australia reproduces the plate motion within a few centimetres; residual differences up to ~10 cm arise because GDA94 was realized from ITRF92 at 1994.0 with the distortions of that era, which is why NTv2 distortion grids are also provided for sub-decimetre work. The shift is almost purely horizontal; ellipsoidal heights change by a few centimetres (the frame-realization difference between ITRF92 and ITRF2014), and AHD orthometric heights are unaffected except through the new geoid model (AUSGeoid2020) that accompanies the datum.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Static datum" (GDA2020, NAD83(2011)) means the *coordinates* are held fixed at a reference epoch; the ground still moves, and observations made in a dynamic frame (PPP, RTK from a network broadcasting ITRF) must be moved to the reference epoch before comparison. "Semi-dynamic" (NZGD2000, Japan) means the same but with an official deformation model for doing so. "Dynamic" or "kinematic" (ITRF, ATRF, the modernized NSRS) means coordinates carry an epoch and are expected to change. "Plate-fixed" describes which plate the static frame rotates with. The four terms are not synonyms, and a dataset labelled with only "WGS 84" or "GDA" has told you none of them.

## 38.3 Deformation models and time-dependent transforms

Within a plate interior a plate-fixed datum works because the residual motion is a few millimetres per year. Near plate boundaries, over GIA centres, and in subsiding basins it does not, and the agency must supply a **deformation model**: a gridded velocity field (and, after large earthquakes, gridded displacement "patches," [Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)) that lets a user move a coordinate from the epoch of observation to the datum's reference epoch and back.

**HTDP** (Horizontal Time-Dependent Positioning; Snay 1999; Pearson & Snay 2013) is the U.S. National Geodetic Survey's tool for this: it combines a plate-rotation model with gridded intraplate velocities for the western United States and Alaska and a catalog of coseismic and post-seismic displacement models for large earthquakes since 1934, and it transforms positions between ITRF realizations and NAD83 realizations at any two epochs. Despite the name, recent versions carry vertical velocities as well. **IFVM** (Intra-Frame Velocity Model) is its designated successor in the modernized NSRS: a 3D velocity model (with vertical from GNSS and InSAR) defined for each of the four plate-fixed frames of the new system — NATRF2022, PATRF2022, CATRF2022, and MATRF2022 for the North American, Pacific, Caribbean, and Mariana plates — all of which are defined as rotations of ITRF2020 so that a point on the stable interior has nearly constant coordinates, with IFVM handling the rest (NOAA Technical Reports NOS NGS 62 and 67).

**New Zealand's NZGD2000 deformation model** is the most complete example of a semi-dynamic datum: a national secular velocity grid (about 4–5 cm/yr of relative motion across the plate boundary, so a point in the central South Island moves several centimetres per year relative to the datum's reference epoch 2000.0) plus a series of dated patches for the Darfield (2010), Christchurch (2011), Cook Strait (2013), and Kaikōura (2016) earthquakes and for slow-slip events. LINZ publishes the model with a version date; coordinates in NZGD2000 are correct only when the model version is known. **Japan's Geospatial Information Authority (GSI)** operates a **semi-dynamic correction** (since 2010) in which surveyors reduce their GNSS observations to the Japanese Geodetic Datum 2011 reference epoch using an annually updated velocity grid derived from the GEONET CORS network; after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, the datum itself was re-realized for the affected regions (JGD2011), and the correction parameters were reset ([Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)). **Australia's ATRF** uses the plate motion model for propagation across the whole continent, since intraplate deformation there is small; **Canada** publishes NAD83(CSRS) velocity grids dominated by GIA.

The transformation mathematics is the **14-parameter Helmert** (seven parameters plus their rates), which handles the *frame-to-frame* part, combined with a **point-motion operation** (velocity model or deformation grid) which handles the *epoch-to-epoch* part within one frame. The two must be applied in the right order and with the right epochs; IOGP Guidance Note 373-25 and ISO 19111:2019 lay out the conventions, and PROJ since version 7 implements both the Helmert-with-rates and the **deformation-model** operations, with grids distributed as GeoTIFF (and, in the emerging **GGXF** — Gridded Geodetic data eXchange Format, OGC — as a general container for velocity grids and patches). The user must specify the coordinate epoch (`+t_epoch`, or a 4D coordinate with time); when it is omitted, PROJ will often apply the static part of the transformation and silently skip the time-dependent part, producing an answer that is correct only at the reference epoch.

> **Try it.** Propagate a GNSS position observed in ITRF2014 at 2023.5 to GDA2020 (ITRF2014 at 2020.0) with PROJ's Australian plate motion model, and compare with the untransformed coordinates.
>
> ```bash
> # Input: lon lat h epoch  (Canberra area), ITRF2014 at epoch 2023.5
> echo "149.1 -35.3 600.0 2023.5" | \
> cct -d 4 +proj=pipeline \
>   +step +proj=cart +ellps=GRS80 \
>   +step +proj=helmert +convention=position_vector +t_epoch=2020.0 \
>         +drx=1.50379 +dry=1.18346 +drz=1.20716 \
>   +step +inv +proj=cart +ellps=GRS80
> # (rotation rates in mas/yr from the GDA2020 Technical Manual plate motion model;
> #  check sign convention against the manual before production use)
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: the output differs from the input by about 0.19 m north and 0.06 m east — 3.5 years of the Canberra velocity (5.5 cm/yr N, 1.8 cm/yr E) — and by millimetres in height. Omitting `+t_epoch` or passing a 3D coordinate without time yields zero change: the classic silent failure.

## 38.4 Vertical land motion

Horizontal plate motion is large, steady, and almost entirely rigid; **vertical land motion (VLM)** is smaller, spatially variable, and driven by half a dozen distinct mechanisms that act at different depths and scales. For elevation data it is the more dangerous of the two, because it changes the quantity DEMs actually report, and because it is often largest exactly where elevation matters most — low-lying coasts, deltas, and cities built on soft sediment.

**Glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA)** is the viscoelastic response of the mantle to the removal of the Pleistocene ice sheets. Regions formerly under the thickest ice are still rising: around 10–13 mm/yr at the centre of the former Laurentide ice sheet near Hudson Bay, about 9–10 mm/yr at the head of the Gulf of Bothnia, several mm/yr in parts of Antarctica and Greenland; the peripheral **forebulge** that surrounded each ice sheet is collapsing, so the U.S. mid-Atlantic coast and the southern North Sea subside at 1–2 mm/yr from GIA alone. Global models such as **ICE-6G_C (VM5a)** (Peltier, Argus & Drummond 2015) predict these rates (and the accompanying geoid change, horizontal motion, and relative sea-level history) from an ice-load history and a mantle viscosity profile; they are indispensable for correcting tide gauges and GNSS, but regional differences between models reach a few mm/yr, which is why GIA is better measured than assumed where it matters ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

**Tectonic uplift and subsidence** occur at plate boundaries: interseismic strain accumulation produces slow uplift or subsidence of a few mm/yr that is released, in the opposite sense, by earthquakes ([Chapter 39](ch39-earthquakes-volcanoes-landslides.md)); long-term rock uplift in active orogens reaches 5–10 mm/yr (Southern Alps of New Zealand, Taiwan) but is largely balanced by erosion ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md)). Volcanic regions inflate and deflate at centimetres per year.

**Fluid extraction** is the largest anthropogenic driver and the fastest VLM on the planet. Pumping groundwater from a confined aquifer system lowers pore pressure, transfers load to the sediment skeleton, and compacts fine-grained interbeds — elastically (recoverable, seasonal) up to the preconsolidation stress and inelastically (permanent) beyond it (Galloway & Burbey 2011). The classic magnitudes: the San Joaquin Valley of California subsided by as much as ~9 m between 1925 and 1977 near Mendota (Poland et al. 1975), and renewed pumping during the 2012–2016 drought produced rates of 20–50 cm/yr in places; Mexico City, built on lacustrine clays, has subsided more than 10 m in its centre over the past century and continues at up to 30–50 cm/yr in parts of the basin; Jakarta's northern districts have subsided at 10–25 cm/yr, approximately 4 m since the 1970s in the worst areas; Houston–Galveston subsided up to ~3 m by the late 1970s before regulation of groundwater withdrawal; Tokyo's Kōtō ward had subsided over 4 m by the time pumping was curtailed in the 1970s, after which it stopped; Bangkok peaked at ~10 cm/yr in the early 1980s and now subsides at 1–2 cm/yr; Shanghai, Taipei, Venice, and the Po delta followed similar trajectories of rapid subsidence, regulation, and partial arrest. Oil and gas production produces the same effect — the Wilmington field at Long Beach subsided ~9 m by the 1960s until water injection — and geothermal fields, salt-solution mining, and underground coal mining add their own, sometimes abrupt, subsidence (mining troughs of metres over months; [Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)).

**Sediment compaction** in deltas and coastal plains is natural, pervasive, and accelerated by human action. Holocene deltaic sediments compact under their own weight at rates of 1–10 mm/yr; levees that cut off sediment supply, drainage that lowers the water table and oxidizes peat, and extraction from shallow aquifers push rates to 10–30 mm/yr. The Mississippi delta is the type example: Dixon et al. (2006) found a 2002–2005 average of ~5.6 mm/yr across New Orleans with InSAR, with some areas exceeding 20 mm/yr; later work using InSAR, GNSS, and rod surface-elevation tables shows comparable or larger shallow rates in the surrounding wetlands. The Ganges–Brahmaputra, Mekong, Chao Phraya, Nile, and Yellow River deltas all subside at rates that exceed global sea-level rise, which is why relative sea-level rise in deltas is two to ten times the global mean (Shirzaei et al. 2021). **Permafrost thaw** produces subsidence of 1–3 cm/yr in thaw-affected terrain and metres where ice-rich ground collapses into thermokarst; it is seasonal (frost heave in winter, thaw settlement in summer) superimposed on a secular trend ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md)).

> **Rule of thumb.** In a coastal city on Holocene sediment, assume VLM is of the same order as sea-level rise (3–4 mm/yr) until measured, and expect order-of-magnitude larger values wherever groundwater is pumped. For any DEM older than ten years in such a setting, the accumulated VLM is likely larger than the DEM's stated vertical accuracy. The rule fails in the opposite direction on uplifting coasts (Alaska, Fennoscandia, Hudson Bay), where relative sea level is *falling* and charts become too deep rather than too shallow.

## 38.5 Measuring vertical land motion

Five techniques measure VLM, with complementary strengths; no single one suffices for a regional VLM model.

**Continuously operating GNSS (CORS)** yields absolute vertical velocities in the ITRF with uncertainties of 0.3–1 mm/yr after five to ten years of data, limited by coloured noise (flicker and random walk), equipment changes, and the seasonal loading signals that must be modelled or averaged. Robust estimators such as **MIDAS** (Blewitt et al. 2016) handle undocumented steps; **Hector** and **CATS** fit explicit noise models. The limitation is spatial: a station every 50–100 km cannot resolve a subsidence bowl 5 km across, and a monument on a deep pile or a building footing may not move with the surface sediment around it — the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory, NGS, and most national agencies publish station velocities, and the user must check the monument type.

**InSAR** time series (persistent scatterer, Ferretti et al. 2001; small-baseline subset, Berardino et al. 2002) measure relative line-of-sight displacement at millions of points with mm/yr precision over years, using Sentinel-1's 6–12 day repeat since 2014 and, from 2025, NISAR's L-band ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)). Limitations are fundamental: the measurement is *relative* to a reference point or area within the scene and must be tied to CORS to become absolute; it is along the radar line of sight (roughly 30–45° from vertical), so the vertical component must be separated from horizontal using ascending and descending geometries or an assumption; it fails where the surface decorrelates (vegetation, snow, water, rapid change); and atmospheric delay, orbit errors, and unwrapping mistakes introduce long-wavelength errors of mm/yr over tens of kilometres that are indistinguishable from GIA-scale signals without external control. European Ground Motion Service (EGMS) products provide calibrated, Europe-wide InSAR velocities; similar national services exist in Norway, Germany, Italy, and elsewhere.

**Repeat spirit levelling** remains the most precise relative height measurement (0.3–1 mm per √km for first-order work) and the only one with records back to the nineteenth century; comparing historical levelling to modern GNSS-on-benchmarks is how pre-satellite subsidence histories are reconstructed (Mexico City, Tokyo, Houston). Its limitations are cost, sparse coverage, dependence on the stability of the starting benchmark, and systematic errors accumulating over long lines ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)).

**Tide gauge minus satellite altimetry** gives VLM at the gauge: the gauge measures sea level relative to the land, altimetry measures it relative to the ellipsoid, and their difference trend is land motion (Wöppelmann & Marcos 2016). Uncertainties of ~1 mm/yr are achievable with the full altimetry record (1993 onward) but depend on altimeter drift calibration and on the coastal altimetry being representative of the gauge.

**Absolute gravimetry** measures the change in $g$ at a site, which combines the free-air effect of vertical motion (about −0.3 μGal per mm of uplift) with the gravitational effect of mass redistribution; ratios of gravity change to height change discriminate GIA (mass added below) from elastic or compaction signals. Repeated absolute gravity at 1–2 μGal precision yields VLM-related rates at the mm/yr level over a decade and is independent of any geodetic reference frame — a valuable check on ITRF scale and origin drift ([Chapter 24](ch24-gravity-magnetics-geophysics.md)).

| Technique | Quantity | Precision (rate) | Spatial character | Reference | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CORS GNSS | 3D absolute velocity | 0.3–1 mm/yr (≥ 5 yr) | Points, 10–100 km apart | ITRF | Monument representativeness |
| InSAR PS/SBAS | LOS relative displacement | 1–2 mm/yr | Dense, 10–100 m, scene-wide | Local reference area | Decorrelation, atmosphere, LOS |
| Repeat levelling | Relative height difference | 0.3–1 mm/√km | Lines | Starting benchmark | Cost; stability of origin |
| Tide gauge − altimetry | Vertical rate at gauge | ~1 mm/yr (25+ yr) | Points (coastal) | Altimeter frame | Altimeter drift; coastal retrieval |
| Absolute gravity | Δg (→ Δh with assumption) | ~1 mm/yr (10 yr) | Points | Physical standard | Mass/height ambiguity; cost |

## 38.6 Consequences for DEMs

The consequences for elevation data follow from a simple fact: a DEM is tied to the ground through control (benchmarks, CORS, GNSS-derived heights on a geoid model), and when the ground under the control moves, the DEM's relationship to the datum decays even though not a single pixel changes.

**Benchmark decay.** A published orthometric height is the height at the time of the levelling adjustment. In a region subsiding at 8 mm/yr, a benchmark levelled for the NAVD 88 general adjustment (observations up to 1991) is ~27 cm too high by 2025. If a lidar survey in 2025 is controlled to that benchmark's published height, the entire DEM is biased by 27 cm *relative to the current geoid and sea level* while agreeing perfectly with the control it was given — and will pass an accuracy assessment against checkpoints levelled from the same benchmark. The NGS datasheet's adjustment date, the regional VLM rate, and the question "has this benchmark been re-observed since?" are the first three things to check in subsiding terrain.

**Control that moved between campaigns.** Two lidar campaigns ten years apart, each controlled to benchmarks current at the time, will show the VLM between them as apparent surface change — correctly, if the control was current, and incorrectly if one campaign used stale control. If both used the *same* stale published heights, the VLM is invisible and the surfaces appear stable while both have sunk. The only robust approach is to tie each campaign to a frame that is itself monitored (CORS in ITRF with velocities applied, or GNSS-on-benchmark observations at campaign time) and to record the epoch of the control heights in the survey report.

**"Stable" ground that isn't.** Change-detection workflows co-register epochs over "stable terrain" — bedrock, roads, parking lots ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)). In a subsiding basin the roads subside too; registering to them removes the regional VLM and reports only the relative pattern. That may be exactly what a geomorphologist wants (local erosion) and exactly wrong for a flood-risk analyst (absolute elevation above sea level). State which was done.

**Relative versus absolute sea-level rise.** For any elevation-based flood or inundation analysis, the relevant quantity is *relative* sea-level rise: global or regional absolute rise (≈ 3–4 mm/yr recently, accelerating) *plus* local land subsidence (or minus uplift). In New Orleans, Jakarta, Bangkok, Manila, and the Mekong delta, the land term dominates. A DEM used for a 2100 inundation map must either be propagated forward with a VLM model or carry an explicit statement that it is not, and the sea-level scenario must be expressed relative to the same land epoch (Gesch 2018; [Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md), [Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)).

**Ellipsoidal versus orthometric consequences.** Plate motion and GIA also move the geoid, slowly (GIA geoid rise in Hudson Bay ≈ 1 mm/yr), and change ellipsoidal heights directly. A geoid model has an implicit epoch too; the modernized NSRS will publish a time-dependent geopotential model (NAPGD2022 with a GIA rate component) precisely so that $H = h - N$ can be evaluated at a consistent epoch (NOAA TR NOS NGS 64).

## 38.7 The Katrina lesson

Hurricane Katrina made landfall on 29 August 2005, and the failure of the New Orleans hurricane-protection system — overtopping and breaching of levees and floodwalls — flooded about 80 % of the city. Among the many causes documented by the Interagency Performance Evaluation Task Force (IPET 2009) was a geodetic one: portions of the system were lower than their design elevations, by amounts of roughly 0.3–0.6 m in places, because they had been built to benchmarks whose published heights no longer described the ground. The vertical control of the region had been established and adjusted at various times from the 1950s onward on NGVD 29 and later NAVD 88, and the region subsides — Dixon et al. (2006) measured ~5.6 mm/yr on average from 2002–2005 InSAR, with local rates several times higher, and earlier levelling implied comparable long-term rates. A benchmark heighted in 1960 and used unchanged in a 1990s design had lost 15–25 cm; some structures were also referenced to local "mean sea level" conventions that had themselves drifted relative to the national datum. The structures matched their drawings; the drawings did not match the sea.

The response was a vertical-control program that is a model for subsiding regions. NGS, with Louisiana agencies, established GPS-derived heights on CORS and on a network of benchmarks, published them as **NAVD 88 (2004.65)** — the first widespread use of an epoch tag on orthometric heights in the United States — and institutionalized periodic re-observation; USACE adopted the new control and raised or rebuilt structures to it; and the episode drove NGS's decision that the modernized NSRS would carry time-dependent coordinates and a velocity model everywhere, not just horizontally. For the DEM practitioner the lesson is operational: in any area with known VLM, control heights are observations with a date, not constants, and a survey report that does not state the epoch of its vertical control has not described its vertical datum.

> **Case file.** *Houston–Galveston.* Up to ~3 m of groundwater-withdrawal subsidence between 1906 and 1979 moved the Brownwood subdivision in Baytown below the Galveston Bay high-water line; it was abandoned after Hurricane Alicia (1983) and is now a nature centre (Coplin & Galloway 1999). The 1906 and 1979 topographic maps of the same quadrangle differ by metres at the coast, with no erosion involved.

## Then & now

The first continental datums were explicitly static. **NAD27** ⟨H⟩ fixed the coordinates of Meades Ranch, Kansas, and the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid, and propagated outward by triangulation; ED50 did the same for western Europe after the Second World War. Neither had an epoch because nothing in their observations could detect motion. The transition began with satellite geodesy: Transit Doppler in the 1970s gave WGS 72 and then **WGS 84** (1984 ⟨H⟩) as global geocentric frames at the metre level; NAD83 (1986) was computed to be geocentric using Doppler and VLBI but inherited a ~2 m offset from the later, better-determined geocentre, which is why NAD83 and ITRF differ by metres today. The **ITRF series** (from ITRF88, through ITRF2000, 2005, 2008, 2014, to ITRF2020) added velocities, then post-seismic models, then seasonal terms, each release improving origin and scale stability toward the current ~1 mm and 0.1 mm/yr level (Altamimi et al. 2016, 2023).

National agencies responded in sequence. Europe froze ETRS89 to the Eurasian plate (1989); the United States re-realized NAD83 with GPS state by state (HARN, 1990s), then with CORS (1998, 2007, 2011) while HTDP (1992 onward) handled the time dependence; New Zealand adopted the first fully semi-dynamic national datum (NZGD2000, with a deformation model from the outset); Japan introduced semi-dynamic correction in 2010 and re-realized its datum after Tōhoku in 2011; Australia moved from GDA94 to GDA2020 and simultaneously introduced the dynamic ATRF (2017–2020). The modernized U.S. NSRS — four plate-fixed terrestrial frames (NATRF2022 and siblings), the IFVM, a new geopotential datum NAPGD2022 with a time-dependent geoid, and the retirement of passive benchmarks as the primary access to the datum — is the fully kinematic endpoint of the trajectory (NOAA TR NOS NGS 62, 64, 67); its release has slipped from 2022 to the latter half of the decade. ISO 19111 caught up with practice in 2019 by defining dynamic datums and coordinate epochs, and PROJ (version 7, 2020) made time-dependent transformation available in open-source software for the first time at production quality.

The vertical story lags the horizontal by about twenty years: levelling-based datums (NGVD 29, NAVD 88, AHD, ODN) have no epoch and no velocity model, and VLM was handled by re-levelling when someone noticed. The modernized NSRS and the European Ground Motion Service are the first systematic attempts to give heights the time-dependence that horizontal coordinates acquired in the 1990s.

## Mathematics

**Rigid-plate velocity.** For a plate rotating about an Euler vector $\boldsymbol\omega = (\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z)$ (rad/yr, geocentric Cartesian), a point at $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$ has velocity

$$
\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_y z - \omega_z y \\ \omega_z x - \omega_x z \\ \omega_x y - \omega_y x \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The Euler pole is at latitude $\arctan\!\big(\omega_z / \sqrt{\omega_x^2 + \omega_y^2}\big)$, longitude $\arctan 2(\omega_y, \omega_x)$, with rate $|\boldsymbol\omega|$; a point at angular distance $\Delta$ from the pole moves at speed $|\boldsymbol\omega|\,R \sin\Delta$. Local north and east components follow from projection onto the local unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf n} = (-\sin\phi\cos\lambda, -\sin\phi\sin\lambda, \cos\phi)$ and $\hat{\mathbf e} = (-\sin\lambda, \cos\lambda, 0)$. Rigid rotation has no radial component.

**14-parameter Helmert transformation.** Between frames A and B, with translation $\mathbf{T}$, scale $D$ (ppb, dimensionless after scaling), and small rotations $\mathbf{R}$ (rad), all linear in time:

$$
\mathbf{x}_B(t) = \mathbf{T}(t) + \big(1 + D(t)\big)\,\mathbf{x}_A(t) + \mathbf{R}(t)\,\mathbf{x}_A(t),\qquad
\mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -R_z & R_y \\ R_z & 0 & -R_x \\ -R_y & R_x & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

where each parameter $P(t) = P(t_0) + \dot P\,(t - t_0)$. The rotation sign convention (position vector versus coordinate frame) differs between IERS and some national publications and reverses the sign of $\mathbf{R}$; mixing conventions produces metre-level errors at continental scale. Velocities transform as $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_B = \dot{\mathbf{T}} + \dot D\,\mathbf{x}_A + \dot{\mathbf{R}}\,\mathbf{x}_A + (1 + D)\dot{\mathbf{x}}_A + \mathbf{R}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_A$, of which the last two terms are negligible in practice.

**Epoch propagation.** Within one frame,

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t_0) + \mathbf{v}\,(t - t_0) + \sum_k \mathbf{d}_k\,\mathcal{H}(t - t_k) + \sum_k \mathbf{p}_k(t - t_k),
$$

where $\mathbf{v}$ is the secular velocity (from the plate model or an interpolated velocity grid), $\mathbf{d}_k$ coseismic displacements applied with the Heaviside step $\mathcal{H}$ at event times $t_k$, and $\mathbf{p}_k$ post-seismic decay functions (ITRF2014 uses sums of logarithmic and exponential terms, $A\log(1 + \Delta t/\tau)$ and $A(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau})$). A complete transformation from frame A at $t_{\text{obs}}$ to frame B at $t_{\text{ref}}$ is: propagate in A from $t_{\text{obs}}$ to $t_{\text{ref}}$ (or to the Helmert reference epoch), apply the Helmert at that epoch, and if necessary propagate in B. Propagating in the wrong frame uses the wrong velocity (plate-fixed frames have near-zero velocities; global frames have plate velocities) and the error is the full plate motion times the elapsed time.

**Interpolating velocity grids.** Deformation models are regular grids of $(v_E, v_N, v_U)$; bilinear interpolation is standard and adequate where the field is smooth, but near faults, patch boundaries, and subsidence-bowl edges the gradient is large and the grid spacing (often 0.05°–0.1°, i.e. 5–10 km) under-resolves it. The interpolation error for a field with second derivative $f''$ and spacing $\Delta$ is of order $f''\Delta^2/8$; for a subsidence bowl with 10 cm/yr peak over 5 km radius sampled at 5 km, this is centimetres per year. The modern practice (NZGD2000, IFVM) is nested grids with finer spacing where gradients are high.

## Validation & uncertainty

**Where the errors come from.** (1) Frame-realization error: the Helmert parameters between two realizations carry uncertainties of a few mm and ~0.1 mm/yr, negligible for DEM work. (2) Epoch error: omitting or mis-stating the coordinate epoch costs $|\mathbf{v}|\,\Delta t$ — 7 cm/yr × Δt in Australia, 2.5 cm/yr × Δt in Europe — horizontally, and the vertical velocity times Δt vertically. (3) Velocity-model error: plate models reproduce stable-interior velocities to ~1 mm/yr; intraplate gridded models are good to 1–3 mm/yr where data are dense and far worse in gaps; vertical velocity models are typically uncertain by 1–2 mm/yr and locally much more. (4) Control decay: published heights decay at the local VLM rate from their adjustment date. (5) InSAR-derived VLM: relative to the reference area, with long-wavelength errors of 1–2 mm/yr across a 100 km scene, LOS-to-vertical projection errors where horizontal motion is non-negligible, and complete loss over decorrelating surfaces.

**How to test.** For horizontal epoch consistency: transform the published coordinates of three or more CORS through your pipeline to the observation epoch and compare with the stations' own ITRF time series; residuals should be centimetres, not decimetres, and show no trend with distance or date. For vertical control: observe GNSS on the project benchmarks at campaign time, convert to orthometric with the current geoid model, and compare with published heights; a consistent offset in the sense of the regional VLM, growing with benchmark age, confirms decay. For InSAR VLM: compare with every CORS in the scene after projecting the GNSS velocity into the LOS; the RMS difference is the InSAR rate uncertainty at points, and the spatial pattern of the difference is the long-wavelength error. For a DEM in a subsiding area: compute the DEM's offset against GNSS checkpoints observed *now* and reduced to the current epoch, separately from its offset against the control it was built to; the difference between the two offsets is the accumulated VLM since acquisition.

**What to report.** Frame and realization, coordinate epoch, deformation-model name and version, Helmert convention used, and software version; for vertical control, the epoch of the heights (e.g. "NAVD 88 (GEOID18), control observed 2023-04, published heights dated 1991 adjustment, VLM-corrected using NGS velocity X") and the VLM rate and source applied; for change products, whether co-registration removed regional VLM.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Vertical error of a 2023 airborne lidar DEM controlled to 1991-adjusted NAVD 88 benchmarks, Mississippi delta.
>
> | Component | Magnitude | Type |
> |---|---|---|
> | Lidar RMSE_V (validated against the same benchmarks) | 6 cm (1σ) | Random |
> | Geoid model error (GEOID18, coastal LA) | 2–4 cm | Systematic, smooth |
> | Benchmark decay, 32 yr × (6 ± 3) mm/yr | 19 ± 10 cm (DEM too high) | Systematic |
> | Differential subsidence between benchmarks (network distortion) | 3–8 cm | Systematic, spatially variable |
> | Monument instability (shallow rods in peat) | 2–10 cm | Systematic, local |
> | **Net** | **≈ 20–25 cm bias, ~10 cm random** | |
>
> The lidar's own error is the smallest term. The DEM passes its accuracy assessment and is a quarter of a metre too high relative to current sea level.

> **Worked example.** *Katrina-era benchmark.* A benchmark levelled in 1960 and adjusted in NGVD 29 at 1.52 m is subsequently readjusted to NAVD 88 in 1991 (height 1.44 m, after datum conversion). If the site subsides at 8 mm/yr, the physical mark was at (NAVD 88-equivalent) 1.44 + 0.008 × (1991 − 1960) ≈ 1.69 m in 1960 and at 1.44 − 0.008 × (2005 − 1991) ≈ 1.33 m in 2005 — the published 1.44 m is 11 cm too high in 2005, and a levee crest designed in 1965 as "4.50 m above the mark" would be, by 2005, 0.008 × 40 = 0.32 m lower relative to sea level than the design assumed, before any settlement of the embankment itself. Epoch-tagged control — NAVD 88 (2004.65) — makes the decay visible and correctable; a datasheet with a height and no date does not.

## Software

**Open source:** **PROJ ≥ 7** (14-parameter Helmert with `+t_epoch`, `deformation` operation with GeoTIFF/GGXF grids, `cct` for 4D points; caveat: the time-dependent part is silently skipped without an epoch); **pyproj** (same via `Transformer`, 4D arrays); **HTDP** (NGS; Fortran source distributed; plate rotations, intraplate velocities, earthquake models for the U.S.; caveat: being superseded by IFVM and will not be maintained indefinitely); **GMT** (`velo` for velocity-field plots, `grdmath`, `sample1d`; `gmtregress` for time series); **MIDAS** (Blewitt et al. 2016; robust station velocities); **Hector** (Bos et al. 2013; noise-model velocity estimation for GNSS and tide-gauge series); **MintPy**, **ISCE2**, **LiCSBAS** (InSAR time series; MintPy's reference-point and GNSS-tie options are essential for VLM); **ICE-6G** model grids (freely distributed by Peltier's group); **Geoscience Australia's GDA2020 tools** and **LINZ's NZGD2000 deformation model** with Python implementations.

**Free but closed:** NGS **OPUS** (returns NAD83(2011) epoch 2010.00 and ITRF coordinates at observation epoch, with the HTDP transformation applied internally — read the report); **European Ground Motion Service** data (free; a product, not software).

**Commercial:** **Trimble Business Center** and **Leica Infinity** (time-dependent datum support including HTDP, NZGD2000, GDA2020/ATRF; caveat: the model version used is not always written to reports); **GAMMA** and **SARscape** (InSAR time series; caveat: reference-point conventions differ from MintPy).

## Pitfalls

- **Mixing ITRF-realized GNSS heights with a plate-fixed national datum without epoch propagation** → PPP/RTK services deliver ITRF "now"; the datum is at 2010.0 or 2020.0 → always carry an epoch; test against CORS published in both frames.
- **Assuming "WGS 84" is one thing** → the label hides realization and epoch → ask for the realization (G1150, G1762, G2139) and the epoch; treat bare "WGS 84" as ±1–2 m.
- **Using a benchmark published decades ago without a VLM check** → datasheets show heights without expiry → read the adjustment date, look up regional VLM, re-observe with GNSS.
- **Attributing subsidence to sea-level rise or vice versa** → tide gauges measure the sum → separate with GNSS at the gauge or altimetry differencing before drawing conclusions.
- **Omitting `+t_epoch`** → PROJ applies the static part only → check that a 4D test coordinate moves by the expected amount.
- **Applying the Helmert in the wrong rotation convention** → position-vector vs. coordinate-frame → verify against a published test point.
- **Propagating in the wrong frame** → using ITRF velocities to propagate NAD83 coordinates (or vice versa) → propagation belongs in the frame whose velocities you have.
- **Trusting InSAR velocities as absolute** → they are relative to a reference area that may move → tie to CORS; report the reference.
- **Using a CORS on a deep-piled structure to represent surface subsidence** → the monument is decoupled from the sediment → check monument metadata; compare with shallow marks and InSAR.
- **Co-registering change epochs on "stable" roads in a subsiding basin** → removes the regional VLM silently → state whether co-registration removed a regional component; keep an absolute tie.
- **Interpolating a coarse velocity grid across a fault or bowl edge** → bilinear smoothing of a sharp gradient → check grid spacing against the feature scale; prefer nested grids.
- **Ignoring the geoid's own time dependence in GIA regions** → $N$ changes ~1 mm/yr in Hudson Bay/Fennoscandia → use the epoch-aware geopotential model where available.

## Key takeaways

- Coordinates have velocities; a datum is a frame plus an epoch plus a deformation model, and all three must be stated.
- Plate-fixed datums trade a growing offset from the global frame (ETRS89 ≈ 0.85 m, GDA94 ≈ 1.8 m at replacement) for locally constant coordinates; dynamic frames trade constant coordinates for honesty.
- The 14-parameter Helmert handles frame-to-frame; epoch propagation handles time; order and frame matter at the decimetre level.
- Vertical land motion is often the largest term in coastal elevation change: GIA at mm/yr, compaction at mm–cm/yr, pumping at cm–dm/yr. Measure it; do not assume it away.
- Published heights decay from their adjustment date; a DEM controlled to stale benchmarks passes its accuracy test and is still wrong relative to the sea.
- InSAR gives the pattern, CORS gives the absolute rate, levelling gives the history, gravity gives the independent check; VLM models need all of them.
- Katrina's geodetic lesson: epoch-tag vertical control (NAVD 88 (2004.65)), re-observe on a schedule, and state the control epoch in every survey report.
- For inundation and flood work, relative sea-level rise (ocean plus land) is the quantity, and the DEM must be propagated to the analysis epoch or explicitly declared not to be.
