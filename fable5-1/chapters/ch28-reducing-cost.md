# Chapter 28 — Reducing cost across collection, processing, validation, and use

> **Part VI — Planning and operating surveys.** The third planning chapter: the economics of elevation data — where money actually goes, which savings are real, which are false economies that reappear as errors, liability, or re-surveys, and how to reason about the value of better data before buying it.

**In this chapter.** Elevation data are expensive to collect and cheap to copy, and most of the lifecycle cost is decided in the first week of planning. You will be able to decompose the cost of a survey into mobilization, platform hours, personnel, processing labor, validation, storage and egress, and licensing, and recognise which terms dominate for airborne lidar, multibeam, UAV, and satellite products; apply value-of-information reasoning to decide how much accuracy is worth buying, with the National Enhanced Elevation Assessment and the 3D Nation study as worked evidence; find real savings in collection (right-sizing, multi-sensor missions, pooled acquisitions, opportunistic and crowdsourced data, reuse of open archives), processing (automation, cloud-native formats, reproducible open pipelines), validation (national control, satellite altimetry checkpoints, stable terrain, designed-in crosslines, sampling strategies), and use (overviews, tiling, APIs, published intermediates); price the cost of errors from groundings to failed acceptance; set refresh cycles by change detection rather than calendar; account for energy; and identify the things that must never be cut.

## 28.1 Cost structure

The cost of an elevation dataset is not proportional to its area or its line kilometres. It is dominated by fixed and quasi-fixed terms, and understanding which term dominates for a given method is the first step in reducing it honestly.

**Mobilization.** Getting a calibrated sensor, platform, and crew to the site: ferry flights or vessel transits, permits, installation and calibration (boresight flights, patch tests), base-station and tide-gauge establishment, standby. For a small airborne project mobilization can exceed half the total; for a vessel survey in a remote region, transit and demobilization alone can be weeks of day rate. Mobilization is why survey cost per unit area falls steeply with project size and why pooled acquisitions (Section 28.2) work.

**Platform hours.** Aircraft, vessel, and vehicle time, with fuel, crew, insurance, and maintenance. Fixed-wing survey aircraft are billed by the flight hour; survey vessels by the day; the platform clock runs during transit, turns, weather holds, and calibration as well as on-line, so the on-line fraction (commonly 50–70 % for airborne work and lower for small-boat hydrography in marginal weather) is a key efficiency metric.

**Personnel.** Field crews, pilots, hydrographers, processing analysts, QA reviewers, project managers. In hydrography, **manual data editing has historically been the largest single labor item in processing** — the motivation behind CUBE (Calder & Mayer 2003) was precisely that hand-cleaning of multibeam soundings scaled with data volume and had become the bottleneck as sounding rates grew by orders of magnitude. In lidar the equivalent is manual classification review and breakline collection; in photogrammetry, GCP measurement and manual editing of the DSM.

**Validation.** Checkpoint surveys, crosslines, reference surfaces, independent QA review, and acceptance testing: typically 5–10 % of acquisition cost when designed in ([Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md)), and far more when retrofitted.

**Storage, compute, and egress.** Raw lidar at 8–20 points m$^{-2}$ runs 1–3 GB km$^{-2}$ in LAZ with waveforms excluded; raw multibeam with water column can reach tens of gigabytes per survey day; national programs hold petabytes. Object storage is approximately US$0.02 per GB-month at standard tiers (2024 list prices for major providers; cold tiers an order of magnitude less), which makes storage cheap relative to collection — but **egress** charges of roughly US$0.05–0.09 per GB mean that a popular 100 TB dataset downloaded in full a hundred times costs more to serve than to store for years. Compute for classification, gridding, and InSAR processing is bursty and benefits from spot pricing; GPU time for learned classifiers adds a line item that did not exist a decade ago.

**Licensing.** Software seats (hydrographic and lidar suites are priced per seat per year, in the thousands to tens of thousands of dollars), data licences for commercial satellite stereo or DEMs (priced per km², with restrictions on derivatives and redistribution), and the legal cost of restrictive terms that forbid the intended use ([Chapter 68](ch68-legal-issues.md)).

| Method | Dominant cost terms | Scales mainly with | Typical false economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airborne lidar (QL2/QL1) | mobilization, flight hours, classification labor | area (weakly), terrain/vegetation complexity | fewer cross-strips, fewer checkpoints, leaf-on season |
| Shallow-water MBES | vessel day rate, editing labor, weather standby | line km at shallowest depth, weather | skipping patch test, no crosslines, 100 % coverage replaced by "object detection" without demonstration |
| UAV lidar/SfM | personnel days, GCP survey, processing | number of sites, access | too few GCPs, no checkpoints, self-calibration only |
| Satellite stereo/InSAR DEM | data licence, processing compute, control for co-registration | area (strongly, by licence) | no independent control; accepting vendor accuracy claims |
| Satellite-derived bathymetry | imagery (often free), calibration soundings | number of images and calibration sites | no in-situ calibration; no turbidity screening |
| Crowdsourced bathymetry | aggregation, QC, vessel offset metadata | number of contributors | treating uncalibrated soundings as surveyed depths |

## 28.2 Collection

### 28.2.1 Right-sizing resolution to the requirement

The largest avoidable collection cost is specifying more than the use needs — or, more insidiously, specifying less and paying twice. [Chapter 3](ch03-fitness-for-use.md) and [Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md) give the method: derive the required density, vertical accuracy, and effective resolution from the decision the data will support, then buy that. Density beyond what the ground-detection and feature-detection requirements need adds storage and processing cost without adding information in the product; accuracy beyond the requirement adds control and calibration cost. But the asymmetry matters: upgrading later costs a full re-collection, whereas collecting once at a slightly higher specification than today's use needs costs a marginal increase in flight hours and storage. The NEEA's conclusion that QL2 was the best national compromise rested on exactly this reasoning — the incremental cost from QL3 to QL2 was small relative to the additional benefits unlocked, while QL1 added cost that most uses could not yet justify (Dewberry 2012). The rule that emerges: specify to the most demanding *credible* use over the data's expected lifetime, not to the first requester.

### 28.2.2 Multi-sensor single missions

A platform that is already mobilized can carry more sensors for a small marginal cost: lidar plus a nadir camera (orthoimagery for classification QC and a second product), topobathy lidar plus hyperspectral for benthic habitat, MBES plus backscatter plus sub-bottom profiler plus water-column logging, UAV lidar plus RGB plus multispectral. The constraints are payload mass and power, conflicting optimal conditions (lidar wants leaf-off; imagery wants leaves and high sun; bathymetric lidar wants clear water; backscatter wants consistent settings), and the processing cost of the extra streams, which is easy to underestimate. Collect the extra sensor when its processing is funded; otherwise archive the raw data with metadata and defer processing — raw data are cheap to keep and impossible to re-create.

### 28.2.3 Shared and pooled acquisitions

Because mobilization dominates, the cost per km² of a block drops when adjacent needs are combined. The USGS 3DEP partnership model — the Broad Agency Announcement through which federal, state, local, and tribal partners co-fund lidar collections to a common specification, with USGS holding and distributing the result — has been the mechanism by which the United States approached national QL2 coverage; the same logic drives state-level lidar consortia, European national programs, and hydrographic partnerships such as the sharing of survey priorities among port authorities, navies, and hydrographic offices. The conditions for pooling to work: one specification (the LBS was written to make this possible), one datum and epoch, one metadata standard, open delivery, and a governance mechanism for priorities. The cost of pooling is schedule inflexibility and the risk that the common specification serves nobody's most demanding use; the remedy is to allow partners to buy up (higher density, extra deliverables) within the shared mobilization.

### 28.2.4 Break-evens between satellite, airborne, and ground

The break-even between methods depends on area, required accuracy, and access. Global satellite DEMs (Copernicus DEM, ALOS World 3D, SRTM-derived products) are free and adequate for regional analyses at 30 m with vertical errors of metres ([Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)); commercial satellite stereo at 0.3–0.5 m GSD yields DSMs of 1–2 m accuracy at a licence cost that becomes competitive with airborne lidar only for remote or inaccessible areas or where a DSM rather than a DTM is sufficient; airborne lidar wins for any area above a few tens of km² where decimetre accuracy and a DTM under canopy are needed; UAVs win below a few km² and for repeat surveys of small sites; terrestrial scanning and GNSS survey win for engineering tolerances over hectares. For bathymetry the equivalent ladder is satellite-derived bathymetry (free imagery, 10–20 % of depth accuracy, clear water only) → bathymetric lidar (fast, 1.5–3 Secchi depths) → MBES (full coverage, any depth, slow and expensive in the shallows) ([Chapter 2](ch02-uses-bathymetry.md), [Chapter 23](ch23-satellite-derived-bathymetry.md)). The planning question is which rung meets the requirement at lowest lifecycle cost, with the explicit caveat that a product on a lower rung often needs a higher-rung product for calibration and validation.

### 28.2.5 Opportunistic and crowdsourced data

**Crowdsourced bathymetry (CSB)** under IHO guidance B-12 (Edition 3.0.0, 2023) collects depths from vessels' own echo sounders and GNSS and aggregates them through trusted nodes to the IHO Data Centre for Digital Bathymetry; the data cost almost nothing to collect, cover routes that surveys do not, and are uncalibrated for draft, sound speed, tide, and transducer offset unless the contributor supplies the metadata, so their uncertainty is decimetres to metres and must be estimated per contributor ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md)). They are valuable for reconnaissance, for change detection in unsurveyed areas, and for filling GEBCO — the Seabed 2030 project's cost estimate for mapping the remaining ocean conventionally (Mayer et al. 2018 put the order of magnitude at billions of US dollars and hundreds of ship-years) is the reason CSB and fleet data matter. **Vehicle fleets** with lidar and cameras, **smartphones** with barometers and GNSS, **fishing-vessel** AIS and sounders, and **satellite altimetry** over inland water are all opportunistic elevation sources with the same economics: near-zero marginal collection cost, high aggregation and QC cost, and uncertainty that must be characterised rather than assumed. Reuse of **open archives** — OpenTopography, the 3DEP collection on public cloud, national lidar portals, Copernicus, EMODnet Bathymetry, NOAA NCEI's BAG archive — is the cheapest collection of all; before any new survey, establish what exists, its epoch, datum, and accuracy, and whether the requirement is a new survey or a validation of the old one ([Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md), [Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md)).

> **Rule of thumb.** Cost per km² of airborne lidar falls roughly as the inverse square root of block area up to the size at which a single mobilization is fully used (a few thousand km²), then flattens; below about 100 km² the per-area cost of crewed airborne collection rises steeply and UAVs or pooling with a neighbour become the economic answer. Indicative, from program experience; actual rates depend on terrain, airspace, and market conditions, and USGS publishes periodic cost guidance for 3DEP partners.

## 28.3 Processing

### 28.3.1 Automation where it is proven

Automated processing reduces labor where the algorithm's behaviour is characterised and its failure modes are detectable. **CUBE** and its successor **CHRT** (Calder & Mayer 2003; Calder & Rice 2017) replaced sounding-by-sounding editing with hypothesis-based gridding carrying an uncertainty per node; hydrographic offices that adopted them report that analyst time moved from cleaning to reviewing flagged hypotheses, which is both cheaper and better documented. In lidar, ground classification by progressive TIN densification or cloth simulation handles most terrain, and learned classifiers ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md), [Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md)) now reach per-point accuracies in the high 90s on benchmark data for the major classes; the cost moves to validating the classifier on this project's data and to the residual manual review of the difficult 5 %. The false economy is automation without the review: the per-point accuracy figure hides spatially clustered failures (a whole embankment classed as vegetation, a whole plume classed as ground) that a sampled visual review catches in minutes.

### 28.3.2 Cloud-native formats and reproducible pipelines

**Cloud-optimised GeoTIFF (COG)**, **COPC** (cloud-optimised point cloud, LAZ with an octree index), and **Zarr** ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)) let a reader fetch only the tiles, levels, or chunks it needs over HTTP range requests, so that a user who wants one watershed from a national DEM moves megabytes, not terabytes, and the publisher pays egress on what was used. The savings are real on both sides: no download-then-subset step, no local mirror, no duplicated derived copies, and compute that can run next to the data. **STAC** catalogues ([Chapter 51](ch51-finding-data.md)) make the data discoverable without a bespoke portal. **Reproducible pipelines** — PDAL and GDAL pipelines as JSON, MB-System and GMT scripts, containerised environments, pinned versions, declared parameters ([Chapter 29](ch29-processing-pipelines.md)) — reduce cost in the way that matters most: when the geoid model changes, a classification rule is improved, or an error is found, the whole product can be regenerated from raw with a command rather than a project. The cost of not having them is paid at every reprocessing, and reprocessing is the normal lifecycle of elevation data.

### 28.3.3 Open-source tooling versus seat licences

Open-source tools now cover the full lidar chain (PDAL, LAStools' open components, lidR, CloudCompare, Entwine, OpenDroneMap), most of photogrammetry (OpenDroneMap, MicMac, COLMAP, the Ames Stereo Pipeline for satellite stereo), the full sonar chain in research settings (MB-System, Kluster), InSAR (ISCE, GMTSAR, SNAP), and all of gridding and analysis (GDAL, GMT, xdem, QGIS). The direct saving is the per-seat licence; the larger saving is the ability to run at any scale on any machine without licence servers, and to inspect and fix the algorithm. The honest caveats: open tools often lack the integrated editing interfaces that make a hydrographic or lidar analyst productive in a commercial suite, support is community-based, and a production hydrographic office certifying charts under S-44 will usually keep a commercial suite for the editing and deliverable steps while using open tools for automation and QC around it. **GPU costs** for learned methods are real but bounded: training a point-cloud classifier for a regional program is hours to days on a single modern GPU; inference is cheap; the expensive item is labelled training data, which is why shared benchmark datasets ([Appendix H](../appendices/appendix-h-datasets.md)) reduce cost for everyone.

> **Try it.** *Cost of serving a national DEM: full download versus cloud-native access.* A 1 m DEM of a 250,000 km² region as float32 is 1 TB uncompressed, about 400 GB as a COG with DEFLATE. Compare the egress for 1,000 users each needing a 50 km² watershed.
>
> ```python
> tb = 400               # GB, compressed COG
> users, need_km2, total_km2 = 1000, 50, 250_000
> egress_rate = 0.09     # USD per GB (approximate list price)
> full_download = users * tb * egress_rate
> cog_access = users * tb * (need_km2 / total_km2) * 1.5 * egress_rate  # 1.5× for tile overfetch
> print(f"full download: ${full_download:,.0f}; COG range requests: ${cog_access:,.0f}")
> ```
>
> Expected output: `full download: $36,000; COG range requests: $11`. The ratio is the ratio of what was needed to what was shipped; it is why requester-pays and cloud-native publication together make large open DEMs sustainable, and why the same dataset published only as a zipped download costs its publisher more every year.

## 28.4 Validation

Validation is where false economies concentrate, because its cost is visible and its benefit is counterfactual. The real savings are structural. **Reuse national control:** NGS datasheets and OPUS-Share positions, national benchmark networks, CORS, and tidal benchmarks ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)) are free, independently maintained, and already in the right datum; a checkpoint survey that starts from them costs a day of GNSS work per few dozen points. **ICESat-2 and GEDI as free checkpoints:** ICESat-2 ATL06/ATL08 terrain heights have decimetre-level accuracy on flat open ground and provide thousands of independent samples across any large block at zero cost ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)); they do not replace designed checkpoints — their footprint, their canopy sensitivity, and their sparse spatial pattern limit them — but they catch datum blunders and regional biases before the expensive field survey, and they are the only practical check in remote regions. **Stable-terrain co-registration** (Nuth & Kääb 2011) against an existing DEM costs compute only and resolves shifts and tilts between epochs. **Crosslines designed in** are the cheapest internal consistency test in existence. **Shared benchmark datasets and reference surfaces** — a permanent calibration site used by every project in a region, a public set of checkpoints maintained by a program — amortise one careful survey over many projects.

**Sampling strategy** is the remaining lever. Checkpoint counts are set by the confidence you need on the statistic ([Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md) gives the $1/\sqrt{2n}$ relation; [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) the full treatment): stratify by land cover and slope so that each stratum's count is sufficient for its own statistic, allocate more points to strata with higher expected variance or higher consequence (floodplains, not ridgetops), and use a **sequential** design where the first 20 points per stratum decide whether more are needed — if the RMSE is a third of the tolerance after 20, more points buy nothing; if it is near the tolerance, the next 20 are worth their cost. Sampling cost is minimised not by fewer points but by placing them where their information is highest, which is also what the standards' distribution rules are trying to achieve.

## 28.5 Use

Most of the lifetime cost of a widely used DEM is incurred by its users, and a publisher who spends a little more at publication saves a lot in aggregate. **Overviews and tiling** (internal pyramids in COG, octree levels in COPC, multiscale Zarr groups) let viewers and analyses read at the resolution they need; a web map that must read full-resolution tiles to render a continent is a cost multiplier for everyone. **Caching and APIs** — tile services, point-query elevation services, STAC endpoints — serve common needs without each user re-deriving the same product. **"Good-enough" derived products** published alongside the primary (hillshade, slope, contours at standard intervals, a hydro-conditioned DTM, a 10 m downsample with documented resampling) prevent a thousand slightly different re-derivations, each with its own undocumented choices. **Publishing intermediates and metadata** — the classified point cloud and not only the DTM, the trajectory, the control report, the masks ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)), the lineage — is the single largest saving for downstream users, because the alternative is that each user who needs a different surface definition either re-processes from scratch or, more often, uses the wrong product. The marginal cost of publishing a classified LAZ next to a DTM is storage; the cost of not publishing it is every reprocessing that anyone ever does.

## 28.6 The cost of errors

Errors are the most expensive line item in the lifecycle because they are paid by someone other than the producer, usually later and usually with a multiplier. **Groundings:** the USS *Guardian* grounded on Tubbataha Reef in January 2013 and was cut up in place; the U.S. Navy's investigation found that a Digital Nautical Chart misplaced the reef by about 8 nmi (a coastal-scale chart that had not been updated after corrected survey data were obtained in 2011) and that the bridge team relied on GPS and the digital chart without reconciling them with other references; the salvage alone was reported at close to US$45 million, the ship was a total loss, and the United States paid about US$1.97 million in reef compensation in 2015. The cost of a correct position for one reef was a small fraction of a single survey day. **Flood maps:** errors in the DEM beneath a flood-insurance map propagate into property-level determinations; FEMA's Letter of Map Amendment process exists largely to correct them, and the aggregate cost of appeals, mispriced insurance, and litigation over flood zones is one of the principal benefits the NEEA attributed to better elevation data (Dewberry 2012). **Failed acceptance:** a lidar block that fails NVA or density is re-flown at a cost equal to the original flight hours plus a new mobilization plus a season; a hydrographic survey that fails crossline tolerance is re-run. **Hidden fitness costs** of "free" global DEMs: a hydraulic model built on a 30 m DSM with 5 m vertical errors in a city costs nothing to download and may cost a mis-sited levee or a misclassified flood zone; the data were not free, the cost was moved to the decision.

The **benefit–cost evidence** is consistent. The NEEA (Dewberry 2012) documented more than 600 mission-critical activities across federal, state, local, tribal, and private users and estimated conservative annual benefits of about US$1.2 billion rising to a potential US$13 billion for a national QL2 program, against annual program costs on the order of US$150 million for the recommended 8-year QL2 cycle — a benefit–cost ratio of about 4.7:1 for the recommended scenario. The *3D Nation Elevation Requirements and Benefits Study* (Dewberry 2022, for USGS and NOAA) extended the analysis to inland and coastal bathymetry and estimated potential annual benefits of about US$13.5 billion once a fully operational topographic-plus-bathymetric program is in place, across 1,352 identified mission-critical activities. Sugarbaker et al. (2014) translated the NEEA into the 3DEP program design. For the oceans, Mayer et al. (2018) framed the Seabed 2030 economics: the cost of completing the global map is large in absolute terms and small relative to the value of the decisions — cable routing, fisheries, hazard modelling, navigation — that depend on it.

> **Worked example.** *Value of information for a levee design (illustrative numbers).* A 10 km levee is to be set at the design flood level plus freeboard; the freeboard includes an allowance of $1.645\,\sigma_{\text{DEM}}$ for uncertainty in the ground elevation along the alignment (a one-sided 95 % bound). With a legacy 10 m DEM, $\sigma_{\text{DEM}} = 0.5$ m, so the allowance is 0.82 m; with QL2 lidar, $\sigma_{\text{DEM}} = 0.1$ m and the allowance is 0.16 m. If raising a levee crest costs on the order of US$1 million per km per metre (illustrative; real unit costs vary widely with foundation, materials, and land), the 0.66 m reduction saves about 10 km × 0.66 m × US$1 M = US$6.6 million. A QL2 lidar collection over the 200 km² corridor needed for the hydraulic model costs a small fraction of that. The expected value of the information is the saving multiplied by the probability that the design is actually built to the computed height, less the survey cost — positive by a wide margin here, and typical of why the NEEA found infrastructure design among the largest benefit categories. The same arithmetic run the other way identifies uses (a regional slope map, a viewshed) where a free 30 m DEM already captures all the value.

## 28.7 Data lifetimes, refresh cycles, and when not to re-survey

A DEM is a measurement at an epoch, and its value decays at the rate the surface changes ([Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md)). Calendar-based refresh (3DEP's nominal 8-year cycle; hydrographic re-survey intervals set by traffic and seabed mobility) is a budgeting convenience, not a measurement principle. The economic rule is to re-survey where change exceeds the product's uncertainty *and* matters to a use, and to detect that change cheaply first: Sentinel-1 coherence and Sentinel-2 imagery flag construction, landslides, and coastal change at no acquisition cost; ICESat-2 repeat tracks and CSB flag elevation and depth change along lines; building-permit and dredging records flag anthropogenic change; a post-storm UAV flight over the affected reach costs a day. Where these show stability, the old survey remains fit and the refresh money goes elsewhere; where they show change, the re-survey is targeted. Hydrographic offices formalise this as risk-based survey prioritisation (traffic, depth margin, seabed mobility, age of survey); the same logic applies to topographic programs, which have been slower to adopt it. The companion rule: when a re-survey is done, process it so that the difference from the previous epoch is itself a product ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) — the change map is often worth more than the new surface.

## 28.8 Energy and environmental cost

Surveys burn fuel and compute burns electricity, and both are increasingly reported. A twin-turboprop survey aircraft consumes on the order of 300–400 L of jet fuel per hour (approximately; type-dependent), so a 600 km² QL2 block flown in about 10 flight hours burns roughly 3,000–4,000 L, about 8–10 t of CO₂ — small per km² compared with almost any use the data serve, and an argument for flying once at the right specification rather than twice. Survey vessels are heavier: a coastal survey launch burns hundreds of litres per day, an ocean-going survey ship tens of tonnes, which is part of why Seabed 2030's remaining ocean is expensive in every currency and why uncrewed surface vessels and fleet-contributed data are attractive. Compute is modest for conventional processing and non-trivial for training large learned models and for repeated reprocessing of national archives; the practical levers are the same as for cost — process once with reproducible pipelines, use cloud-native access to avoid moving data, size compute to the task, and publish intermediates so that others do not recompute them. Report energy where a funder asks for it, and do not let it become an argument against the validation flights that keep the first survey from becoming two.

## Then & now

The economics of elevation data have inverted twice. In the era of plane-table and levelling, collection was labor and labor was the cost; the U.S. Geological Survey's topographic quadrangle program took most of a century and the national map was refreshed in decades. Photogrammetry (from the 1930s) and then satellite imagery (Landsat, 1972 ⟨H⟩) made collection per unit area cheap and moved cost to processing and to the analogue-to-digital transition; SRTM (2000) ⟨H⟩ produced a near-global DEM in eleven days and distributed it free, which set the expectation that baseline elevation is a public good. Lidar made high accuracy affordable but re-concentrated cost in mobilization and manual editing, and the NEEA (2012) and 3DEP (from 2012–2014) established, with a formal benefit–cost analysis, that a national program at QL2 was worth four to five times its cost. In hydrography, multibeam increased data volume by orders of magnitude from the 1980s and CUBE (2003) answered the resulting editing crisis; Seabed 2030 (2017) and IHO B-12 crowdsourced bathymetry (2018; Ed. 3.0.0, 2023) reframed the ocean-mapping problem as one of cost and contribution rather than technology. The current decade's inversion is on the use side: cloud-native formats (COG, 2016 onward; COPC, 2021), STAC, and public-cloud hosting of national archives (3DEP on AWS, Copernicus DEM, EMODnet) have made the marginal cost of access near zero and shifted the economic question from "can we afford to collect?" to "can we afford not to publish well?" — while learned processing has introduced GPU and labelled-data costs and energy accounting has begun to appear in tenders.

## Validation & uncertainty

Cost decisions are uncertainty decisions, and this section states how to make and defend them.

**Cost models carry uncertainty too.** A survey estimate is a sum of terms with very different variances: platform hours are well known, weather days are a distribution (use climatology, not a flat percentage), editing labor depends on terrain and data quality in ways that are hard to predict, and re-survey probability depends on QC discipline. Estimate each with a range, not a point, and carry the range into the decision; a bid that is 20 % cheaper because it omits the weather distribution is not cheaper.

**Value-of-information reasoning, explicitly.** The expected value of better data for a decision is $\text{EVI} = \mathbb{E}[\max_a U(a \mid \text{better data})] - \max_a \mathbb{E}[U(a \mid \text{current data})]$, the improvement in the expected outcome from being able to choose the action with the better information rather than without it. In practice this reduces to three questions: what decision will change if the uncertainty shrinks; by how much does the outcome improve; with what probability is the decision actually taken on the basis of the data? The levee example in Section 28.6 is this calculation; the NEEA was this calculation applied to hundreds of activities with surveyed users supplying the outcome values. Uses whose decision does not change as σ falls (a regional viewshed; a basin-scale slope class map) have EVI near zero and should use free data; uses with steep loss functions near a threshold (flood-zone boundaries, dredging volumes, navigational clearance) have large EVI and should buy accuracy.

**Where savings become errors.** Each cost cut has a predictable uncertainty consequence, and the planner should write it down: fewer checkpoints → wider confidence interval on RMSE and an undetected bias risk; no crosslines → systematic errors unobservable; longer baselines → trajectory height errors of centimetres to decimetres; leaf-on season → DTM bias under canopy; skipped patch test → depth errors at the swath edge; lossy compression → quantisation error added to every cell; automation without review → spatially clustered misclassification; reused data without re-validation → an unknown epoch and datum error. If the consequence is not acceptable, the cut is not a saving.

**Lossy compression is a measurement decision.** Storing elevation as 16-bit integers in metres quantises to 0.5 m; in centimetres it quantises to 1 cm and halves the storage of float32 without harm for most DEMs; LERC and similar lossy encoders with a stated maximum error are acceptable only if that error is added to the product's uncertainty statement and recorded in the metadata; lossy point-cloud compression that drops returns or coarsens coordinates changes the data and should not be called compression. LAZ is lossless and typical ratios are 5–10:1 for lidar; DEFLATE or ZSTD on predictor-encoded float32 GeoTIFF typically reach 2–3:1 losslessly. Take the lossless saving first; it is large and free.

**Validate reused data as if it were new.** Open data have a documented accuracy for a documented epoch and datum; the cost of reuse is the validation that confirms they still hold where you need them — a stable-terrain comparison, a sample of ICESat-2 crossings, a check of the datum transformation. The reports in [Chapter 54](ch54-evaluating-others-data.md) describe what to look for when the documentation is thin.

**What to report.** Alongside the accuracy statement: the cost of validation as a fraction of acquisition; which checks were designed in and which retrofitted; the compression and quantisation applied; the provenance and re-validation of any reused data; and, for programs, the benefit–cost basis and the refresh trigger. A dataset whose economics are documented is one whose corners can be audited.

> |---|---|---|
> | Halve checkpoints (40 → 20) | ≈ 2 days of GNSS survey | 95 % CI on RMSE widens from ±25 % to ±31 %; VVA percentile set by one point |
> | Drop cross-strips (7 % of line km) | ≈ 7 % of flight hours | Roll/pitch/height biases between lines unobservable |
> | Single base at 45 km instead of two at ≤ 20 km | one receiver and a day | Ambiguity-fix failures; 5–15 cm height excursions on far lines |
> | Fly leaf-on to use this fiscal year | schedule convenience | DTM bias of 0.1–0.5 m under canopy; voids; failed VVA |
> | Store DEM as int16 metres | 50 % of DEM storage | 0.5 m quantisation — product destroyed; use int16 cm or float32 |
> | Skip classification review | analyst days | Clustered misclassification undetected; NVA may pass while the DTM is wrong on embankments |
> | Publish DTM only, not classified LAZ | storage | Every user with a different surface definition reprocesses or uses the wrong product |

## Standards & guides

- Dewberry (2012), **National Enhanced Elevation Assessment** — the benefit–cost methodology and scenario analysis behind 3DEP; and Dewberry (2022), **3D Nation Elevation Requirements and Benefits Study** (USGS/NOAA).
- USGS **Lidar Base Specification** (2024 rev. A) and the **3DEP Broad Agency Announcement** — the common specification and partnership mechanism that make pooled acquisitions possible.
- IHO **B-12 Guidance on Crowdsourced Bathymetry**, Edition 3.0.0 (2023) — contributor metadata, trusted nodes, uncertainty handling for CSB.
- IHO **S-44** Ed. 6.1.0 (2022) and NOAA **HSSD** (2024) — the acceptance tests that define what cannot be cut in hydrography.
- U.S. OMB **Circular A-94** — guidelines for benefit–cost analysis of federal programs (discount rates, treatment of uncertainty), the framework the NEEA followed.
- ISO **19157-1:2023** (geographic information — data quality) and the **FAIR** principles (Wilkinson et al. 2016) — what publication must include for reuse to be cheap.
- OGC **Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF** (2023) and **STAC** (1.0, 2021) specifications; the **COPC** specification (2021) — cloud-native formats for low-cost access.
- Open data licences (CC-BY 4.0, ODbL, public-domain dedications) and the EU **INSPIRE** and Open Data Directive — the terms under which reuse is actually permitted.

## Pitfalls

- **Saving on control and crosslines.** The cheapest-looking cuts remove the only evidence of accuracy. → The cost reappears in a failed acceptance, a liability claim, or an undetected bias; keep validation at 5–10 % of acquisition and make crosslines line items.
- **"Free" global DEMs with hidden fitness costs.** Zero download cost is mistaken for zero cost. → State the use's accuracy requirement first; if metres of error change the decision, the free product is the expensive one.
- **Lossy compression of elevation to save bytes.** Integer metres, aggressive LERC, 8-bit hillshade-as-data. → Lossless compression first (LAZ, DEFLATE/ZSTD with predictors); any lossy step enters the uncertainty statement and the metadata.
- **Licence terms that forbid the intended derivative.** A commercial DEM bought cheaply cannot be redistributed in the flood map it was bought for. → Read derivative and redistribution clauses before purchase; prefer open data where the product must be published.
- **Mobilizing twice.** A small project flown alone, then a neighbour flown next year. → Pool through programs (3DEP BAA, state consortia, port partnerships); buy up within a shared mobilization.
- **Specifying to the first requester.** QL3 bought for a slope map, re-flown at QL2 three years later for a flood study. → Specify to the most demanding credible use over the data's lifetime.
- **Automation without review.** A 97 % per-point classifier accepted on its benchmark number. → Sample-based visual review per tile; report recall and precision on this project.
- **Publishing only the final grid.** Users reprocess from nothing or use the wrong surface. → Publish the classified point cloud, masks, trajectory, control report, and lineage; the storage is trivial.
- **Downloads instead of range requests.** A national DEM served as zipped tiles. → COG/COPC/Zarr with overviews and a STAC catalogue; requester-pays where volumes demand.
- **Calendar refresh.** Re-surveying stable terrain on schedule while a changed coast waits. → Change-detection triggers from free satellite data, CSB, and records; risk-based prioritisation.
- **Treating CSB and fleet data as surveyed depths.** Uncalibrated soundings merged into a chart grid. → Per-contributor uncertainty estimation under B-12; use for reconnaissance and change detection unless validated.

## Key takeaways

- Mobilization and labor dominate cost; line kilometres and bytes do not. Once mobilized, overlap, crosslines, calibration, and extra sensors are cheap, and cutting them saves little while removing the evidence of quality.
- Specify to the most demanding credible use over the data's lifetime and collect once; upgrading later is a full re-collection.
- Decide how much accuracy to buy with value-of-information reasoning: what decision changes, by how much, and with what probability — the NEEA and 3D Nation studies are this calculation at national scale, with benefit–cost ratios near 4–5:1.
- Pool acquisitions through common specifications and governance; reuse open archives and national control; use ICESat-2, stable terrain, and designed-in crosslines as near-free validation.
- Automate where the algorithm is characterised and keep a sampled human review; CUBE/CHRT and learned classifiers move labor from editing to reviewing, not to zero.
- Publish cloud-native (COG, COPC, Zarr), with overviews, STAC, intermediates, masks, and lineage: the publisher's marginal cost is small and the users' aggregate saving is large.
- Errors are the most expensive line item — groundings, flood-map disputes, failed acceptance, re-surveys — and they are paid by someone else, later, with a multiplier.
- Refresh by detected change, not by calendar; make the change map a product.
- Never cut: control, crosslines, calibration events, raw-data retention, lossless storage, metadata and lineage, and the licence check.
