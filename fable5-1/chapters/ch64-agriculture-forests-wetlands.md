# Chapter 64 — Agriculture, forests, wetlands, and the living surface

> **Part XIV — Domain deep dives.** Surfaces that grow, flood, breathe, and are harvested: what "elevation" means where the ground is under vegetation or water, how each sensor sees or fails to see it, and how to design acquisitions so that growth is not mistaken for ground change.

**In this chapter.** Over most of the land surface the thing a sensor sees first is alive. You will be able to build and error-budget a canopy height model from a DSM and a DTM whose errors are both unknown and correlated; state which sensors penetrate vegetation and how far (lidar, X-, C-, L-, and P-band radar, optical stereo), and why leaf-off acquisition is a specification rather than a preference; quantify the positive biases that vegetation imposes on lidar DTMs—centimeters in grass, decimeters in marsh, meters in mangrove and dense forest from spaceborne radar—and apply the correction methods that exist; recognize croplands as surfaces with seasonal canopy, tillage micro-relief, and engineered grades that demand centimeter RTK; treat peat, permafrost, and snow as surfaces that oscillate; design multi-season acquisitions around phenology and separate growth from ground change in a time series; and evaluate the spaceborne and machine-learned canopy height products (GEDI, ICESat-2, GLAD, ETH, Meta/WRI) as priors with known failure modes rather than as measurements.

## 64.1 Forests: the canopy, the ground beneath it, and what each sensor sees

A **canopy height model (CHM)** is the difference between a surface model and a terrain model, CHM = DSM − DTM, computed per cell or per lidar return as height above ground. Everything about its quality follows from how the two terms were obtained. With airborne lidar both come from the same point cloud: the DSM from the first or highest returns, the DTM from the ground-classified last returns ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md)), and the CHM's error is dominated by the DTM's, because the canopy top is sampled densely while the ground under a dense canopy is sampled by the few pulses that reach it. The DTM under forest is therefore an interpolation between sparse ground hits whose spacing grows with canopy density: at 8 pulses/m² and 95 % canopy closure, perhaps 0.4 ground returns/m² in summer broadleaf and far fewer in dense conifer or tropical forest. A CHM over such a DTM inherits meter-scale ground error on slopes where the interpolation spans ridges and gullies.

**Penetration by sensor** is the organizing fact. Small-footprint **discrete-return and full-waveform lidar** at 1064 or 1550 nm penetrates through gaps; a fraction of each pulse's energy reaches the ground wherever there is a line of sight, and that fraction falls exponentially with leaf area index. **Leaf-off** flights in deciduous forest raise ground-return rates by factors of 3–10 and are a requirement in the USGS Lidar Base Specification and most national programs precisely because the DTM under summer canopy is not fit for purpose; evergreen forest has no leaf-off season, and its DTMs are correspondingly worse. **Optical stereo** (satellite, aerial, UAV SfM; [Chapter 22](ch22-photogrammetry-sfm.md)) sees only the canopy top and produces a DSM; a DTM under forest from imagery alone does not exist, and a CHM from SfM needs a lidar DTM. **SAR** penetration depends on wavelength ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)): **X-band** (TanDEM-X, 3 cm) scatters from the upper canopy and its InSAR height sits a few meters below the canopy top; **C-band** (Sentinel-1, SRTM, 5.6 cm) penetrates somewhat further, so the SRTM "elevation" over forest is typically within the upper canopy, biased above the ground by roughly half to two-thirds of the canopy height; **L-band** (ALOS-2, NISAR, 24 cm) scatters from branches and trunks and partially from the ground; **P-band** (ESA's Biomass mission, launched 2025, 69 cm) penetrates to the ground in most forests and is the first spaceborne sensor designed to measure forest structure and sub-canopy topography by polarimetric interferometry and tomography. The consequence is that every global DSM from radar or optical stereo carries a vegetation bias of meters in forest; the bare-earth corrections (MERIT, FABDEM; [Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)) remove the statistical part using canopy height maps and leave residuals that are largest in exactly the dense, tall forests where the bias was largest.

**Structure and biomass** are estimated from the vertical distribution of lidar returns rather than from the CHM alone. The **area-based approach** (Næsset 2002) regresses field-plot timber volume or biomass on plot-level lidar metrics—height percentiles (p50, p90, p95), canopy cover (fraction of first returns above a height threshold), density by height stratum—and applies the model wall to wall; it is the operational basis of lidar-assisted national forest inventories in the Nordic countries, Canada, and elsewhere, with plot-level biomass RMSE commonly 15–30 % (approximately; varies by forest type). Spaceborne lidar extends this: **GEDI** (on the ISS since December 2018) records full waveforms in 25 m footprints and derives relative height metrics (RH25–RH100) and ground elevation (L2A), footprint biomass (L4A), and 1 km gridded biomass (L4B); **ICESat-2's** ATLAS photon-counting lidar yields along-track terrain and canopy heights in 100 m segments (ATL08). Both are samples, not maps, and both have ground-finding failures on steep slopes and under dense canopy that propagate into height.

**Forest DTM error by density and slope** is well characterized. Hodgson and Bresnahan (2004) measured lidar DTM error by land cover and found RMSE growing from about 0.2 m on pavement and low grass to about 0.3 m in scrub and deciduous forest (approximately; leaf-off flight), with slope increasing error in every class; later studies at higher densities report similar ordering with smaller magnitudes. The error is a positive bias plus noise: the lowest return in a cell is rarely the ground under dense vegetation, so the DTM sits above it. The ASPRS and USGS vegetated vertical accuracy (**VVA**; 95th percentile of absolute error in vegetated classes under ASPRS Ed. 1 and the pre-2024 LBS, RMSE$_V$ reported as found under ASPRS Ed. 2 and LBS 2024) exists to report this separately from the non-vegetated NVA, and a lidar deliverable's VVA is the number to use for forest work ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)).

**ML forest removal versus measured DTMs.** FABDEM-class products predict the forest bias from canopy height, cover, and terrain and subtract it from a DSM; the result is a statistical bare-earth estimate whose error is the regression's residual—several meters in tall, dense, or structurally unusual forest (mangroves, bamboo, plantations) and in the mountainous terrain where SRTM/Copernicus heights are themselves worst. A measured lidar DTM has errors of decimeters in the same places. The two are not substitutes; the ML product is a prior for places without lidar, to be used with its stated residual, not its nominal resolution.

> **Worked example.** *CHM error budget with correlated terms.* A leaf-on CHM is built from a UAV SfM DSM (σ$_{DSM}$ = 0.3 m on canopy, from check-point comparison on open ground, inflated for canopy texture) and a national lidar DTM flown leaf-off (VVA 95th percentile 0.35 m in the forest class, so σ$_{DTM}$ ≈ 0.18 m, with a positive bias of +0.10 m estimated from RTK transects). For a cell with DSM = 123.4 m and DTM = 105.2 m:
>
> CHM = 123.4 − 105.2 = 18.2 m. Bias-corrected CHM = 18.2 + 0.10 = 18.3 m (the DTM sits above the true ground, so the raw CHM is too short).
>
> If the two errors are independent, σ$_{CHM}$ = $\sqrt{0.3^2 + 0.18^2}$ = 0.35 m. If the SfM DSM was co-registered to the lidar DTM on open ground (the usual practice), the datum errors are shared and cancel, and the residual DSM error is dominated by canopy-top matching noise—σ$_{CHM}$ is somewhat smaller. If instead the DSM and DTM come from *different* datums with an unknown offset of up to 0.2 m, add it as a bias term: CHM uncertainty is then 0.35 m random plus up to 0.2 m systematic, and a stand-mean CHM over 1,000 cells has random uncertainty 0.35/$\sqrt{1000}$ ≈ 0.01 m but systematic uncertainty still 0.2 m. For a 10 m plantation stand the datum term alone is a 2 % height error and, through an allometric biomass model with height exponent ~1.5–2, a 3–4 % biomass error—systematic across the whole estate.

## 64.2 Croplands: canopy, tillage, and engineered grade

Agricultural land has three elevation signals at three scales, and a DEM made without regard to timing mixes them.

**Seasonal canopy** rises from zero at planting to 0.5 m (soy, wheat), 1 m (rapeseed), 2–3 m (maize, sorghum, sugarcane), or more (orchards, hops) and then is removed at harvest. A summer DSM over a maize field is a canopy model; a lidar "DTM" over the same field depends entirely on whether the ground filter reached the soil through the crop (it usually does in row crops with gaps, usually not in lodged or dense cereal at full canopy). National programs that fly in leaf-off season for forests are also flying in bare-soil season for most temperate crops, which is why leaf-off is the right rule for both; where the acquisition is summer, the specification must state what "bare earth" means over crops, and the user must expect decimeters of positive bias. UAV SfM in crops is used deliberately the other way round: a bare-soil DSM at planting is the DTM, repeat flights through the season give **crop height** as DSM − DTM at centimeter precision, and that height tracks biomass and yield.

**Tillage micro-relief** is the second signal: ploughing, ridging, and bedding produce furrows with 10–30 cm amplitude and 0.5–1 m wavelength, and the furrow direction changes between operations. At 1 m grid spacing the furrows alias; at 0.1 m (UAV SfM) they are resolved; in either case the "field elevation" differs by ±10 cm depending on whether the sample falls on ridge or furrow and when in the rotation it was taken. Soil erosion studies by DEM of difference ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md)) must either sample at the same tillage state or filter the micro-relief, because a tillage event moves more soil vertically within the field than a year of erosion removes from it.

**Engineered grade** is the third. **Land levelling** for surface (flood and furrow) irrigation grades fields to designed slopes—often 0.1–0.2 % along the run and 0 % across—with tolerances of ±1–2 cm, executed by laser- or GNSS-controlled scrapers; **irrigation furrow design** needs the same precision; **terraces** are earthworks whose riser heights and bench slopes are the design variables; **drainage tiles** are installed at designed grades of 0.1–0.5 % at 0.8–1.2 m depth and are invisible to every remote sensor afterwards. The DEM requirements here are centimeter-level and are met with RTK-GNSS topographic surveys on 10–30 m grids or with UAV SfM/lidar at 2–5 cm, both tied to a local benchmark; the USDA NRCS conservation practice standards for land levelling (Practice 464, Irrigation Land Leveling) and precision land forming specify the design and tolerance conventions, and the commercial design suites (Trimble FieldLevel, Topcon) consume the survey directly. **Precision agriculture** more broadly uses DEMs for wetness and erosion zoning (TWI, slope; [Chapter 61](ch61-hydrology.md)), where 1–5 m lidar DTMs are adequate and the micro-relief should be filtered out.

## 64.3 Grasslands, shrub, tundra: the small biases that matter for change

Low vegetation imposes a small positive bias on lidar DTMs that is negligible for a topographic map and decisive for centimeter-level change detection. Grass and herbaceous cover 0.1–0.5 m tall produces last returns that are a few centimeters to a few decimeters above the soil, depending on density, pulse footprint, and the filter's aggressiveness; the usual range reported is **5–30 cm** of positive bias, seasonally varying with growth and mowing. Shrubs and heather add more and are less penetrable. In a DEM of difference between two surveys, a change in grass height between epochs—mowing, grazing, drought—appears as ground change of the same magnitude and sign, and the first question for any sub-decimeter change claim on vegetated ground is whether the vegetation was the same.

**Tundra and permafrost** add ground motion: seasonal **frost heave and thaw settlement** of several centimeters, and **thermokarst** subsidence of decimeters to meters where ground ice melts, superimposed on tussock and shrub biases of the same order as the seasonal signal. Repeat lidar or InSAR over permafrost must therefore be timed to the same point in the thaw season, and the vegetation bias checked with RTK transects at both epochs.

## 64.4 Wetlands and marshes: the surface that is mostly plant

Tidal marshes are the extreme case of vegetation bias, because the vegetation is dense, the ground is flat and soft, and the elevation question—where does the marsh surface sit relative to the tidal frame—is asked at the centimeter level. **Lidar positive bias in marsh** is well documented: Hladik and Alber (2012) compared a lidar DTM of a Georgia salt marsh with RTK-GNSS and found positive biases that varied by vegetation class, on the order of a decimeter to a quarter meter in tall *Spartina alterniflora* and smaller in short-form and mud classes (approximately; their class-specific values are the reference), and showed that **class-specific correction**—subtracting a per-class mean bias estimated from RTK points, with vegetation classes from hyperspectral or multispectral imagery—reduced the overall RMSE substantially. Rogers et al. (2018) extended this with full-waveform metrics and nonparametric models that predict the bias cell by cell. The correction is essential because the uncorrected bias exceeds the quantity of interest: a marsh's **elevation capital**—its height above the local mean sea level or mean high water, which determines how much sea-level rise it can absorb before drowning—is typically 0.1–0.5 m, and the lidar bias is a large fraction of it.

The frame of reference compounds the problem. Marsh elevations are meaningful relative to **tidal datums** (MHW, MHHW, MSL), which are local and derived from tide gauges, while lidar comes in NAVD 88 or another orthometric datum; the conversion (VDatum or local gauge analysis) carries 5–15 cm of uncertainty in many estuaries, comparable to the vegetation bias ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). **GNSS-RTK transects** are the truth for both problems: they measure the marsh surface directly, with the rod pushed to the sediment surface through the vegetation, at centimeter precision, and they can be tied to a local tide gauge. **Surface Elevation Tables (SETs)** measure elevation change at fixed benchmarks to millimeter precision over years and are the reference for marsh accretion versus relative sea-level rise; a lidar time series cannot resolve the few millimeters per year that SETs do, and the two are complementary (lidar for spatial pattern, SETs for rate).

**Mangroves** are forests on tidal mud, and the spaceborne DSMs are biased by the full canopy height—SRTM over mangroves is a canopy model, which Simard et al. (2019) exploited to map global mangrove canopy height (up to about 60 m in Gabon) by calibrating SRTM against lidar and field data. A mangrove's "ground" from SRTM, Copernicus, or even FABDEM is therefore wrong by meters, and coastal flood and SLR exposure analyses that use those products over mangrove coasts systematically *underestimate* exposure of the areas behind them while the mangrove itself appears as high ground; lidar (airborne or GEDI/ICESat-2 ground returns where they exist) is the only remote source of mangrove-floor elevation.

**Peatlands** move. The surface of a raised or blanket bog oscillates seasonally by centimeters—**"bog breathing"**, swelling in winter as the peat stores water and shrinking in summer—and subsides over years where the peat is drained and oxidizing. Alshammari et al. (2020) used InSAR (the intermittent SBAS method, which tolerates the low coherence of vegetated peat) to map these motions and showed that the amplitude and phase of the seasonal oscillation indicate peat condition: intact bogs breathe, degraded ones subside. For a DEM user the implications are that a peatland elevation has a season, that two lidar surveys of the same bog will differ by centimeters from water storage alone, and that long-term peat subsidence (centimeters per year in drained agricultural peat) is a real ground change that must be separated from the oscillation.

> **Case file.** *Marsh DEM bias and the sea-level-rise verdict.* Coastal wetland vulnerability assessments through the 2000s used uncorrected lidar DTMs to place marsh platforms within the tidal frame and routinely found marshes sitting high—close to MHHW—implying resilience to several decades of SLR. RTK surveys and the correction studies of the 2010s (Hladik and Alber 2012 and others) showed the lidar surfaces were 10–25 cm too high in the dominant vegetation classes, which in a tidal frame with a 1 m range moved many platforms from "high marsh, resilient" to "mid/low marsh, vulnerable" and changed projected drowning dates by decades. The lesson has since been written into USGS and NOAA coastal elevation practice: report lidar marsh elevations with class-specific bias corrections and RTK validation, or not at all.

## 64.5 Floodplain roughness and riparian edges from the same lidar

The lidar that produces a floodplain DTM also measures the vegetation standing on it, and hydraulic roughness is better taken from that measurement than from a land-cover class. Per cell, the point cloud yields vegetation height (p95 of non-ground returns above ground), fractional cover by height stratum (returns between 0–0.5 m, 0.5–2 m, 2–5 m, > 5 m as a fraction of total), and stem-level obstruction proxies; these map onto Manning's $n$ through relationships calibrated by flume and field studies—dense shrub 0.5–2 m tall near 0.1, mature open forest 0.08–0.12, grass 0.03–0.04 (approximately; standard hydraulic references give ranges). The advantage over a land-cover lookup is spatial detail (a 5 m hedgerow across the flow path is a 5 m hedgerow, not a field-average class) and shared epoch with the DTM. The caveat is season: a leaf-off acquisition under-represents summer understory and leaf density, so roughness derived from it is a lower bound for summer floods.

**Riparian canopy and shoreline extraction** ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)) face the same vegetation: overhanging canopy hides the bank and the water's edge from airborne sensors, the lidar water-surface void boundary is pushed outward under the canopy, and a shoreline digitized from a DSM follows the canopy drip line rather than the bank. Last-return and ground-class points under the canopy, where they exist, define the bank better than any surface; where they do not, the bank position carries an uncertainty of the canopy overhang width (meters) that should be stated.

## 64.6 Snow and ice: the seasonal surface measured by differencing

Snow is a seasonal surface layer whose depth is exactly what a DEM of difference measures. The **Airborne Snow Observatory** (ASO; Painter et al. 2016) flies scanning lidar over a basin in late summer (snow-free DTM) and repeatedly through winter and spring, differencing each snow-on surface against the snow-free DTM to produce snow depth at 3 m and, with modelled density, snow water equivalent at basin scale; reported depth uncertainties are at the decimeter level per pixel (approximately) and much smaller for basin means, and the products feed water-supply forecasts in California and Colorado. The method's requirements are instructive for all seasonal differencing ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md), [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)): the snow-free DTM must be bare earth under the vegetation that will later be buried (a leaf-off, high-density acquisition), the snow-on surface must be the snow, not the shrubs protruding through it, co-registration between epochs must be verified on snow-free rock to a few centimeters, and the vegetation bias of the snow-free DTM (grass 5–30 cm, §64.3) becomes a direct snow-depth error unless corrected. Glacier and ice-sheet surfaces ([Chapter 66](ch66-coastal-marine-polar-lakes-rivers.md)) extend the same logic to multi-year change, where the surface is the thing of interest and penetration (of radar into firn, of lidar into nothing) is the complication.

## 64.7 Temporal design: phenology, epochs, and growth versus ground

Because the living surface has a calendar, an acquisition has a season, and a DEM has a date that matters as much as its accuracy. **Phenology calendars** for the project area—leaf-out and leaf-fall dates for deciduous forest, planting and harvest for each major crop, growing season for marsh grass, peak and minimum for peat water storage, snow-on and snow-free dates—determine the acquisition window for each purpose: leaf-off and snow-free for a bare-earth DTM in temperate regions (a window often only 4–8 weeks wide in spring and autumn, which constrains flight scheduling and drives cost; [Chapter 26](ch26-survey-planning.md)); peak growth for a canopy or crop-height product; the same point in the tidal and growth cycle for repeat marsh surveys; the same thaw stage for permafrost.

**Multi-season acquisitions** are the only way to get both canopy and ground where evergreen or dense cover prevents one flight from doing both, and they are what makes a CHM time series meaningful: a summer DSM over a winter DTM from the same year. **Consistent definitions across epochs** matter more than the sensor: a DTM produced in 2010 with one ground filter and in 2020 with another differs under vegetation by the filters' bias difference, and a national program that changed its specification between cycles has created an apparent land-surface change. **Separating growth from ground change** in a DoD is then a classification problem: changes that coincide with vegetation presence, scale with vegetation height, and reverse seasonally are growth; changes on bare, stable surfaces that persist are ground. Compute the DoD on bare-ground cells (identified in both epochs), estimate the vegetation-induced residual on vegetated cells from the stable-area statistics by class, and report the two separately.

## 64.8 Products and benchmarks

The spaceborne lidar missions provide the global reference samples. **GEDI L2A** gives footprint ground elevation and RH metrics; **L4A/L4B** footprint and gridded biomass; its ground-finding fails on steep slopes within the 25 m footprint and under very dense canopy, and the geolocation uncertainty (~10 m) matters when comparing with 1 m lidar. **ICESat-2 ATL08** gives terrain and canopy height per 100 m segment with photon-counting sensitivity that reaches the ground through moderate canopy but with sparse photons under dense canopy and at night-vs-day noise differences. Both missions' footprints and segments, filtered by quality flags, serve as validation samples for gridded products and as calibration for regional models.

The gridded canopy height maps are models trained on these samples. **GLAD Global Forest Canopy Height** (Potapov et al. 2021) is a 30 m map for 2019 made by regressing GEDI RH95 on Landsat time-series metrics; **ETH Global Canopy Height** (Lang et al. 2023) is a 10 m map for 2020 from Sentinel-2 with GEDI as training labels, delivered with a per-pixel uncertainty; the **Meta/WRI** global canopy height map (Tolan et al. 2024) is a ~1 m map from Maxar imagery using a self-supervised vision transformer trained on aerial lidar CHMs. The last illustrates the validation caveat that applies to all three: the product's *grid spacing* is 1 m, but its *information content* is that of an optical image interpreted by a model, and its reported accuracy (a mean absolute error of a few meters against lidar in the authors' evaluation, approximately) is an average over conditions. Errors are structured, not random: saturation in tall forests (heights above ~30–35 m are compressed), underestimation at forest edges and in sparse canopy, overestimation on steep terrain and in some shadowed urban settings, and systematic differences between training regions and application regions. Independent evaluations against airborne lidar in regions not used for training have found larger errors than the published figures and have documented the saturation and edge effects. The right use is as a prior, a stratification layer, or a gap-filler, with local calibration against lidar or GEDI; the wrong use is to treat a 1 m ML pixel as a 1 m measurement.

**National forest inventories with lidar** (Norway, Finland, Sweden, Denmark, parts of Canada and the US) provide the strongest benchmarks because they combine wall-to-wall lidar with thousands of measured field plots; **NEON** (US National Ecological Observatory Network) flies lidar, hyperspectral, and camera over its sites annually under published AOP protocols with field plots and makes the data open, which makes it the best public testbed for CHM and biomass methods; **USGS marsh elevation datasets** and the coastal RTK/SET networks provide the wetland references. ## Then & now

Forest height was measured tree by tree with **clinometers and hypsometers** on field plots, and stand height was a plot statistic scaled up by stratified sampling. **Airborne lidar forestry** began with profiling lasers in the 1980s and scanning systems in the 1990s; Næsset's area-based approach (2002) turned plot regressions into operational inventory, and by the 2010s national forest inventories in the Nordic countries were lidar-assisted. **Spaceborne lidar** arrived with ICESat's GLAS (2003–2009, 70 m footprints), designed for ice but used for forest height, and matured with **GEDI** and **ICESat-2** (both launched 2018). **Radar** added structure-sensitive measurements from SRTM's canopy-biased C-band DSM (2000) through TanDEM-X (2010–) to the P-band **Biomass** mission (2025). The 2020s brought **machine-learned canopy height maps** at 30 m, 10 m, and 1 m, whose validation—how to characterize structured error in a model output that looks like a measurement—is the open question of the decade, and the extension of the same approach to bare-earth correction (FABDEM) carries the same caveats. In wetlands, the arc runs from uncorrected lidar in the 2000s to class-corrected, RTK-validated marsh DEMs as standard practice in the 2010s; in agriculture, from laser-levelled fields (1970s–) to RTK-GNSS and UAV SfM at centimeter scale.

## Mathematics

**CHM error.** With CHM = DSM − DTM and errors $\epsilon_S$, $\epsilon_T$ having variances $\sigma_S^2$, $\sigma_T^2$, biases $b_S$, $b_T$, and correlation $\rho$,

$$b_{\mathrm{CHM}} = b_S - b_T, \qquad \sigma^2_{\mathrm{CHM}} = \sigma_S^2 + \sigma_T^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_S \sigma_T .$$

Positive DTM bias under vegetation ($b_T > 0$) shortens the CHM; shared datum error ($\rho \to 1$) cancels; independent processing ($\rho = 0$) adds in quadrature. For a stand mean over $n$ cells with error correlation length $r$ and cell size $\Delta$, the effective sample size is about $n (\Delta/r)^2$ and the systematic terms do not shrink.

**Percentile metrics.** For returns above ground with heights $h_1 \le \dots \le h_m$ in a cell or plot, $p_q = h_{\lceil qm \rceil}$; **cover** $C = \#\{h_i > h_0\}/m_{\text{first}}$ with $h_0$ typically 2 m and $m_{\text{first}}$ the number of first returns. Percentile definitions differ across tools (interpolation method, inclusion of ground returns, first vs all returns), and the same stand yields p95 values differing by 0.5–1 m across definitions; state the definition.

**Penetration.** The fraction of pulse energy reaching the ground through a canopy of leaf area index $L$ with extinction coefficient $k$ (≈ 0.5 for random leaf orientation) is $P_g \approx e^{-kL}$; at $L$ = 4, $P_g \approx 0.14$; at $L$ = 8, 0.02. The expected ground-return density is $P_g$ times the pulse density times the probability the return exceeds the detection threshold, which is why 8 pulses/m² in dense tropical forest can yield fewer than 0.1 ground returns/m² and why the DTM there is an interpolation over tens of meters.

**Marsh bias correction.** Per vegetation class $c$ with $n_c$ RTK points, $\hat b_c = \frac{1}{n_c}\sum_i (z^{\text{lidar}}_i - z^{\text{RTK}}_i)$ and corrected DTM $z' = z - \hat b_{c(x,y)}$; the residual RMSE after correction is $\sqrt{\sigma_c^2 + \sigma_{\text{RTK}}^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat b_c)}$, and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat b_c) = \sigma_c^2 / n_c$ demands $n_c$ of order 30+ per class for the correction's own uncertainty to be below 2 cm when $\sigma_c$ ≈ 0.1 m. Regression on continuous predictors (NDVI, waveform width, return intensity) replaces the class mean with $\hat b(\mathbf{x})$ and reduces within-class variance.

**Seasonal signal decomposition.** For a surface time series $z(t)$ at a point (InSAR over peat, repeat lidar over marsh), fit $z(t) = z_0 + v t + A\cos(\omega t - \phi) + \epsilon(t)$ with $\omega = 2\pi/\text{yr}$; $v$ is the long-term trend (subsidence or accretion), $A$ and $\phi$ the seasonal amplitude and phase (bog breathing). With $N$ epochs spanning $T$ years and noise $\sigma_\epsilon$, $\sigma_v \approx \sigma_\epsilon \sqrt{12/(N T^2)}$ for evenly spaced samples—two lidar epochs five years apart with 5 cm noise give $\sigma_v$ ≈ 1.4 cm/yr, inadequate for millimeter-per-year marsh accretion (hence SETs) but adequate for centimeter-per-year peat subsidence.

## Validation & uncertainty

Validation on the living surface has one extra rule: **truth has a date and a vegetation class**, and a check point without both is not a check point.

**Ground truth.** RTK-GNSS or total-station points with the rod on the mineral soil (through grass, litter, or marsh vegetation), recorded with the vegetation class, height, and condition at the time, and surveyed within days of the acquisition for seasonal surfaces; transects rather than scattered points, so that the correlation length of the bias can be estimated; and enough points per class (≥ 30 as a working minimum) to estimate class bias to a few centimeters. For canopy height, field-measured tree heights on plots co-located with the lidar (NEON and national inventories provide these), or a higher-density, leaf-off lidar as a reference for the CHM. For snow, probe depths and snow pits at the time of flight.

**Statistics by class.** Report NVA and VVA separately and, within VVA, per vegetation class: mean error (the bias, which is the actionable number), RMSE, and the 95th percentile of absolute error, because the vegetated distribution is skewed (ASPRS Edition 2 reports VVA as RMSE$_V$ as found; the percentile, the Edition 1 / LBS-era statistic, remains the honest companion number). A single RMSE across classes hides a 5 cm pavement error and a 25 cm marsh bias in one meaningless number.

**Testing the DTM under canopy.** Compare ground-return density maps with the canopy cover map; where density falls below roughly 0.1–0.2 returns/m², treat the DTM as interpolated and mask or flag it. Compare against GEDI L2A or ICESat-2 ATL08 ground elevations filtered by quality flag and slope as an independent, if sparse, check at landscape scale. On slopes, check for the characteristic positive bias on ridges and negative in gullies that interpolation across sparse ground produces.

**Testing a canopy height product.** Stratify by height, slope, canopy cover, and land-cover class; compute bias and RMSE per stratum against airborne lidar CHMs with the *same percentile definition* and against GEDI RH95/RH98 in footprints with good quality flags; plot predicted against reference to see saturation; test at forest edges separately. For ML products, test in regions not used for training if that information is available, and treat the published accuracy as a best case.

**Separating growth from change.** For a DoD on vegetated terrain, compute the stable-area statistics on bare ground only, estimate the per-class vegetation residual from areas known to be unchanged, and report the minimum detectable change per class ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)); a 3 cm change claim on a grass surface with 10 cm seasonal bias is not detectable without RTK at both epochs.

## Standards & guides

- **USGS Lidar Base Specification** (current online edition) — leaf-off and snow-free acquisition conditions, ground classification, NVA/VVA reporting.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Ed. 2 (2023)** — VVA reported as RMSE$_V$ as found (the 95th percentile is the Ed. 1 / pre-2024 LBS statistic); vegetated check-point requirements.
- **NEON Airborne Observation Platform protocols and data product documentation** (lidar DTM/CHM, ecosystem structure) — a published, repeatable airborne protocol with field plots.
- **FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment and IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories (2006; 2019 Refinement)** — how canopy height and lidar-derived biomass enter carbon accounting, with uncertainty reporting requirements.
- **USDA NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 464 (Irrigation Land Leveling)** and **Precision Land Forming (462)** — design grades and tolerances.
- **Ramsar Convention wetland inventory and mapping guidance** — wetland delineation conventions relevant to marsh DEM classes.
- **GEDI and ICESat-2 Algorithm Theoretical Basis Documents (L2A, L4A; ATL08)** — definitions of RH metrics, ground finding, and quality flags.

## Pitfalls

- **Leaf-on lidar DTM under dense canopy used as bare earth** → few or no ground returns; the DTM is an interpolation with positive bias → check ground-return density; require leaf-off; report VVA per class; mask low-density areas.
- **Using FABDEM or other corrected global DSMs over mangroves, marsh, or dense tropical forest** → the ML correction's residual is meters precisely there → use lidar or spaceborne-lidar ground samples; state the residual.
- **Crop-season "bare earth"** → a summer DTM over cereal or maize is a crop or partial-crop surface → acquire at bare-soil stage; or treat crop height as the product and the planting-date DSM as the DTM.
- **Comparing canopy heights computed with different percentile definitions or tools** → p95 vs p98 vs max, first vs all returns, differ by 0.5–1 m or more → fix one definition and recompute both.
- **Believing a 1 m ML canopy height map has 1 m accuracy** → grid spacing is not information content; errors are structured (saturation, edges, slopes) → validate locally against lidar/GEDI; use as a prior or stratifier.
- **Forgetting grass and low-vegetation bias in centimeter-level change studies** → 5–30 cm seasonal bias masquerades as ground change → RTK at both epochs; compute change on bare ground; report per-class MDC.
- **Marsh elevations in NAVD 88 compared with tidal datums without a local conversion** → 5–15 cm conversion uncertainty, sometimes larger → use VDatum or a local gauge analysis and carry its uncertainty.
- **Snow depth from differencing without a vegetation-corrected snow-free DTM** → shrub and grass bias becomes snow-depth error → correct the snow-free DTM per class; verify co-registration on bare rock.
- **Peat or permafrost "subsidence" from two epochs at different seasons** → bog breathing and thaw settlement are centimeters → match season; fit the seasonal term with more epochs (InSAR).
- **Changed ground-filter or specification between epochs** → apparent surface change under vegetation → reprocess both epochs with one pipeline or estimate the filter bias difference on stable vegetated areas.
- **Treating GEDI/ICESat-2 footprints as exact** → ~10 m geolocation and slope-induced ground error → buffer and filter by quality and slope; expect several meters of scatter on steep terrain.

## Key takeaways

- The living surface has a season and a bias: every DTM under vegetation sits above the ground by an amount that depends on the vegetation class, the season, and the sensor; measure it with RTK and report it per class.
- CHM = DSM − DTM inherits both terms' errors; the DTM under dense canopy is the weak term, and shared datum errors cancel only if the two surfaces were co-registered.
- Penetration ranks the sensors—lidar (through gaps), P-band, L-band, C/X-band, optical (none)—and determines whether a product is a ground, a canopy, or something in between; global DSMs are canopy-biased by meters in forest and mangrove.
- Leaf-off and bare-soil acquisition windows are specifications, not preferences; design the acquisition calendar around phenology and keep definitions constant across epochs.
- Marsh, mangrove, and peat elevations are decided at the centimeter level in a tidal or hydrological frame; class-specific bias correction, RTK transects, SETs, and a careful tidal-datum conversion are all required.
- Croplands carry three signals—seasonal canopy, tillage micro-relief, and engineered grade—at three scales; choose the acquisition and the filtering for the one you want.
- Treat ML canopy height and bare-earth products as priors with structured, locally testable error, not as measurements; validate against lidar and spaceborne lidar in your own region.
- Separate growth from ground change explicitly: compute change on bare stable ground, estimate vegetation residuals per class, and report minimum detectable change by class.
