# Chapter 36 — Seasonal and environmental variability (leaves, snow, groundwater, crops, steam, tides)

> **Part VII — From sensor data to products.** The last chapter of the processing part asks what the sensor actually measured on the day: a surface that has a season, a weather, and a tide, none of which the file name mentions.

**In this chapter.** Two surveys of the same hillside, both correct, can differ by a metre of canopy, half a metre of snow, 20 cm of crop, 10 cm of tilled soil, 3 cm of groundwater-driven uplift, and 20 cm of Earth tide—before any error. You will be able to list the environmental terms that move an elevation measurement, with magnitudes and periods: leaf-on/leaf-off penetration and phenology; crop growth, harvest, and tillage; snow and ice as a surface (and how the Airborne Snow Observatory turns that into a product); groundwater, soil moisture, and swelling clays; tides, surge, solid-Earth tides, and ocean, atmospheric, and hydrological loading; atmospheric refraction in laser, EDM, levelling, GNSS, and InSAR; fog, smoke, dust, and industrial steam; aeolian and volcanic deposition. You will know which of these are measurement conditions to be corrected, which are real surface states to be recorded, and which are confounds that masquerade as change, and you will be able to specify acquisition windows and the acquisition-condition metadata that make a DEM comparable to another one.

## 36.1 Vegetation

Vegetation is the largest seasonal term in most temperate and tropical DEMs, and it acts on both the DSM (the canopy top moves) and the DTM (the ground is seen or not).

**Leaf-on versus leaf-off.** A deciduous canopy in full leaf intercepts most of a lidar pulse's energy; the fraction of pulses that reach the ground falls from 40–80 % in winter to 5–20 % in summer for typical temperate broadleaf stands, with wide variation by species, density, and pulse footprint. Fewer ground returns mean a sparser, noisier ground surface, more interpolation, and a greater chance that low vegetation and understory are classified as ground, biasing the DTM upward by decimetres. This is why the USGS Lidar Base Specification and most national programs specify **leaf-off** acquisition for bare-earth products wherever deciduous cover is significant—and why leaf-off is not always possible (evergreen forest, tropical forest, or a northern leaf-off window that coincides with snow cover). For the DSM, leaf-on versus leaf-off changes the canopy top by the depth of the leafy crown—typically 1–5 m in broadleaf stands—and the canopy height model derived from a leaf-off DSM minus a leaf-off DTM is a different quantity from a leaf-on one. Photogrammetric DSMs are more affected than lidar: in leaf-on conditions image matching sees only the canopy surface and the DTM beneath is unrecoverable; in leaf-off conditions, matching on bare branches is noisy and the "surface" lies somewhere within the crown.

**Phenology and growth.** Between the two extremes, canopy height and density track the growing season; an acquisition in May and one in August in the same year differ by the seasonal leaf expansion. Over years, forests grow: 0.2–1 m yr⁻¹ in height for young plantations, less for mature stands; **clear-cuts** remove 20–40 m of surface overnight and **regrowth** restores it over decades. A DSM-to-DSM difference over forest is therefore dominated by growth, harvest, and phenology, and a DTM-to-DTM difference is contaminated wherever the ground-return fraction changed between epochs ([Chapter 64](ch64-agriculture-forests-wetlands.md)).

**Crops.** A maize field is bare soil in April, 2.5–3 m of canopy in August, and stubble in October; wheat reaches 0.7–1 m; sugar cane 3–4 m. A DSM over cropland is a crop-height map with a date; a DTM filter will usually remove tall crops but struggles with dense short ones (ripe wheat, rapeseed), leaving a bias of 10–50 cm. **Tillage** changes the soil surface itself: ploughing and ridging create microtopography of 10–30 cm amplitude, so that two bare-soil DEMs of the same field, one after ploughing and one after harrowing or rolling, differ by that amount in roughness and by several centimetres in mean level (loosened soil has a lower bulk density and stands higher until it settles). For precision agriculture and soil-erosion studies this is the signal; for everyone else it is noise with a calendar.

**Grass, marsh, and low vegetation.** Lidar rarely penetrates dense grass or marsh canopy to the soil; the "ground" return comes from somewhere within the sward, and the bias scales with vegetation height and density—10–30 cm for a *Spartina* marsh, up to 50 cm in tall reeds, less after grazing, mowing, or winter die-back ([Chapter 32](ch32-dsm-to-dtm.md), [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). Because the bias is seasonal, a marsh surveyed in late summer and again in early spring will appear to have subsided by the difference in vegetation height.

A composite scenario, assembled from recurring experience in state and county programs rather than a single documented incident, shows the failure mode. A county is acquired leaf-on in one year to meet a funding deadline and re-flown leaf-off six years later to a newer specification. A downstream change analysis of the two DTMs reports widespread "subsidence" of 10–30 cm across forested parcels and no change on open ground. The signal is the difference in ground-return density and understory classification between leaf-on and leaf-off; after reclassifying the earlier cloud with a stricter ground filter and restricting the comparison to cells with a measured ground return in both epochs, the forest "subsidence" vanishes. This is why the USGS LBS and several state programs require the acquisition season and a ground-return density raster to be delivered with the DEM—and why comparing a leaf-on DSM to a leaf-off DTM remains the most common way to report tree growth that did not happen.

## 36.2 Snow and ice

Snow is a surface. A lidar, photogrammetric, or radar DEM acquired over snow-covered ground measures the snow surface (or, for radar, some depth into it), and a winter DEM is a **snow DEM**, not a terrain model. The difference is not small: seasonal snowpacks are 0.5–3 m deep in mountain basins, with drifts exceeding 10 m, and a lidar flown in March over the Sierra Nevada or the Alps returns a surface that will be metres lower by August. Winter acquisition for terrain is therefore specified against—snow-free is a standard clause in topographic lidar specifications alongside leaf-off and no flooding—unless the snow is the target.

When the snow is the target, the same physics becomes a measurement method. The **Airborne Snow Observatory (ASO)** (Painter et al., 2016) flies scanning lidar over a basin once in snow-free conditions to produce a reference DTM and repeatedly through the season to produce snow-surface DEMs; snow depth is the difference, at 3 m resolution across entire watersheds, and an imaging spectrometer measures snow albedo. Combined with modelled density, the product is snow water equivalent, the quantity water managers need. The method's accuracy depends on both surveys' vertical accuracy and co-registration (stable-terrain differences are used to check and correct them), on the snow-free DTM being truly snow-free and recent (a landslide or a new road between the reference and the snow survey appears as snow depth), and on vegetation: under forest, the snow surface is seen only through canopy gaps and the depth is sampled, not mapped. Reported snow-depth uncertainties at the ASO's 3 m posting are of the order of a decimetre in open terrain, larger under canopy (see Painter et al., 2016 for the validation details). The general lesson is that a change-detection product is only as good as the stability of what is assumed not to have changed.

**Glaciers** are ice surfaces that move and thin; a glacier DEM is a snapshot of the surface at a date in the melt season or the accumulation season, and glacier-change studies (Nuth and Kääb, 2011; Hugonnet et al., 2021) go to considerable lengths to correct for seasonal acquisition dates, co-registration biases on stable terrain, and radar penetration into firn, which makes a C- or X-band DEM of an accumulation zone lie metres below the optical surface. **River and lake ice** at acquisition presents a flat surface at or above the open-water level, roughened by pressure ridges; it is neither water nor terrain and must be masked. **Sea ice is not terrain** at all: its surface rises and falls with the tide and drifts kilometres per day; altimetric measurements over sea ice are freeboard relative to the local sea surface, not elevation relative to a datum, and no sea-ice surface belongs in a DEM.

Frozen ground moves. **Frost heave** lifts soil surfaces by centimetres to decimetres in winter as ice lenses grow, and the surface settles in spring; in seasonally frozen agricultural soils the annual cycle is 1–5 cm, in permafrost active layers it is larger. **Permafrost thaw subsidence** is secular and accelerating: active-layer deepening and ground-ice loss lower the surface by millimetres to centimetres per year on average, and **thermokarst** produces metres of local collapse. A DEM of permafrost terrain has a date and a season in both senses: where in the freeze–thaw cycle, and how many years of thaw.

## 36.3 Water: groundwater, soil moisture, reservoirs, tides, and the solid Earth

Water moves the ground as well as covering it.

**Groundwater and soil moisture.** Aquifer systems compress when pumped and re-expand when recharged; the elastic part of that cycle produces seasonal vertical motion of millimetres to a few centimetres in many basins (the Central Valley of California, Mexico City's margins, and the Houston area are well-studied examples), superimposed on the inelastic, permanent subsidence of over-drafted aquifers that reaches decimetres per year in the worst cases ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)). **Swelling clays** (smectite-rich soils) expand when wet and shrink when dry, with seasonal surface displacements of centimetres in expansive-soil regions; InSAR time series over London, Texas, and parts of Australia show the annual cycle clearly. Neither is an error; both are real surface motion with a period of one year, and a DEM from the wet season and one from the dry season will differ by the amplitude whether or not anything irreversible happened.

**Reservoirs and rivers** were treated in [Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md): the water surface at acquisition is the "ground" of the day, and reservoir drawdown, river stage, and flooding each change which cells are water and at what elevation. The acquisition date relative to the hydrograph is as important a metadata item as the sensor.

**Tides and surge at acquisition.** On the coast, the water level at acquisition determines where the land–water boundary falls and, for topographic lidar over tidal flats, how much of the flat was exposed; a flight at mid-tide loses half the intertidal zone. Storm surge and wave set-up can raise the water surface by a metre or more above the predicted tide, so predicted tide alone is not a sufficient record; the observed water level from the nearest gauge should be logged per flight line.

**Solid-Earth tides and loading.** The Earth itself deforms under the Moon's and Sun's gravity: the **solid-Earth (body) tide** displaces the crust vertically with amplitudes up to about ±20 cm at low latitudes (semi-diurnal and diurnal constituents combined; the peak-to-peak range can approach 40 cm), everywhere, every day. Its effect on a DEM depends on how the heights were obtained. GNSS processing in a modern geodetic reference frame removes the body tide using the IERS Conventions model (Petit and Luzum, 2010), so a GNSS-derived height is a "tide-free" or, more precisely, conventional-tide-system height at the epoch; a kinematic trajectory from a survey aircraft is corrected the same way inside the GNSS/INS processing, so the lidar surface inherits the correction. Levelled heights and tide-gauge heights contain the tide at the moment of measurement unless corrected. The **permanent tide**—the time-averaged part of the tidal deformation—creates the distinction between tide-free, zero-tide, and mean-tide systems, which differ by up to about 10 cm in the geoid and about 3 cm in heights depending on latitude; mixing systems between a GNSS height and a geoid model produces a latitude-dependent bias of that order ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md), [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). **Ocean tide loading**—the flexing of the crust under the weight of the moving ocean tide—adds vertical displacements of a few centimetres near coasts, exceeding 5 cm beside shelf seas with large tides (the Bay of Fundy, the Channel, Patagonia), with semi-diurnal and diurnal periods; it is modelled from ocean-tide models (FES, TPXO, GOT) and applied in precise GNSS processing. **Atmospheric pressure loading** displaces the crust by about 1–2 cm (peak-to-peak) with weather systems, more over continental interiors; **hydrological loading** by seasonal water storage produces annual vertical cycles of 1–3 cm over most continents and 5–7 cm peak-to-peak in the Amazon basin (Bevis et al., 2005); the **pole tide** adds about 1–2 cm with a 14-month period. For a single DEM at decimetre accuracy, these are below the noise; for cm-level GNSS control, for repeat surveys that aim at centimetre change, and for interpreting InSAR time series, they are the signal floor and must be modelled or averaged out.

> **Worked example.** A GNSS base station for a lidar survey is occupied for 2 hours at 09:00–11:00 local time on a coastal site at 45° N, and its height is computed by a quick single-baseline solution without tidal corrections. The body tide at that place and time is +12 cm (crust displaced upward); the ocean tide loading is −3 cm; atmospheric loading, with a 1010 hPa high over the region, is +0.5 cm. The computed station height is therefore about 9.5 cm higher than its conventional (tide-free) value, and every lidar point referenced to it inherits a +9.5 cm bias. A PPP or network solution with IERS 2010 corrections removes this; a 24-hour static occupation averages the semi-diurnal and diurnal terms to a residual of a centimetre or two but not the loading. Against a 10 cm NVA requirement, 9.5 cm of avoidable bias is not acceptable, and it is invisible in the survey's internal consistency checks because every strip carries it.

## 36.4 Atmosphere

The atmosphere affects the measurement rather than the surface, but the magnitude depends on the weather of the day and so belongs in the acquisition record.

**Refraction in optical ranging and angles.** Light bends toward denser air. For terrestrial lines of sight, the standard **coefficient of refraction** $k \approx 0.13$ expresses the curvature of the ray relative to the Earth's; the combined correction for Earth curvature and refraction to a measured vertical difference over distance $d$ is approximately $(1-k)\,d^2/(2R)$, about $0.067\,d^2$ m with $d$ in km—7 cm at 1 km, 27 cm at 2 km. Near the ground on a sunny day, temperature gradients make $k$ strongly negative and variable, and refraction becomes the dominant error in long trigonometric height traverses; **levelling** controls it with short, balanced foresight and backsight distances and by avoiding lines close to hot ground. For a laser rangefinder or lidar, the group refractive index of air (about 1.00027 at 1064 nm under standard conditions) changes the measured range by roughly 1 ppm per 1 °C and 0.3–0.4 ppm per hPa; an airborne system at 1,500 m range with an atmospheric model wrong by 10 ppm is wrong by 1.5 cm, and the error is a scale error that grows with range and shows up as a swath-edge-to-centre difference. EDM instruments apply a correction from measured temperature and pressure; a 10 °C error in the assumed temperature at 1 km is a 1 cm range error.

**Tropospheric delay in GNSS and InSAR.** Microwaves are slowed by the neutral atmosphere: the zenith delay is about 2.3–2.6 m, of which the hydrostatic part (modelled from surface pressure to a few millimetres) is the bulk and the **wet delay** (0–40 cm, from water vapour) is the variable, poorly modelled part. In GNSS positioning the wet delay is estimated as a parameter, but its correlation with the height component means that residual tropospheric error maps roughly 3:1 into height error; kinematic trajectories on humid days and over mountainous terrain are the most affected ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)). In InSAR, differences in tropospheric delay between the two acquisitions appear directly as phase and therefore as apparent height (for DEM generation) or displacement (for deformation): turbulent water-vapour variations produce centimetre-scale, kilometre-scale artefacts, and the stratified delay, correlated with topography, produces a height-dependent error that can mimic a DEM error of metres in a repeat-pass DEM (single-pass systems such as SRTM and TanDEM-X are immune to this term). Weather-model corrections (ERA5-based) and multi-interferogram stacking reduce but do not eliminate it ([Chapter 21](ch21-radar-sar-insar.md)).

**Fog, cloud, dust, smoke.** These block optical sensors and attenuate lidar; a lidar flown under a cloud base too close to the aircraft loses pulses to the cloud and gains false high returns from it; haze and smoke reduce return energy and therefore ground-return density under canopy, converting a usable leaf-off flight into a sparse one. **Industrial steam**—from cooling towers, refineries, paper mills—is a transient surface that returns lidar pulses and matches in imagery, producing plumes tens to hundreds of metres tall in DSMs that drift with the wind; [Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md) treats their detection and removal, and this chapter's point is that their presence is a property of the acquisition day (wind, temperature, plant operation) that should be recorded rather than rediscovered.

> **Rule of thumb.** Environmental terms that are *measurement* conditions (refraction, tropospheric delay, Earth tides, loading) can and should be corrected in processing, and the corrections and their models named in the metadata. Terms that are *surface* conditions (leaves, snow, crops, tillage, soil moisture, water level, steam) cannot be corrected without a model of the surface; they must be recorded as acquisition state and respected in comparison. The rule's limit is radar penetration into snow, firn, sand, and canopy, which is both a measurement condition and a surface condition and resists clean separation.

## 36.5 Aeolian and volcanic deposition, dune migration, ash

Some surfaces are rebuilt by the wind on the time scale of a survey campaign. **Dunes** migrate: barchans in unvegetated deserts move metres to tens of metres per year, with small dunes moving fastest; coastal foredunes advance and retreat seasonally with storm erosion and summer recovery; transverse ridges in large ergs move more slowly but carry sand volumes that make any two DEMs a year apart differ by metres over the slip faces. A DEM of a dune field is a snapshot of a moving pattern, and change detection between epochs measures migration, which is the point of such studies ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md)), but it also means that a 2015 DEM used as "terrain" for a 2025 road design in the same field is wrong by the migration distance. Wind also strips and deposits sand around structures, infilling ditches and burying fences between surveys, and **aeolian deflation** lowers unvegetated surfaces by millimetres to centimetres per year in dry regions—below detection for single pairs, visible in decadal comparisons.

**Volcanic ash and tephra** deposit a new surface in hours. Falls of centimetres are common tens of kilometres from an erupting vent; decimetres to metres occur near-field (Eyjafjallajökull 2010, Calbuco 2015, Taal 2020 produced such deposits in inhabited areas). The deposit compacts, erodes, and is remobilised by wind and rain over the following months and years, so a post-eruption DEM is a transient surface. The same applies to **snow-like** deposits of industrial or mining dust, and, at smaller magnitude, to leaf litter and organic accumulation on forest floors, which raise the lidar "ground" by a few centimetres between autumn and spring in deciduous stands.

## 36.6 Scheduling and metadata

Because the surface has a season, acquisition is scheduled against it, and the schedule's assumptions become part of the data's meaning.

**Acquisition windows.** A topographic lidar specification typically requires leaf-off, snow-free, no flooding, no standing water on fields, low tide for coastal flats, and often a minimum sun angle for concurrent imagery. In the northern temperate zone this is a window of a few weeks in late autumn and another in early spring, shortened further by weather suitable for flying; in the US Midwest, a leaf-off and snow-free and not-flooded window may be only days in some years, which is why multi-year statewide programs spread acquisition across seasons and why some blocks end up leaf-on. Bathymetric lidar needs clear water, calm seas, and often a seasonal window between runoff turbidity and plankton blooms. Glacier and snow surveys target the end of the melt season (minimum snow) or peak accumulation (ASO's purpose). Agricultural DEMs target post-harvest bare soil before tillage, or specific crop stages. Each choice fixes what the DEM shows; the specification should state the window, and the delivery should state whether it was met, block by block.

**Per-pixel dates in mosaics.** Global and national products are composites of acquisitions spread over months or years, and the acquisition date varies across the product. SRTM is the exception that proves the rule: acquired 11–22 February 2000, it is a single-epoch snapshot of the northern-hemisphere winter and southern-hemisphere summer, with the consequence that its forest canopy is leaf-off in the temperate north and leaf-on in the south, and its snow cover is maximal in northern mountains. The Copernicus DEM, from TanDEM-X acquisitions spanning 2011–2015 (with gap-filling from later years), has different seasons in neighbouring tiles and multiple years within one tile; ASTER GDEM stacks scenes from 2000 onward; national lidar mosaics stitch blocks flown years apart. A per-pixel acquisition-date raster (or at least a per-block date attribute) is therefore essential metadata, and its absence makes change detection against the mosaic uninterpretable: a difference of 2 m over forest may be growth since 2011 or since 2015, or may be leaf-on versus leaf-off between adjacent tiles.

**Metadata of acquisition conditions.** The minimum set that makes a DEM comparable to another:

| Item | Why it matters | Where recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition start/end date and time, per block or flight line | Season, phenology, tide, sun angle | Flight-line index; per-pixel date raster |
| Vegetation state (leaf-on/off, crop stage) | DTM bias, DSM meaning | Project report; land-cover-stratified density raster |
| Snow and ice cover at acquisition | Snow DEM vs terrain | Project report; snow mask |
| Water levels: tide (predicted and observed), river stage, reservoir level | Land–water boundary, flattening elevation | Per flight line; gauge references |
| Weather: temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, cloud base, visibility | Refraction, tropospheric delay, steam, dropouts | Flight log; nearest meteorological station |
| Tidal and loading corrections applied in GNSS processing | Height system consistency | Processing report; model names and versions |
| Recent events (storm, flood, fire, eruption, harvest, tillage, construction) | Transient surfaces | Project report; event mask |

**Seasonal confounds in change detection.** The design rule is to compare like with like: the same season, the same vegetation state, the same tide stage, the same water level, and the same processing conventions. Where that is impossible, the comparison must be restricted to cells where the seasonal term is negligible—stable, open, unvegetated, well-drained ground—and those cells used both to co-register the epochs and to estimate the noise floor ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)). Aliasing is the specific hazard of sparse temporal sampling: two DEMs 2.5 years apart, one in spring and one in autumn, cannot distinguish a secular trend from the seasonal cycle, and a trend fitted to them is contaminated by half a seasonal amplitude; three or more epochs spanning complete cycles, or a model of the seasonal term from auxiliary data (GNSS stations, soil-moisture or phenology indices), are required to separate them ([Chapter 37](ch37-time-scales-of-change.md)).

> **Try it.** Build a per-pixel acquisition-date raster from a lidar project's flight-line index and use it to flag cells whose two epochs differ in season. Expected outcome: `date1.tif` and `date2.tif` holding day-of-year; `season_flag.tif` marking cells where the epochs fall in different leaf states, to be excluded or treated separately in a DTM difference.
>
> ```bash
> # Flight-line polygons with an ISO date attribute -> day-of-year raster per epoch
> ogr2ogr -f GPKG -sql "SELECT *, CAST(strftime('%j', acq_date) AS INTEGER) AS doy FROM lines" lines1_doy.gpkg lines1.gpkg
> gdal_rasterize -a doy -tr 1 1 -te 500000 4100000 510000 4110000 -ot Int16 -a_nodata -1 lines1_doy.gpkg date1.tif
> ogr2ogr -f GPKG -sql "SELECT *, CAST(strftime('%j', acq_date) AS INTEGER) AS doy FROM lines" lines2_doy.gpkg lines2.gpkg
> gdal_rasterize -a doy -tr 1 1 -te 500000 4100000 510000 4110000 -ot Int16 -a_nodata -1 lines2_doy.gpkg date2.tif
> # Leaf-on if DOY in [135, 290] for this mid-latitude site; flag cells whose leaf state differs
> gdal_calc.py -A date1.tif -B date2.tif --outfile=season_flag.tif --type=Byte \
>   --calc="((A>=135)*(A<=290)) != ((B>=135)*(B<=290))"
> gdalinfo -hist season_flag.tif | tail -2
> ```
>
> Adjust the leaf-on day-of-year bounds to the site's phenology; the flagged fraction tells you how much of a forest difference map is comparable at all.

## Then & now

Classical surveying knew its seasons: levelling parties worked in the cool hours to limit refraction, triangulation observed at night or in the "shimmer-free" windows, and the 19th-century geodetic arcs recorded temperature with every measurement. What the classical era lacked was the ability to see the whole surface at once, so seasonal change in vegetation and snow was a field note, not a data layer. The first large DEMs from map sources carried no acquisition date at all, inheriting whatever the aerial photography's season had been.

SRTM's eleven days in February 2000 made epoch and season a visible property of a global product; the SRTM–lidar and SRTM–ICESat comparisons of the 2000s quantified canopy and snow effects as biases. The repeat-lidar era—ASO from 2013 for snow, national programs on 5–10-year cycles, and glacier DEM time series culminating in Hugonnet et al.'s 2000–2019 global assessment—made seasonal state a variable to design around. On the geodetic side, IERS Conventions formalised the tidal and loading models (1996, 2003, 2010), GNSS processing centres adopted them, and the question shifted from whether to correct Earth tides to whether the loading models agree at the millimetre. The frontier is per-pixel condition metadata as standard deliverables, continuous observation of the seasonal terms themselves (SWOT for water levels, GRACE-FO for loading, Sentinel-1 time series for soil-moisture and clay cycles, GEDI and ICESat-2 for phenology-tagged canopy), and change-detection methods that model the seasonal term rather than merely avoiding it.

## Validation & uncertainty

Environmental variability enters the error budget in two ways: as a bias in a single DEM relative to the surface the user wanted, and as a confound in a difference between DEMs. Validating against it means testing the DEM under the conditions the user cares about, not under the conditions most convenient for checkpoints.

**Stratify the accuracy test by condition.** The ASPRS standard already separates non-vegetated and vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA and VVA); extend the stratification to the conditions that varied in the project: leaf-on versus leaf-off blocks, snow-affected versus snow-free, pre- and post-harvest fields, marsh versus upland. The VVA computed in a leaf-off block says nothing about a leaf-on block flown to meet a deadline, and the project report should give the statistics per block with the acquisition date. Where ground truth exists in vegetated terrain (RTK profiles under canopy, in marsh, in crops), use it to estimate the bias, not just the spread, and publish the bias by land-cover class as a correction table.

**Use stable terrain as the seasonal thermometer.** In any two-epoch comparison, difference the DEMs over surfaces that should not have changed—bare rock, paved roads, building roofs where the DSM is compared—and examine the statistics: the mean tells you the co-registration or datum offset, the spread tells you the noise floor, and any dependence on elevation, aspect, or land cover tells you that something seasonal or systematic survives. Nuth and Kääb (2011) showed how aspect-dependent differences reveal horizontal misregistration and how elevation-dependent differences reveal scale and penetration effects; the same diagnostics reveal snow (elevation-dependent positive differences in winter epochs) and vegetation (land-cover-dependent differences).

**Check the geodetic corrections.** Verify in the GNSS/INS processing report which tidal and loading models were applied (IERS 2010 solid-Earth tide, which ocean-tide-loading model, whether atmospheric loading was applied) and in which tide system the heights are expressed; verify that the geoid model used for h → H is in the same system. A mismatch produces a latitude-dependent bias of up to a few centimetres that no checkpoint test will attribute correctly, because the checkpoints were processed the same way. Compare the survey's base-station heights against published CORS/IGS solutions as an independent check.

**Record and propagate.** Each environmental term that could not be corrected should appear in the uncertainty statement as a bias range by condition—"DTM in marsh: +0.10 to +0.30 m relative to sediment surface; DTM in leaf-on deciduous forest: +0.05 to +0.20 m; snow-affected cells (mask provided): not terrain"—rather than being folded into a single RMSE. For differences, the minimum detectable change must include the seasonal amplitude of any term not matched between epochs.

> **Uncertainty budget.** Environmental contributions to the vertical difference between two DEMs of the same area acquired in different seasons, as biases (not random errors), representative magnitudes:
>
> | Term | Magnitude | Period | Correctable? |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Deciduous canopy, DSM | 1–5 m | Annual | No; match season |
> | Ground-return bias under canopy, DTM | 0.05–0.3 m | Annual | Partly; density-stratified |
> | Crops, DSM | 0.5–3 m | Annual (crop calendar) | No; match stage |

> The first six rows dwarf the sensor's accuracy; the last five are at the sensor's accuracy and are routinely corrected. The middle rows are the ones most often forgotten.

## Software

**Open source:** xdem (Python: DEM co-registration on stable terrain, Nuth & Kääb and ICP methods, elevation-dependent bias correction, difference statistics by mask—the natural tool for separating seasonal from secular change); py4dgeo and CloudCompare M3C2 for point-cloud change with per-point uncertainty; PDAL (density rasters by class for ground-return stratification; `filters.returns`); GDAL/OGR (date rasters, masks, as in the Try-it); GRASS GIS and R (`terra`, `lidR` for canopy metrics by season); GNSS: RTKLIB (applies solid-Earth tide; ocean loading optional with BLQ files), GAMIT/GLOBK and Bernese (academic/licensed; full IERS loading models), CSRS-PPP and other online PPP services (free; IERS corrections applied); SPOTL and the Chalmers online OTL provider for ocean-tide-loading coefficients; Google Earth Engine for phenology (MODIS/Landsat NDVI, snow cover from MODIS and Sentinel-2) and water-level context (GSW). Caveat: xdem's co-registration will happily remove a real seasonal uplift if the "stable terrain" mask includes affected ground.

**Free but closed:** NOAA VDatum (tidal datums); USGS/NASA phenology and snow products (MODIS MOD10, VIIRS); ERA5 reanalysis (meteorology for refraction and tropospheric modelling, free with registration).

**Commercial:** Trimble Business Center and Leica Infinity (GNSS processing with tidal corrections); TerraSolid (strip adjustment, classification by season requires operator attention); PLS-CADD (conductor temperature, [Chapter 33](ch33-wires-and-thin-structures.md)); ENVI SARscape and GAMMA (InSAR tropospheric correction). Caveat: commercial GNSS packages differ in which loading terms are applied by default; read the processing options.

## Standards & guides

- **IERS Conventions (2010),** Petit & Luzum (eds.), IERS Technical Note 36, with online updates. Solid-Earth tide, ocean tide loading, atmospheric and pole-tide displacement models; tide-system definitions.
- **USGS Lidar Base Specification** (v2.1, 2020, with online revisions). Acquisition conditions: leaf-off preferred for bare-earth deliverables, snow-free, no flooding or standing water; requirement to document conditions and dates.
- **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023).** NVA and VVA separation; land-cover-stratified reporting.
- **IAG/IUGG Resolution 16 (1983) on the permanent tide**, and the IAG 2015 International Height Reference System resolution (zero-tide/mean-tide conventions for heights). Tide-system consistency between GNSS heights and geoid models.
- **NOAA NGS guidelines for GNSS-derived ellipsoid and orthometric heights** (NOS NGS 58 and 59). Occupation lengths and procedures that average tidal effects; required corrections.
- **ISO 19115-1:2014** (metadata) and **ISO 19157** (quality). Temporal extent and acquisition information elements; where condition metadata belongs.
- **NOAA NOS coastal mapping specifications** (e.g., NGS topobathy lidar requirements). Tide windows and water-level recording per flight line.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing a leaf-on DSM to a leaf-off DTM and reporting "growth."** Different products, different seasons. Detect by checking the product type and acquisition dates of both; avoid by comparing DSM to DSM or DTM to DTM in matched seasons.
- **Using a snow-season DEM as terrain.** Winter acquisition captured the snowpack. Detect by elevation-dependent positive differences against a summer DEM and by the acquisition date; avoid with a snow-free specification and a snow mask when it fails.
- **Ignoring Earth tides and loading in cm-level GNSS control.** Short occupations with simple processing carry up to ±20 cm of body tide. Detect by comparing with a PPP or network solution; avoid by applying IERS corrections or occupying long enough to average them.
- **A mosaic with hidden multi-year dates.** Tiles flown years apart, different seasons, no date layer. Detect by differences following tile boundaries; avoid by delivering a per-pixel or per-block date raster.
- **Trend from two epochs in different seasons.** The seasonal cycle aliases into the rate. Detect by asking what season each epoch was; avoid with three or more epochs or a seasonal model.
- **Co-registering on "stable terrain" that is not stable.** Fields, marsh, or clay soils included in the mask absorb a real seasonal signal into the registration. Detect by checking the mask against land cover; avoid by restricting to rock, pavement, and roofs.
- **Mixing tide systems between GNSS heights and the geoid model.** Latitude-dependent bias of centimetres. Detect by reading both products' tide-system statements; avoid by converting to one system before h → H.
- **Tillage or harvest between epochs read as erosion or deposition.** Decimetre changes across whole fields. Detect by field-boundary-shaped change patches; avoid by checking farm calendars and restricting to unmanaged ground.
- **Treating a post-ash or post-dune-migration DEM as permanent terrain.** The surface is transient. Detect by the date relative to the event; avoid by labelling event-affected areas.
- **Flying lidar under a low cloud base or through smoke.** Lost pulses and sparse ground under canopy, discovered only in processing. Detect from the density raster; avoid by logging cloud base and visibility and re-flying affected lines.
- **Assuming the tropospheric delay was handled because the GNSS solution converged.** Wet delay error maps 3:1 into height on humid days. Detect by comparing trajectory heights on repeated lines flown hours apart; avoid by estimating ZTD with appropriate constraints and checking against nearby CORS.

## Key takeaways

- Elevation has a season. Leaves, crops, tillage, snow, frozen ground, soil moisture, water level, and tides change the measured surface by amounts from centimetres to metres on daily to annual cycles, independent of any error.
- Separate measurement conditions (refraction, tropospheric delay, Earth tides, loading)—which are corrected with named models—from surface conditions (vegetation, snow, water, soil), which are recorded and respected.
- A winter DEM is a snow DEM; a leaf-on DEM is a canopy-biased DEM; a flood-season DEM is a water DEM. Say which one you have.
- Record acquisition conditions per flight line or block—date and time, vegetation state, snow, water levels (observed, not just predicted), meteorology, corrections applied—and deliver a per-pixel date raster for any mosaic.
- Solid-Earth tides reach ±20 cm and loading several centimetres; precise GNSS removes them with IERS models, but short uncorrected occupations do not, and tide-system mismatches between GNSS heights and geoid models bias heights by centimetres.
- Design change studies to compare like with like; where seasons cannot be matched, restrict to genuinely stable ground, use it to estimate the noise floor, and sample enough epochs to separate cycles from trends.
- Stratify accuracy statements by condition (leaf state, snow, land cover) and publish biases, not just RMSE.
