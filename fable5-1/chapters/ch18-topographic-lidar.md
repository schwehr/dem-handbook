# Chapter 18 — Topographic lidar

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** The first of the sensor deep dives: laser ranging from aircraft, drones, vehicles, tripods, and orbit, applying the ranging and footprint physics of [Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md) to the sensor that produces most modern DTMs.

**In this chapter.** Topographic lidar is the dominant source of high-resolution DSMs and DTMs on land, and its accuracy is set less by the laser than by the trajectory, the calibration, and the classification. You will be able to describe the system types (discrete-return, full-waveform, single-photon and Geiger-mode, photon-counting from orbit), write down the lidar georeferencing equation and propagate trajectory, boresight, lever-arm, range, and scan-angle errors through it, plan and interpret a boresight calibration and a strip adjustment, and read a project against the USGS Lidar Base Specification: density, spacing, overlap, intra- and inter-swath relative accuracy, NVA and VVA. You will understand why vegetation penetration and leaf-off timing govern DTM quality, why 1,064 nm lidar leaves voids on water, how terrestrial, mobile, and UAV scanning differ in their error budgets, and how to use ICESat-2 and GEDI as sparse but independent truth. The chapter ends with the deliverables and the QA checks that a competent reviewer runs before accepting a lidar project.

## 18.1 System types

An **airborne laser scanner (ALS)** combines three subsystems: a laser rangefinder, a scanning mechanism that sweeps the beam across the flight line, and a GNSS/INS position-and-orientation system (POS) that tells each pulse where it was fired from and in which direction ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), [Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)). Topographic lasers emit at 1,064 nm (Nd:YAG and its fibre equivalents) or 1,550 nm (eye-safer at higher power, more strongly absorbed by water and by wet surfaces), with pulse lengths of 1–5 ns, pulse repetition frequencies (PRF) of 100 kHz to 2 MHz or more per channel, and beam divergences of 0.2–0.5 mrad. The scanner is an oscillating mirror (sawtooth or sinusoidal pattern, with slow-down and point bunching at the swath edges), a rotating polygon (parallel lines, uniform density), a Palmer or nutating mirror (elliptical pattern, each point seen from two directions), or a fibre-optic array; drone and mobile systems increasingly use rotating multi-channel heads.

**Discrete-return** systems detect echoes in real time with analogue electronics and record up to 4–15 returns per pulse with a range, an intensity, and a return number. **Full-waveform** systems digitise the entire backscattered echo (typically at 1 GHz, so 15 cm range bins) and either decompose it into Gaussian components in post-processing (Wagner et al. 2006; Mallet & Bretar 2009) or store it for later analysis; they detect weaker and closer-spaced returns, give calibrated backscatter cross-sections, and allow re-processing when the detection rules change. **Single-photon lidar (SPL)** and **Geiger-mode lidar (GmL)** take sensitivity to its limit: instead of needing hundreds or thousands of photons per detection, they trigger on single photons, using arrays of detectors behind a diffractive beam splitter (SPL, e.g. the Leica SPL100 from 2017, 100 beamlets at 60 kHz each) or a focal-plane array of avalanche photodiodes in Geiger mode (GmL). The payoff is collection from 4–10 km altitude at tens of points per square metre; the cost is a noisy raw cloud (solar background and detector dark counts) that must be filtered statistically, a "blooming" of returns around bright targets, reduced penetration into dense canopy per pulse, and intensity that is not comparable with linear-mode systems (Degnan 2016; Stoker et al. 2016). **Photon-counting** from orbit (ICESat-2's ATLAS) is the same physics at 500 km (Section 18.8).

Three derived quantities matter for every system. **Footprint** is range × divergence ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md)): 0.3 mrad at 1,200 m is 36 cm, and the footprint's energy distribution weights the range measurement toward its centre. **Intensity** is the recorded return amplitude, uncalibrated in most discrete systems and dependent on range, incidence angle, atmosphere, and target reflectance; it is useful for classification and for seeing painted road markings, and it is not a reflectance product unless the vendor has radiometrically calibrated it. **Multiple-time-around (MTA) ambiguity** arises because at high PRF more than one pulse is in flight: the unambiguous range is $c/(2\,\text{PRF})$ — 150 m at 1 MHz — so at 1,500 m AGL ten pulses are airborne at once and the system must assign each return to the right outgoing pulse, using range gating, pulse coding, or a prior terrain model. MTA-zone boundaries are visible as artefacts when the assignment fails, especially over steep terrain or tall structures.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Point density" and "pulse density" differ by the number of returns per pulse. The USGS specification is written in **pulses** (aggregate nominal pulse density, ANPD, and nominal pulse spacing, NPS), counting first returns only, because returns multiply in vegetation and would otherwise flatter the density of a forested project. A vendor quoting 20 "points per square metre" over forest may be delivering 8 pulses; ask which, and compute it yourself from first returns in open areas.

## 18.2 The georeferencing equation and its error budget

Every lidar point is computed, not measured, from the sensor's range and scan angle, the POS trajectory, and the mounting parameters. In the mapping frame $m$ (an ECEF or a local-level frame) the coordinates of a return are

$$\mathbf{p}^m = \mathbf{p}^m_{\text{GNSS}}(t) + \mathbf{R}^m_{b}(t)\left[\mathbf{l}^{b} + \mathbf{R}^{b}_{s}\,\mathbf{r}^{s}(t)\right],$$

where $\mathbf{p}^m_{\text{GNSS}}(t)$ is the antenna (or IMU) position at the pulse time, $\mathbf{R}^m_b(t)$ the rotation from the body (IMU) frame to the mapping frame built from roll, pitch, and heading, $\mathbf{l}^b$ the **lever arm** from the IMU to the scanner origin in the body frame, $\mathbf{R}^b_s$ the **boresight** rotation from the scanner frame to the body frame (three small, constant angles), and $\mathbf{r}^s(t) = \rho\,[\sin\theta,\ 0,\ -\cos\theta]^T$ (for a cross-track scanner) the range vector of length $\rho$ at encoder angle $\theta$ in the scanner frame. The equation is deceptively short; the error budget is in its time dependence and its products.

| Component | Typical error (1σ) | Propagates as | Effect on height at 1,500 m AGL |
|---|---|---|---|
| GNSS position (PPK, good geometry) | 2–5 cm H, 3–8 cm V | additive | 3–8 cm |
| Roll / pitch (navigation-grade IMU, post-processed) | 0.003–0.008° | $\rho\sin\theta\,\delta\omega$ at scan angle $\theta$ | 1–4 cm at 20° |
| Heading | 0.005–0.02° | horizontal, $\rho\sin\theta\,\delta\kappa$ | ~0 vertical; 3–20 cm horizontal at swath edge |
| Boresight residual (after calibration) | 0.002–0.005° | like attitude but constant per installation | 1–2 cm; shows as swath-to-swath tilt |
| Lever arm | 1–2 cm per axis | additive | 1–2 cm |
| Range (hard target) | 1–2 cm | additive; worse on slopes/vegetation | 1–2 cm |
| Scan-angle encoder | 0.001–0.003° | like roll | < 1 cm |
| Timing / synchronisation | 10–50 µs | velocity × Δt, attitude rate × Δt | 0.1–0.5 cm (70 m/s); more if attitude rates high |
| Atmospheric refraction / group delay | ~0.3 mm/m uncorrected | scale | < 1 cm if modelled; 40 cm if ignored at 1,500 m |
| Combined (RSS, open flat ground) | | | ≈ 5–10 cm |

Two features of the table deserve emphasis. First, the attitude terms multiply range, so the same IMU on a drone at 80 m contributes 20 times less height error than on an aircraft at 1,500 m — which is why drone lidar with a tactical IMU can match crewed ALS with a navigation-grade one. Second, the attitude and boresight terms scale with $\sin\theta$ and so vanish at nadir and grow toward the swath edge; the swath edges are where calibration errors live, where overlaps are evaluated, and where specifications limit the usable field of view (the USGS LBS limits the scan angle so that off-nadir degradation stays within the accuracy class). Timing errors convert through the platform velocity and, more dangerously, through attitude rates in turns, which is why points collected in turns are excluded.

## 18.3 Calibration and strip adjustment

Lever arms are measured by terrestrial survey of the installation (total station to targets on the IMU and scanner housings) to about 1 cm; boresight angles cannot be measured mechanically to the required 0.001–0.005° and are instead **calibrated in flight**. The classic **boresight site** is flat, open terrain with gabled buildings or other sloped planar features, flown in opposing directions along one line and in a perpendicular pair, at two heights. Each boresight angle has a signature: a **roll** error tilts the swath about the flight line, so opposing lines show a cross-track height difference that grows linearly from nadir to the edge; a **pitch** error displaces points along track, visible on roof ridges and slopes as an along-track shift reversing sign between opposing lines; a **heading** error rotates the swath, producing horizontal displacements at the swath edges that reverse between opposing lines and scale with range. Range offset and scale are checked against surveyed targets or the height of flat control surfaces flown at two altitudes.

Modern practice replaces manual signature-reading with **least-squares calibration** of all mounting parameters (and, for some scanners, encoder and range corrections) using correspondences in overlapping strips (Skaloud & Lichti 2006; Habib et al. 2010). The same machinery, applied with the trajectory itself allowed to deform (per-strip offsets and drifts, or time-dependent corrections to the IMU-derived attitude), is **strip adjustment** (Glira et al. 2015): planar patches or ICP-style point-to-plane correspondences are found in all overlaps, residuals are minimised, and ground control is added to fix the absolute datum. A well-adjusted block shows inter-strip height differences on flat hard surfaces with a mean near zero and an RMS of 2–5 cm and no spatial pattern; a block with residual roll shows cross-track ramps, residual pitch shows displacement on slopes, and a block with GNSS problems shows per-strip vertical steps and, in long strips, slow undulations. Strip adjustment is powerful enough to be dangerous: it can absorb a systematic sensor error into trajectory corrections that look plausible, and a project should report the calibration parameters, the adjustment model, and the before/after overlap statistics so that a reviewer can tell what was corrected.

> **Case file.** A recurring artefact in airborne lidar DEMs is "**corduroy**" — regular across-track ridges of 2–5 cm amplitude at the scan-line spacing, visible in hillshades of flat ground such as airport aprons and lake beds. The cause is a residual between the forward and backward scan directions of an oscillating mirror: a small timing offset between the encoder and the range measurement, or a scanner-specific angular lag, displaces alternate scan lines in opposite directions. It is invisible in point statistics (the RMS is within specification) and obvious in a hillshade. Vendors correct it with a scanner-specific latency or encoder correction; reviewers detect it by hillshading flat areas at low sun angles and by computing the along-track autocorrelation of residuals from a plane. The lesson is that relative accuracy is a *spatial* property, and that the hillshade is a validation tool ([Chapter 57](ch57-visualizing-dems.md)).

## 18.4 Density, spacing, overlap, relative accuracy, and quality levels

The USGS **Lidar Base Specification (LBS)** organises project requirements into **quality levels**. The current figures (2024 rev. A; the online specification is revised more often than printed, so verify) are:

| Quality level | ANPD (pulses/m²) | NPS (m) | RMSEz, non-vegetated (cm) | NVA at 95 % (cm) | VVA, 95th percentile (cm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| QL0 | ≥ 8 | ≤ 0.35 | ≤ 5 | ≤ 9.8 | ≤ 15 |
| QL1 | ≥ 8 | ≤ 0.35 | ≤ 10 | ≤ 19.6 | ≤ 30 |
| QL2 | ≥ 2 | ≤ 0.71 | ≤ 10 | ≤ 19.6 | ≤ 30 |
| QL3 | ≥ 0.5 | ≤ 1.41 | ≤ 20 | ≤ 39.2 | ≤ 60 |

**Aggregate nominal pulse density (ANPD)** is the first-return density over the whole project including swath overlap, assessed on open terrain in cells of a specified size (density is to be met in at least 90 % of cells, excluding water and acceptable voids); **nominal pulse spacing** is $1/\sqrt{\text{ANPD}}$. Density is a *sampling* property, not a resolution: a QL2 project at 2 pulses/m² supports a 1 m DEM but resolves nothing smaller than about 1.5–2 m, and under canopy where only a fraction of pulses reach the ground the effective ground density may be 0.1–0.5 pulses/m² ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).

**Swath overlap** is required (the LBS asks for no data gaps and a nominal sidelap, with 10–30 % typical in practice) so that the project has no holes at the edges of swaths, where roll and turbulence move the swath boundary, and so that **relative accuracy** can be measured. The LBS distinguishes **intra-swath** (within a swath: the "smooth-surface repeatability," measured as the range of heights of points on a hard flat surface in a small window, or more recently the RMS difference between the two scan directions) and **inter-swath** accuracy (between overlapping swaths, measured as the RMS difference, RMSDz, of surfaces built from each swath on open hard ground, and its maximum). For QL0–QL2 the thresholds are of the order of 6 cm intra-swath and 8 cm RMSDz / 16 cm maximum inter-swath, and double those for QL3. Relative accuracy is reported as a map (a raster of swath-to-swath differences, with its histogram), because a project that meets the RMS threshold while hiding a 15 cm tilt in one corner has a calibration problem that the summary statistic conceals.

**Absolute accuracy** in the LBS follows the ASPRS Edition 1 (2014) model ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)): the **non-vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA)** is $1.96 \times \text{RMSE}_z$ on checkpoints in open terrain and urban hard surfaces, where errors are approximately Gaussian; the **vegetated vertical accuracy (VVA)** is the 95th percentile of absolute errors on checkpoints in vegetated classes, where they are not. ASPRS Edition 2 (2023; Version 2, 2024) drops the 95 % multipliers and reports RMSE$_z$ directly for both classes, with a 3 × RMSE outlier threshold; read which edition a specification invokes before comparing numbers. The checkpoints must be surveyed independently (static or RTK GNSS, [Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)) to at least three times the accuracy being tested, be at least 30 per land-cover class, and lie on flat or gently sloping ground so that horizontal error does not leak into the vertical test.

## 18.5 Vegetation, leaf-off timing, ground returns, and water

Lidar's defining advantage over photogrammetry is that a fraction of each pulse's energy passes through gaps in the canopy and returns from the ground. The fraction depends on canopy closure, leaf state, footprint, pulse energy, and detector sensitivity. In temperate deciduous forest in **leaf-off** conditions the ground-return fraction is typically 30–60 %; in **leaf-on** it falls to 5–20 %; under dense conifer canopy or tropical forest it can be 1–5 % in any season. Because a DTM is interpolated from ground returns, the effective ground sampling under canopy is the pulse density times this fraction, and DTM error under forest is dominated not by the lidar's range precision but by interpolation across gaps of metres to tens of metres and by misclassification of low vegetation as ground ([Chapter 30](ch30-point-cloud-classification.md), [Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)). Hodgson & Bresnahan (2004) found vegetated RMSEz two to three times the open-ground figure in a mixed landscape, and larger ratios are common in dense cover. National specifications therefore require or strongly prefer leaf-off acquisition for DTM-grade projects, together with snow-free and low-water conditions ([Chapter 36](ch36-seasonal-variability.md)). A leaf-on acquisition can be excellent for forestry and for a DSM and should not be sold as DTM-grade without evidence from vegetated checkpoints.

Ground-return statistics are a QA product in their own right: a map of the fraction of pulses with a ground-classified return, or of the distance from each DTM cell to the nearest ground return, shows where the DTM is measured and where it is interpolated, and it belongs in the deliverable.

**Water** is the other void. At 1,064 nm and especially at 1,550 nm, absorption by water is strong ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md)) and the surface is specular: near nadir a glint can return a strong echo from the surface, off nadir the energy is reflected away and nothing comes back. The result is that lakes, rivers, and the sea are mostly empty in a topographic lidar point cloud, with a scattering of surface returns near nadir and noise. DTMs over water are therefore **hydro-flattened** or otherwise constructed, not measured ([Chapter 34](ch34-water-in-dems.md)). Wet surfaces — tidal flats, saturated soil, wet asphalt — return weakly and create dropouts that look like voids and are sometimes filled with low-reflectance noise; bathymetric lidar at 532 nm is the remedy where the water is clear enough ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)).

## 18.6 Terrestrial and mobile laser scanning

**Terrestrial laser scanning (TLS)** puts the scanner on a tripod and removes the trajectory problem entirely: each scan is internally consistent to the instrument's range noise (1–5 mm at tens of metres for phase or pulsed survey scanners; 5–10 mm at hundreds of metres for long-range pulsed units reaching 1–6 km). The error budget moves to **registration** — aligning multiple scans into one frame — and to georeferencing. Registration uses retro-reflective or spherical **targets** surveyed by total station or GNSS, or cloud-to-cloud (ICP) alignment on overlapping geometry, or both; a well-executed network closes to a few millimetres over a site, and registration residuals on targets should be reported like a traverse closure. Long-range scans of cliffs and slopes additionally suffer atmospheric refraction and range-dependent beam divergence (a 0.3 mrad beam at 1 km is 30 cm, so "resolution" on a distant cliff is not the point spacing), and oblique incidence makes range noise grow and surfaces appear to recede ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md)). The **ASTM E57** format (E2807) carries scans with their poses, images, and metadata; LAS/LAZ is used once the cloud is georeferenced ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)).

**Mobile laser scanning (MLS)** reintroduces the trajectory, at short range: scanners 2–3 m above the road with ranges of tens of metres, so attitude errors matter less and GNSS outages in **urban canyons** matter more ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md)). Typical open-sky accuracy is 2–5 cm vertical; in canyons the solution drifts to decimetres between fixes and must be pulled back with surveyed control targets (painted pavement marks, checkerboards) observed in the cloud. MLS adjustment software applies the same strip-adjustment ideas as ALS, with the trajectory corrected piecewise between control observations. The products — road surfaces at thousands of points per square metre, curb lines, façades — are complementary to ALS, which sees roofs and back lots that MLS cannot; merging the two requires co-registration on shared hard surfaces ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).

## 18.7 UAV lidar

Drone lidar payloads span two orders of magnitude in price and in quality, and the price buys the IMU and the scanner's range precision more than anything else. **Survey-grade** payloads (Riegl VUX/miniVUX series, Teledyne Optech CL-360 and similar, with Applanix or NovAtel/Honeywell tactical IMUs) achieve 2–4 cm vertical RMSE on hard ground from 60–120 m AGL with PPK trajectories and a boresight calibration; **mid-grade** integrations of automotive scanners (Livox, Hesai, Ouster/Velodyne) with MEMS or lower tactical IMUs — including the DJI Zenmuse L1/L2 class — achieve 5–10 cm with good practice and 10–30 cm when flown fast, high, or with a cold IMU. The multi-channel heads of the latter produce an irregular, multi-directional point pattern (each point seen from several channels at different angles) that is forgiving in classification but hides calibration residuals; check strip overlaps on hard ground as rigorously as for ALS.

Flight planning follows the aircraft rules at small scale: 60–120 m AGL for density of 100–500 pts/m² at 5–10 m/s, 30–50 % sidelap, a cross-strip for calibration, and lines short enough that the IMU's heading does not drift between GNSS updates — with the added items that matter at this altitude: a figure-of-eight or dynamic alignment before and after each flight to initialise the IMU heading, avoidance of hovering (which starves the IMU of the acceleration it needs to observe heading), and attention to the eye-safety class and to Remote ID and airspace rules ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md)). PPK with a local base at a surveyed point gives a few centimetres; network RTK adds the network's datum and the radio link's dropouts. Typical accuracies reported in independent evaluations are 3–5 cm vertical on hard open ground for survey-grade payloads, which is as good as crewed ALS and at far higher density; the open question for every drone project is the datum tie (base-station coordinates, geoid model, [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)) and the checkpoints, not the scanner.

## 18.8 Spaceborne lidar as sparse truth

Three spaceborne lidars have measured the land surface. **ICESat/GLAS** (2003–2009) fired a 1,064 nm beam producing ~70 m footprints every ~170 m along track and recorded full waveforms; it was built for ice sheets but its land-surface elevations were used to evaluate SRTM and later global DEMs. **ICESat-2/ATLAS** (September 2018–) is a photon-counting system: a 532 nm laser at 10 kHz, split into six beams in three pairs (strong and weak, 90 m apart within a pair, 3.3 km between pairs), footprints of about 11–17 m with an along-track spacing of 0.7 m. The **ATL03** product is the geolocated photon cloud with per-photon confidence flags; **ATL06** is land-ice surface height from 40 m segments; **ATL08** is land and vegetation height, in which a density-based filter separates ground, canopy, and noise photons and reports terrain and canopy heights per 100 m segment (Neumann et al. 2019; Neuenschwander & Pitts 2019). Over flat, open ground ICESat-2 terrain heights agree with airborne lidar to about 0.1–0.5 m (bias of a few decimetres, RMSE below a metre, in most published comparisons), degrading with slope and canopy. **GEDI** (December 2018–) on the ISS fires 1,064 nm pulses in eight ground tracks of 25 m footprints spaced 60 m along and 600 m across track, between ±51.6° latitude; **L2A** gives ground elevation and relative height metrics per footprint from waveform processing, **L3** grids them at 1 km, and **L4A/L4B** give biomass (Dubayah et al. 2020). GEDI's ground elevation has geolocation uncertainty of about 10 m and vertical errors of a metre or so on flat ground, more under dense canopy.

These instruments are **sparse truth**: profiles, not grids, but with globally consistent, independently calibrated heights referenced to the ITRF ellipsoid and the WGS84/EGM2008 geoid as documented. They are the standard way to evaluate and co-register regional and global DEMs where airborne checkpoints are absent ([Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md), [Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)), and ICESat-2's 532 nm photons also reach shallow seabeds ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)). The caveats are that ATL08 "ground" is a statistical estimate from a 100 m segment, that geolocation errors of a few metres on slopes become height errors (slope × offset), and that snow, clouds, and seasonal canopy change what the photons return from.

## 18.9 Deliverables and QA

A complete airborne lidar project delivers: the **classified point cloud** in LAS 1.4 or LAZ (point data record format 6–10, with per-point GPS time in adjusted standard GPS time, swath/flight-line ID, scan angle, intensity, return number, and classification codes per ASPRS — ground = 2, low/medium/high vegetation = 3/4/5, building = 6, low noise = 7, water = 9, bridge deck = 17, high noise = 18, with the LBS prescribing which must be populated), tiled with no gaps and overlaps; the **DTM** (bare-earth, hydro-flattened per the LBS) and often the **DSM**, as GeoTIFF/COG; **intensity** images; **breaklines** used for hydro-flattening; the **trajectory** (SBET or equivalent) and the **calibration report**; **checkpoint tables** with NVA/VVA statistics; **swath overlap** difference rasters; a **project report** describing dates, conditions, sensors, processing, and the geoid and datum used ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md), [Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

The reviewer's checks, in the order they catch problems: (1) **coverage and voids** — classify voids as water, acceptable (low-reflectance), or unacceptable; (2) **density** — ANPD map on open terrain, 90 % of cells above threshold; (3) **relative accuracy** — intra- and inter-swath statistics and maps, hillshades of flat surfaces for corduroy and tilts; (4) **absolute accuracy** — NVA and VVA against independent checkpoints, plus a comparison of the DTM with any existing control; (5) **classification** — visual inspection of ground under vegetation, buildings misclassified as ground (flat roofs), bridges, vehicles, and "pits" and "spikes" from noise; (6) **DEM artefacts** — tile seams, interpolation across voids, hydro-flattening errors; (7) **metadata and lineage** — GPS time convention, CRS and geoid, LAS header consistency. A project that passes (4) and fails (3) or (5) will produce a DTM whose errors are spatially correlated and invisible to checkpoint statistics, which is why the order matters.

> **Try it.** Compute a swath-overlap difference check with PDAL and GDAL from a LAZ tile carrying flight-line IDs (PointSourceId): build one ground-only DTM per swath, difference them, and read the statistics on the overlap. On a well-calibrated project the mean should be within ±2 cm and the RMS below 5–8 cm on open hard ground.
>
> ```bash
> # 1. Split ground returns by swath (PointSourceId 101 and 102 here) and grid each at 1 m.
> for id in 101 102; do
>   pdal translate tile.laz swath_$id.tif \
>     --json="{\"pipeline\":[
>       {\"type\":\"filters.range\",\"limits\":\"Classification[2:2],PointSourceId[$id:$id]\"},
>       {\"type\":\"writers.gdal\",\"resolution\":1.0,\"output_type\":\"mean\",\"gdaldriver\":\"GTiff\",\"nodata\":-9999}]}"
> done
> # 2. Difference the two swath DTMs where both exist.
> gdal_calc.py -A swath_101.tif -B swath_102.tif --outfile=diff.tif \
>   --calc="(A-B)*((A!=-9999)&(B!=-9999)) + (-9999)*((A==-9999)|(B==-9999))" --NoDataValue=-9999
> # 3. Statistics and a histogram of the overlap differences.
> gdalinfo -stats -hist diff.tif | grep -E "STATISTICS_(MEAN|STDDEV|MINIMUM|MAXIMUM)"
> ```
>
> Expected: STATISTICS_MEAN near 0.00 and STATISTICS_STDDEV of 0.03–0.06 (m) on a flat, open overlap; a mean of several centimetres indicates a vertical offset between swaths, and a difference raster that ramps across track indicates a residual roll or boresight error. Hillshade `diff.tif` to see the pattern.

## Then & now

The laser was demonstrated in 1960 and the first laser ranging and "lidar" experiments followed in 1961 ⟨H⟩. Airborne laser profiling — a single nadir beam, no scanner, positioned by ground tracking — was flown through the 1960s–1980s; Krabill et al. (1984) reported sub-decimetre agreement with ground survey from a NASA profiler and foresaw the GNSS-positioned scanners that arrived with commercial ALS in the mid-1990s (Optech ALTM, Saab TopEye, and the Azimuth/Leica systems), once GPS and IMUs could be integrated at the required accuracy. The ASPRS **LAS** format (version 1.0 in 2003) ⟨H⟩ gave the industry a common point-cloud container; LAZ compression (Isenburg 2013) made national archives feasible. National programs made lidar infrastructure: the Netherlands' AHN from 1997, England's Environment Agency surveys from 1998, the USGS 3DEP from 2012–2014, swissSURFACE3D and dozens of others in the 2010s. Full-waveform digitisers (2004 onward) and single-photon and Geiger-mode systems (2015–2017 onward) raised altitude and density; ICESat-2 and GEDI (2018) ⟨H⟩ put photon-counting and waveform lidar in orbit. In the 2020s drone lidar is everywhere, from survey-grade payloads to sub-$10,000 integrations, and the field's limiting factors have shifted from the sensor to the trajectory, the classification, and the datum.

## Mathematics

**Georeferencing.** With the notation of Section 18.2, $\mathbf{p}^m = \mathbf{p}^m_{\text{GNSS}} + \mathbf{R}^m_b\,(\mathbf{l}^b + \mathbf{R}^b_s\,\mathbf{r}^s)$. Writing $\mathbf{R}^m_b = \mathbf{R}_z(\kappa)\mathbf{R}_y(\varphi)\mathbf{R}_x(\omega)$ for heading, pitch, and roll (conventions differ between vendors and must be checked), and $\mathbf{r}^s = \rho\,[\sin\theta, 0, -\cos\theta]^T$, the linearised error in the ground point for small errors in each parameter is

$$\delta\mathbf{p} = \delta\mathbf{p}_{\text{GNSS}} + \mathbf{R}^m_b\,\delta\mathbf{l} + \left[\mathbf{R}^m_b(\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{R}^b_s\mathbf{r}^s)\right]_\times^{T}\delta\boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{R}^m_b\left[\mathbf{R}^b_s\mathbf{r}^s\right]_\times^{T}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{R}^m_b\mathbf{R}^b_s\left(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,\delta\rho + \rho\,\partial_\theta\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,\delta\theta\right),$$

where $[\cdot]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric (cross-product) matrix, $\delta\boldsymbol{\omega}$ the attitude errors, $\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}$ the boresight errors, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ the unit range vector. For level flight and a cross-track scanner the vertical components reduce to

$$\sigma_z^2 \approx \sigma_{z,\text{GNSS}}^2 + \sigma_{l_z}^2 + (\rho\sin\theta)^2\,(\sigma_\omega^2 + \sigma_{\beta_\omega}^2) + (\rho\sin\theta\,\tan\theta)^2\,\sigma_\theta^2 + \cos^2\theta\,\sigma_\rho^2 + \ldots,$$

(Baltsavias 1999; Glennie 2007; Schenk 2001 give the full forms). With $\rho = 1{,}600$ m at $\theta = 20°$, $\sigma_\omega = 0.005° = 87$ µrad, $\sigma_{\beta_\omega} = 0.003°$, $\sigma_\rho = 0.02$ m, $\sigma_{z,\text{GNSS}} = 0.05$ m, $\sigma_{l_z} = 0.01$ m: the attitude term is $1600 \times 0.342 \times \sqrt{87^2 + 52^2}\times10^{-6} = 0.055$ m, and $\sigma_z \approx \sqrt{0.05^2 + 0.01^2 + 0.055^2 + 0.019^2} = 0.077$ m. At nadir the attitude term vanishes and $\sigma_z \approx 0.055$ m; this difference between nadir and edge is the physical reason relative accuracy is assessed in overlaps.

**Range precision.** For a leading-edge or constant-fraction detector on a pulse of rise time $t_r$ with signal-to-noise ratio SNR, the timing jitter is $\sigma_t \approx t_r/\text{SNR}$ and $\sigma_\rho = c\,\sigma_t/2$; with $t_r = 1$ ns and SNR = 10, $\sigma_\rho = 1.5$ cm. Waveform fitting improves on this by using the whole pulse. On a surface inclined at angle $\alpha$ to the beam, the footprint $D$ spreads the return over a range interval $D\tan\alpha$, broadening the pulse and biasing the detection toward the near edge; this "slope-induced" range error is $\sim D\tan\alpha/2$ in the worst case — 15 cm for a 0.5 m footprint on a 30° slope — and is the physical floor of lidar accuracy on steep terrain.

**Footprint and density.** Footprint diameter $D = \rho\,\gamma$ for divergence $\gamma$. For a scanner with PRF $f$, swath width $W = 2h\tan(\Theta/2)$ at height $h$ and full field of view $\Theta$, and ground speed $v$, the single-swath pulse density is $f/(W v)$, and across-track spacing $W/(f/f_{\text{scan}})$ varies with the scan pattern. Points per square metre is not resolution: the smallest resolvable feature is governed by $\max(D, \text{NPS})$ and, after gridding, by the interpolation window ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).

**Timing.** A synchronisation error $\delta t$ between the POS and the scanner displaces points by $v\,\delta t$ along track and by $\rho\,\dot{\omega}\,\delta t$ for attitude rate $\dot{\omega}$; at $\dot{\omega} = 5°/\text{s}$ in a turn and $\delta t = 1$ ms, the latter is 14 cm at 1,600 m — which is why points in turns are discarded and why the scanner's time stamps are checked against the POS at the microsecond level ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)).

## Validation & uncertainty

Lidar validation has three layers, and a competent project reports all three.

**Internal (relative) consistency.** The cheapest and most sensitive check is the data against itself: inter-swath and intra-swath differences on open hard ground, mapped and summarised (Section 18.4). Compute the difference raster of per-swath DTMs in every overlap, its mean, RMS, and 95th percentile, and its spatial pattern; a cross-track ramp means roll/boresight, an along-track displacement on slopes means pitch or timing, periodic ridges mean scanner latency, and per-swath steps mean GNSS. The statistics should be computed on surfaces where the comparison is meaningful — flat, hard, unvegetated — and the areas used should be shown. Overlap statistics are not a substitute for absolute checks (the whole block can be offset by a geoid or base-station error with perfect overlaps), but they catch calibration failures that checkpoints cannot.

**External (absolute) accuracy.** Checkpoints surveyed independently with GNSS to ≤ 1/3 of the target accuracy, at least 30 per land-cover class and well distributed; NVA as $1.96\,\text{RMSE}_z$ in open terrain, VVA as the 95th percentile in vegetation, each reported with the bias (mean error) separately, because a 5 cm systematic offset is a datum problem and a 5 cm random scatter is a noise problem, and the two have different fixes ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)). Checkpoints should be compared to the DTM *and* to the nearest ground points or a local plane fit of the point cloud, to separate interpolation error from measurement error. Horizontal accuracy is harder to test and is usually inferred from the trajectory and attitude budget or from well-defined features (roof corners, painted markings in intensity).

**Spatial structure of error.** Errors in lidar DTMs are spatially correlated at the scale of swaths (calibration), of GNSS solutions (kilometres), and of canopy gaps (metres). A validation that reports only a global RMSE has assumed independence that does not exist, and downstream change detection ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) will be mis-calibrated by exactly the correlated part. Report, where possible, the semivariogram of checkpoint residuals or of overlap differences, and map residuals rather than tabulating them.

## Pitfalls

- **Point density mistaken for resolution** → a 1 m DEM from 2 pulses/m² advertised as "1 m resolution" → detect by computing footprint and ground-return spacing; report effective resolution ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md)).
- **Swath-edge and turn artefacts** → attitude errors grow with $\sin\theta$ and attitude rates in turns → detect by stratifying overlap differences by scan angle; clip the FOV and exclude turn data.
- **"Corduroy" from scanner latency or encoder lag** → within specification by RMS, obvious in hillshades → detect by hillshading flat areas; fix with the scanner's latency correction.
- **Vegetated accuracy reported with Gaussian statistics** → RMSE understates the tails of interpolation and misclassification errors → use the 95th percentile (VVA) and report ground-return fraction.
- **Leaf-on acquisition sold as DTM-grade** → ground-return fraction of 5–20 % and metre-scale interpolation gaps → check acquisition dates against phenology; require vegetated checkpoints.
- **GPS-time convention errors** → GPS week seconds versus adjusted standard GPS time (−10⁹ s) mislabelled in the LAS header → detect by range of time values and the global-encoding bit; fix header, not data.
- **Classification codes reinterpreted between vendors or versions** → class 7/18 noise, class 9 water, "model keypoints" and overlap flags used differently → detect by reading the project report and the class histogram; renormalise before merging.
- **Strip adjustment absorbing sensor errors** → trajectory corrections that look plausible but hide a range scale or encoder error → demand calibration parameters and before/after statistics; test on an independent block.
- **Water voids filled silently** → hydro-flattening or interpolation across lakes and rivers presented as measured elevations → check the void/breakline deliverable; flag interpolated cells.
- **Base-station or geoid errors offsetting the whole block** → perfect overlaps, systematic checkpoint bias → always include independent checkpoints and report the geoid model and base coordinates.
- **Drone IMU heading drift from hovering or short lines** → per-line yaw errors and horizontal smear → plan dynamic alignment and cross strips; check overlaps on buildings.
- **Intensity treated as reflectance** → uncorrected for range and angle, inconsistent between swaths and sensors → use intensity for classification and visualisation only unless radiometrically calibrated.

## Key takeaways

- Lidar accuracy is a trajectory-and-calibration problem more than a laser problem: GNSS, attitude × range, boresight, and lever arm dominate the budget; the range term is a few centimetres.
- Attitude and boresight errors scale with range and $\sin\theta$, so swath edges and high altitudes are where errors live and where overlaps must be checked.
- The ground in a DTM is a classification decision made on a fraction of pulses; vegetation and leaf state determine DTM quality far more than the sensor's specification sheet.
- Overlap statistics (intra- and inter-swath) are the cheapest, most sensitive check and should be mapped, not just summarised; checkpoints provide the absolute tie that overlaps cannot.
- NIR lidar does not measure water; voids over water are physics, and hydro-flattened surfaces are constructed, not measured.
- Density is sampling, footprint is resolution; USGS quality levels specify both, and effective resolution is coarser than either.
- Terrestrial scanning moves the error to registration; mobile scanning moves it to the trajectory in urban canyons; drone lidar moves it to the IMU and the datum tie.
- ICESat-2 and GEDI are sparse, independent, globally consistent truth — use them to check regional DEMs, but understand what their "ground" is.
- Report the full budget and the lineage: calibration, adjustment, overlap, checkpoints, classification, datum, and time convention.
