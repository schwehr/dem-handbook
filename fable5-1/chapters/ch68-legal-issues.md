# Chapter 68 — Legal issues

> **Part XV — Law, policy, security, privacy, ethics.** The first of two chapters on the social rules that attach to elevation data: here, the rules that have the force of law — boundaries, datums written into statute, licences, liability, licensure, contracts, evidence, and export control.

**In this chapter.** Elevation numbers acquire legal meaning the moment they define where a border runs, whether a house needs flood insurance, who may sign a plat, or whether a product may leave a country. This chapter explains how low-water lines and 2,500 m isobaths become maritime boundaries under UNCLOS; how *mean high water* became a legal quantity defined by an 18.6-year tidal epoch; which datums and units are written into law and what happened when the US survey foot was retired; how public-domain, attribution, share-alike, and non-commercial licences differ in what they let you build; who may certify a survey and what "standard of care" means when a chart or flood map is wrong; how a procurement specification becomes a contract term; what makes a DEM admissible as evidence; and which equipment and data are export-controlled or nationally restricted. After reading it you should be able to spot the legal questions in a project before they become disputes, and to document provenance as if it will be read aloud in court.

> **This is not legal advice.** The authors are geodesists, hydrographers, and engineers, not lawyers. Statutes, regulations, case law, and licence texts change and vary by jurisdiction; everything below is a map of the terrain, not a route through it. Consult qualified counsel in the relevant jurisdiction before relying on any statement here.

## 68.1 Boundaries defined by elevation and water

### 68.1.1 Maritime baselines and zones under UNCLOS

The 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS; in force 1994) is the most consequential legal text that depends on a vertical datum. The 12 nmi territorial sea, 24 nmi contiguous zone, and 200 nmi exclusive economic zone (EEZ) are all measured from the **baseline**, and Article 5 defines the **normal baseline** as "the low-water line along the coast as marked on large-scale charts officially recognized by the coastal State." Three things in that sentence matter to a DEM practitioner. The datum of the low-water line is whatever the state's charts use — lowest astronomical tide (LAT) in most IHO member states, mean lower low water (MLLW) in the United States ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)) — so a state that lowers its chart datum moves its baseline seaward. The operative line is the one *on the official chart*, not the one you would derive today from a topobathy lidar survey; a better survey has no legal effect until the chart is updated and the baseline published and deposited with the UN (Article 16). And "large-scale" is undefined; a 1:50,000 shoreline generalizes a low-water line by tens of metres.

Article 7 permits **straight baselines** on deeply indented or island-fringed coasts; Article 10 closes bays; Article 13 lets a **low-tide elevation** — dry at low tide, submerged at high — serve as a baseline point if it lies within the territorial-sea breadth of land. That rule makes a drying rock worth up to 12 nmi of territorial sea, which is why the vertical accuracy of a feature a few decimetres above chart datum can be worth a great deal of seabed. Article 121 distinguishes **islands**, which generate an EEZ and continental shelf, from **rocks** that "cannot sustain human habitation or economic life of their own," which generate only a territorial sea; the 2016 South China Sea arbitral award (*Philippines v. China*, PCA Case 2013-19) applied the test feature by feature and held that building up a low-tide elevation does not change its legal status.

**Article 76** defines the outer limit of the continental shelf beyond 200 nmi and is where bathymetry becomes a cartographic-legal exercise in its own right. The coastal State may extend its shelf to the outer edge of the continental margin, located by either the **Gardiner formula** (sediment thickness at least 1 % of the distance to the foot of the slope) or the **Hedberg formula** (60 nmi from the foot of the slope), capped by the more favourable of 350 nmi from the baseline or 100 nmi from the **2,500 m isobath**. The **foot of the continental slope** (FOS) is, "in the absence of evidence to the contrary," the point of maximum change in gradient at its base. Every term depends on a bathymetric surface and its derivatives: the 2,500 m isobath moves with the sound-speed profile and the gridding method ([Chapter 20](ch20-sonar.md), [Chapter 31](ch31-interpolation-and-gridding.md)), and the FOS is a second-derivative quantity exquisitely sensitive to smoothing. The IHO/IAG/IOC *Manual on Technical Aspects of the UNCLOS* (the **TALOS Manual**, IHO C-51, Edition 5.0.0, 2014) is the practitioner's guide, and the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS) *Scientific and Technical Guidelines* (CLCS/11, 1999) set the evidentiary expectations: well-controlled bathymetry, documented sound-speed corrections, gradient analysis of the slope.

> **Worked example.** *How much seabed does a bathymetric bias buy?* Suppose a margin has a regional seabed gradient of 1:200 (0.29°) in the depth range around 2,500 m. A systematic depth bias of +10 m (depths reported too deep, for example from an unrepresentative sound-speed profile in a 2,500 m water column where a 0.4 % velocity error is entirely plausible) shifts the apparent 2,500 m isobath landward by $10 \text{ m} \times 200 = 2{,}000$ m ≈ 1.1 nmi. If the depth-constraint line (isobath + 100 nmi) is the controlling limit along 300 km of margin, the lost area is roughly $2 \text{ km} \times 300 \text{ km} = 600$ km². The opposite sign of bias gives the same gain, which is why CLCS reviewers ask to see the sound-speed casts. On a gentler 1:1,000 slope the same bias is worth 10 km of line.

### 68.1.2 The ambulatory shoreline and sea-level rise

Under customary interpretation a normal baseline is **ambulatory**: it moves with the low-water line. That was harmless while coasts prograded and is a problem now that they are being drowned: a low-lying atoll state could see its baselines retreat and its islands reclassified as rocks, shrinking its EEZ. The International Law Association's Committee on International Law and Sea Level Rise (2012–2018) and the International Law Commission's study group (from 2019) have both leaned toward letting states keep baselines and outer limits once properly published, and the Pacific Islands Forum's 2021 *Declaration on Preserving Maritime Zones* asserts exactly that. For practitioners the consequence is that the *survey of record* from which published baselines were derived becomes a permanent legal artefact whose provenance, datum, epoch, and uncertainty ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)) must be preserved indefinitely.

### 68.1.3 Tidal boundaries in property law

On land the same question arises at the scale of a backyard. In common-law jurisdictions the boundary between private upland and public tidelands is generally the ordinary high-water mark, and the US Supreme Court fixed its meaning in *Borax Consolidated, Ltd. v. City of Los Angeles*, 296 U.S. 10 (1935) ⟨H⟩: the boundary is the line of **mean high water** (MHW), "the average height of all the high waters" at the place, averaged over the 18.6-year lunar nodal cycle — the **National Tidal Datum Epoch** (NTDE) in modern NOAA practice ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)). A legal word became a statistical quantity with a defined averaging period, which is why a surveyor retracing a tidal boundary must know which NTDE (1960–1978, 1983–2001, or the forthcoming epoch) the elevation refers to and must transfer the datum from the nearest control station by a documented simultaneous-observation comparison (Shalowitz 1962; Hicks 1985). States vary: most follow MHW; Massachusetts, Maine, New Hampshire, Delaware, Pennsylvania, and Virginia use **mean low water** (MLW) under colonial ordinances; Texas uses the civil-law line of higher high tide for Spanish-grant lands (*Luttes v. State*, 1958); Hawaii uses the vegetation or debris line. The MHW–MLW difference is the mean range — about 1.3 m at Boston, 0.5 m at Galveston — which on a 1:100 beach face is 130 m and 50 m of horizontal boundary.

> **Definitions that bite.** *Mean high water* is a tidal datum defined by averaging, and so is *mean higher high water* (MHHW); on a mixed semidiurnal coast such as California's they differ by roughly 0.2–0.3 m. The *ordinary high-water mark* used by the US Army Corps of Engineers for Clean Water Act jurisdiction in non-tidal waters is a physical indicator (shelving, vegetation change, litter line; 33 CFR 328.3(c)(7)), not a datum at all. Federal Emergency Management Agency flood maps use neither. A single project can legitimately involve MHW (title), OHWM (permit), and the base flood elevation (insurance) on the same stretch of shore, all at different heights.

### 68.1.4 Rivers, ridges, and lakes

Boundaries along rivers commonly follow the **thalweg** — the line of deepest continuous channel, or in some treaties the centreline of the main navigable channel — and move with it when change is gradual (**accretion**) but stay put when change is sudden (**avulsion**) (*Nebraska v. Iowa*, 1892). Both tests presuppose that someone can prove, with surveys of record, where the channel was and when it moved; repeated bathymetry and historical DEMs ([Chapter 40](ch40-erosion-and-geomorphic-change.md), [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)) are the evidence. Mountain borders defined by the **watershed** or **ridgeline** move when ice does: Italy and Austria made their glaciated border "mobile" in 2006, and Switzerland and Italy agreed in 2023–2024 to redraw a stretch near the Matterhorn after the Theodul glacier's retreat moved the divide. State boundaries in lakes are usually fixed coordinate lines, which a lakebed DEM cannot move but which inherit the datum problem of [Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md): a NAD27 line reprojected to NAD83 without a datum shift is off by up to ~100 m in the western US.

## 68.2 Vertical datums and units in law

Legal instruments freeze elevations in whatever datum and unit were current when they were written, and the instruments outlive the datums. US deeds and plats from before the 1990s cite the National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929 (NGVD29) — often as "mean sea level," which it was not; NAVD88 superseded it in 1991–1993 and the difference ranges from about −0.4 m to +1.5 m across the conterminous US. The National Geodetic Survey's **NSRS modernization** will replace NAVD88 with the North American–Pacific Geopotential Datum of 2022 (NAPGD2022), with a beta release expected in 2026 and formal adoption still pending, shifting heights by up to roughly a metre in parts of the west, with transformation grids rather than exact conversions; every ordinance that cites a NAVD88 elevation will then need a transformation and its uncertainty attached.

Units are a second layer. Until the end of 2022 the United States had two feet: the **international foot** (0.3048 m exactly) and the **US survey foot** (1200/3937 m ≈ 0.304800609601 m). The difference is 2 parts per million — negligible for elevations but about 1 m in State Plane northings with large false origins. NIST and NGS announced in the Federal Register (October 2019) that the US survey foot would be deprecated effective 31 December 2022; states are amending statutes at their own pace and legacy plats will carry the old unit forever. The practical rule: never accept "feet" on a coordinate list without asking which foot, and store the answer in metadata ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

**Grid versus ground** is a third. State Plane and UTM coordinates are projected, so distances between coordinates differ from distances measured on the ground by the combined scale factor (grid scale × elevation factor), typically within ±1:10,000 but up to 1:2,500 at altitude. Plats that mix ground distances with grid coordinates without stating the factor are a standing source of litigation; low-distortion projections exist largely to make the two agree.

Regulatory elevations build on the datums. The **base flood elevation** (BFE) on a FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Map is the modeled 1 %-annual-chance water-surface elevation in NAVD88; a structure's lowest-floor elevation relative to BFE, certified by a licensed surveyor or engineer on the **Elevation Certificate** (FEMA Form FF-206-FY-22-152), sets National Flood Insurance Program premiums and permit eligibility. A 0.3 m error in the certified elevation can change premiums by thousands of dollars a year and ground a negligence claim. Under 14 CFR Part 77 the FAA defines obstruction surfaces around airports; proponents of structures penetrating them or exceeding 200 ft above ground must file Form 7460-1, which asks for site elevation and height in **feet above mean sea level** with an accuracy code defined in AC 70/7460-2 (code 1A is ±20 ft horizontal / ±3 ft vertical). Filing in the wrong datum or with "ground" heights read from a coarse national DEM is a documented cause of obstacle-database errors ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)).

Building height limits illustrate how a legal elevation can be ambiguous without a datum at all. Zoning codes measure height from "grade," defined variously as the average finished grade along the building perimeter, the lowest adjacent grade, the curb level at the centre of the frontage, or a pre-development natural grade — and whether a DTM or an as-built survey establishes that grade is often the whole dispute. Solar-access and view ordinances create rights that are lines of sight over a DSM, not a DTM. Air rights (transferable development rights above a parcel) and mineral rights by depth (severed estates defined below a stated elevation or depth from surface) are three-dimensional parcels whose boundaries are elevations ([Chapter 63](ch63-buildings-cities-innerspace.md)).

## 68.3 Licensing and intellectual property

### 68.3.1 Public domain, Crown copyright, and open licences

Works of the US federal government are not subject to domestic copyright (17 U.S.C. §105), so USGS 3DEP lidar, NOAA bathymetry and charts, and SRTM are in the public domain in the United States; agencies may still ask for attribution, and the OPEN Government Data Act (Title II of the Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act, 2018) requires federal data to be published as open, machine-readable data by default. The same is not true of state or local governments (some claim copyright, some do not), of federal *contractors* (whose deliverables may carry restrictions unless the contract assigns them), or of any other national government. The United Kingdom's Crown copyright is licensed through the **Open Government Licence v3**, under which the Environment Agency's national lidar programme is released; Ordnance Survey's premium products are not.

Among global DEMs the licensing is a minefield that matters for anyone building a product. **Copernicus DEM** GLO-30 and GLO-90 are released under a licence from ESA/Airbus that permits free use including commercial use with attribution ("© DLR e.V. 2010–2014 and © Airbus Defence and Space GmbH 2014–2018 provided under COPERNICUS by the European Union and ESA; all rights reserved") but with specific restrictions in the licence text, while the 10 m EEA-10 tier is restricted to eligible European users; the **TanDEM-X 12 m** DEM is a commercial product of DLR/Airbus, with science proposals possible. **ALOS World 3D-30 m (AW3D30)** from JAXA is free with attribution and a requirement to accept JAXA's terms. **FABDEM** (Hawker et al. 2022) is released under **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** — non-commercial, share-alike — and **CoastalDEM** (Climate Central) is non-commercial for the free tier with a paid commercial licence. **MERIT DEM** and **MERIT Hydro** are CC BY-NC 4.0. OpenStreetMap, which contributes shoreline and water polygons to many DEM-derived products, is under the **Open Database Licence (ODbL)**, whose share-alike clause applies to derived *databases*. NASADEM and SRTM are public domain; GEBCO grids are free with attribution. [Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md) and [Appendix E](../appendices/appendix-e-public-products-tables.md) tabulate the terms; read the licence file, not the summary, before you ship.

### 68.3.2 Derivative works and the NC question

Whether a product derived from a licensed DEM is itself a derivative work — and therefore bound by NC or SA terms — is a question that Creative Commons has addressed only partially. A DTM produced by filtering FABDEM is almost certainly an adaptation; a flood-risk score for a city computed from FABDEM is arguable; a machine-learning model trained on FABDEM and then applied to other data is contested territory in 2024–2025 case law and licence revisions. Two further complications: in the EU the **sui generis database right** (Directive 96/9/EC) protects the investment in compiling a database independently of copyright and can be infringed by extracting a substantial part, which is why ODbL and the Copernicus terms are written as contract-plus-database-right instruments; and in the US, *facts* (including measured elevations) are not copyrightable (*Feist*, 1991), so a US court might find no protectable expression in a DEM at all while a European one finds a database right. The conservative engineering posture is to treat every input's licence as flowing to the output unless counsel says otherwise, to keep a bill of materials of inputs with licence identifiers (the SPDX licence list gives machine-readable names: `CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0`, `ODbL-1.0`, `OGL-UK-3.0`), and to build the commercial product line only from inputs whose licences permit it.

### 68.3.3 Open-data mandates

The EU **INSPIRE Directive** (2007/2/EC) obliged member states to publish discoverable, interoperable spatial data in 34 themes, including Elevation (Annex II), with harmonized data specifications ([Chapter 70](ch70-specifications-guided-tour.md)) but without mandating free reuse. The **Open Data Directive** (2019/1024) and its **Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/138** on high-value datasets changed that: six thematic categories — geospatial, earth observation and environment, meteorological, statistics, companies, and mobility — must be made available free of charge, under CC BY 4.0 or equivalent, via APIs and bulk download, and "Elevation" is expressly among the earth-observation datasets listed, alongside orthoimagery, hydrography, land cover, and others; the obligations applied from June 2024. National lidar DTMs across the EU are consequently open or becoming so; recheck any licence you last read before 2024. Conversely, "open" under a national mandate does not always mean *public domain*: attribution and licence propagation obligations remain.

### 68.3.4 Machine learning and licences

Training data licences, model weights, and model outputs form a chain in which each link may be governed differently. Datasets assembled for DEM super-resolution or DSM-to-DTM learning ([Chapter 45](ch45-super-resolution.md), [Chapter 43](ch43-traditional-vs-ml.md)) commonly mix public-domain 3DEP tiles, CC BY national data, and NC-licensed global DEMs; the resulting model, if released, inherits at minimum the attribution obligations and arguably the NC restriction. Output licensing is largely unsettled. Practical hygiene: record the licence of every training sample at ingestion, keep NC data in a separate pipeline from anything commercial, and state in the model card which inputs were used and under what terms.

## 68.4 Liability and professional practice

### 68.4.1 Who may survey, and what counts as surveying

Every US state and most countries license land surveyors, and the statutes define a protected **practice of surveying** that typically includes boundary work, plats and legal descriptions, and — in many states — "topographic surveys" and "the measurement of elevations" for engineering purposes. Whether photogrammetric and lidar terrain mapping by an unlicensed firm falls within that definition has been contested since the 1990s. The NCEES Model Law and joint NSPS/ASPRS/MAPPS positions settled on a rough compromise: *authoritative* elevation determinations relied on for design, construction, or boundaries require a licensed professional surveyor's (PLS/RPLS) seal; *non-authoritative* mapping for planning, GIS, or inventory does not. State boards have nonetheless pursued drone-mapping firms selling orthomosaics and DSMs with stated accuracies (North Carolina's *360 Virtual Drone Services v. Ritter*, decided for the board by the Fourth Circuit in 2024). Hydrographic surveying is rarely licensed as such; the US relies on the NSPS/THSOA **Certified Hydrographer** credential and IHO/FIG/ICA **Category A and B** recognized programs, and USACE and NOAA contracts increasingly require one in responsible charge. ASPRS offers Certified Photogrammetrist, Certified Mapping Scientist (Lidar, Remote Sensing, GIS/LIS), and UAS certifications; chartered and registered surveyor regimes in the UK, Australia, and Canada fill analogous roles. Before a project begins, determine whether any deliverable will be used for a purpose the local statute reserves to licensees, and whether the contract requires a certification regardless.

### 68.4.2 Standard of care, negligence, and charts

A professional is negligent when their work falls below the **standard of care** — what a reasonably prudent practitioner would have done — and the breach causes foreseeable harm. For elevation products the questions are whether the published accuracy was tested as claimed, whether known errors were disclosed, and whether the product was fit for its advertised use. Nautical and aeronautical charts have the longest liability history: US courts have held the government liable for chart errors in some circumstances while the discretionary-function exception shields policy choices about *where* to survey (*Reminga v. United States*, 1980, concerning an uncharted tower on an aeronautical chart), and the Ninth Circuit in *Aetna v. Jeppesen* (1981) and *Brocklesby v. United States* (1985) held a chart publisher strictly liable for a defective approach chart as a *product*. A DEM supplied for obstacle clearance or flood design is closer to those facts than to a textbook.

Disclaimers ("not for navigation"; "provided as-is") limit but do not eliminate liability; they are generally ineffective against gross negligence, against third parties who did not accept them, and against statutory duties. A **duty to warn** attaches once a producer knows of a defect: a provider who learns of a bust in a tile used for flood mapping and does nothing owns the subsequent harm in a way it would not had the error been unknown. Errata pages and versioned re-releases ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)) are legal protection as well as courtesy.

> **Case file.** *What was known, and who was told.* In claims involving elevation products the recurring issue is rarely the measurement error itself but the absence of any record of its disclosure. Federal oversight of FEMA's flood mapping has made the point repeatedly: the GAO's 2010 review (GAO-11-17) examined how elevation-data quality and map-update priorities affected accuracy, and the DHS Office of Inspector General's 2017 audit (OIG-17-110) found that FEMA could not demonstrate that a large share of its effective maps were accurate or current, and that this was not communicated to the property owners who relied on them. The remedy both recommended was not better lidar alone but documentation and disclosure of data vintage and accuracy on the product. The lesson generalizes: a court asks what you knew, when you knew it, and whom you told.

### 68.4.3 Certification and the seal

A surveyor's seal on a plat or elevation certificate is a representation that the signer took professional responsibility for the work, performed or directly supervised it, and that it meets the applicable standards of practice — in the US the state's minimum technical standards and, for title work, the **ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey** standards (2021 edition). Sealing a product you did not control (a DEM downloaded from a portal) is misconduct in most jurisdictions; incorporating it with an explicit statement of source and limitations is not.

## 68.5 Contracts and specifications as law

A specification becomes binding when a contract incorporates it. "Deliverables shall conform to the USGS Lidar Base Specification 2024 rev. A, Quality Level 1" imports the whole document — density, classification, NVA and VVA tests, swath overlap, hydro-flattening, metadata — as contractual requirements, and a contractor who meets the accuracy table but not the checkpoint rules has not performed. Four clauses deserve attention.

**Acceptance testing** states who tests, with what data, against what criterion; "Government will test using independent checkpoints" and "Contractor shall report NVA using its own checkpoints" allocate risk very differently. Good contracts specify the checkpoint survey standard, the minimum count (30 per ASPRS Ed. 2), the statistic (RMSE$_z$ including checkpoint error), and land-cover stratification. **Remedies** should be graduated — re-flight for voids, reprocessing for classification failures, price adjustment for marginal misses, rejection for systematic failures — with cure periods. **Data ownership and licence** clauses decide whether the agency receives raw data (trajectories, waveforms, calibration logs) or only products, whether the contractor may resell, and whether the deliverable can be released as open data. **Format requirements** should name versions; "LAS format" has meant five incompatible things.

Hydrographic contracts cite IHO S-44 orders and NOAA HSSD editions the same way, and *order* is a trap: S-44 orders are minimums that the hydrographic office *selects*, so a contract must name the order or, since Ed. 6, a project-specific survey matrix. USACE dredging contracts under EM 1110-2-1003 go further, because the before- and after-dredge surveys *are* the payment measurement; disputes turn on the datum transfer, the water-level correction, and the volume method ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)). An **implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose** can arise when a seller knows the buyer's purpose; sellers disclaim it, buyers should state purpose in the scope. Dispute clauses should provide for **independent verification** by a neutral third party under an agreed procedure — far cheaper than discovery.

> **Try it.** A minimal contractual acceptance test for a lidar DTM against independent checkpoints. Expected output: per-cover count, bias, RMSE$_z$ including checkpoint uncertainty (as ASPRS Ed. 2 requires), and 95th percentile.

```python
import numpy as np, rasterio, geopandas as gpd

dtm = rasterio.open("dtm_ql1.tif")
cp = gpd.read_file("checkpoints.gpkg")      # columns: z_obs, sigma_cp, cover
assert cp.crs == dtm.crs, "CRS mismatch is a contractual non-conformance"
z_dtm = np.array([v[0] for v in dtm.sample(zip(cp.geometry.x, cp.geometry.y))])
d = z_dtm - cp.z_obs.values

def rmse_with_cp(d, sigma_cp):
    # ASPRS Ed. 2: product RMSE includes checkpoint survey uncertainty
    return np.sqrt(np.mean(d**2) + np.mean(sigma_cp**2))

for cover, grp in cp.assign(d=d).groupby("cover"):
    r = rmse_with_cp(grp.d.values, grp.sigma_cp.values)
    print(f"{cover:12s} n={len(grp):3d} mean={grp.d.mean():+.3f} m "
          f"RMSEz={r:.3f} m  P95|d|={np.percentile(abs(grp.d),95):.3f} m")
```

Keep the script, its version, and the checkpoint file hash with the acceptance memo.

## 68.6 Evidence: DEMs and surveys in litigation

Elevation data appear in court in boundary cases (where was MHW in 1952?), flood-damage cases (would the water have reached the house had the levee been at design grade?), mining and royalty disputes, landslide and subsidence causation, and construction claims. Under **Federal Rule of Evidence 702** and *Daubert v. Merrell Dow* (1993), expert testimony must rest on reliable methods reliably applied; courts consider testability, peer review, known error rate, controlling standards, and general acceptance. A DEM-based opinion survives *Daubert* when the analyst can state the method's error rate from an accuracy assessment ([Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md)), point to the standards that document it, and show that this dataset was validated. "I used the national DEM" without checkpoints is the testimony that gets excluded. Commonwealth court rules (England's CPR Part 35; Australian expert codes) impose comparable duties, with the expert's overriding duty owed to the court.

Chain of custody and provenance are the second battleground. Raw sensor files, trajectories, calibration reports, processing logs, software versions, and hashes of each intermediate product ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)) establish that the exhibit is what it claims to be and was not altered. Drone imagery and SfM DEMs are now routine evidence in accident reconstruction and construction disputes; courts have admitted them when the operator could show flight logs, GCP survey records, and processing reports, and have discounted them when "the software produced it" was the whole explanation. Historical DEMs carry a special burden: the opponent will argue that a 1970s photogrammetric contour map digitized to a DEM has an unknown error; your answer should be a quantified comparison against surviving control, not an assertion.

## 68.7 Privacy and data protection touching elevation

[Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md) treats privacy as a design and ethics question; the legal skeleton is brief. The EU **General Data Protection Regulation** (2016/679) applies to data relating to an identifiable person, which can include a georeferenced façade, a licence plate, or a backyard captured in oblique imagery or a dense point cloud; European mobile-mapping operators blur faces and plates and offer opt-outs because of 2010-era data-protection decisions against Google Street View. Drone privacy is governed by general and state law, not by aviation regulators: *United States v. Causby* (1946) recognized a landowner's interest in the immediate reaches of the airspace, and several states (Texas Gov. Code ch. 423; Florida §934.50) restrict drone imaging of private property without consent, with exceptions for licensed surveyors and mapping. A terrestrial lidar scan that captures a person is personal data in GDPR jurisdictions even without an image; the mitigation is removal of moving objects and non-retention of identifiable raw scans ([Chapter 27](ch27-moving-and-transient-objects.md)).

## 68.8 Export control and national secrecy law

### 68.8.1 Equipment and software

The instruments that make precise elevation data are dual-use and controlled. In the United States the **ITAR** (22 CFR 120–130) govern the Munitions List and the **EAR** (15 CFR 730–774) govern dual-use items on the Commerce Control List, which mirrors the multilateral **Wassenaar Arrangement**. Inertial measurement units above stated bias and drift thresholds (ECCN 7A003), GNSS receivers that work above 18 km or 600 m/s or use military signals (7A005), certain lidar and laser systems (6A005, 6A008), multibeam and sidescan sonars beyond stated performance (6A001), and underwater vehicles (8A001) all appear. Export includes carrying the item abroad and, under the "deemed export" rule, disclosing controlled technology to a foreign national at home; uploading controlled technical data to a foreign server is also an export. A survey-grade GNSS/INS may travel under a temporary-export exception with paperwork, and may not travel at all to embargoed destinations. Equivalent regimes exist in the EU (Regulation 2021/821), the UK, and Japan.

### 68.8.2 Data restrictions by country

Several states restrict the collection, possession, or export of geospatial data about their territory. **India**'s *Guidelines for Acquiring and Producing Geospatial Data* (Department of Science and Technology, 15 February 2021) replaced a permission regime with **thresholds**: on-land data finer than 1 m horizontal or 3 m vertical, gravity finer than 1 mGal, and bathymetry finer than 10 m within 500 m of shore or 100 m beyond may be created and owned only by Indian entities, must be stored and processed in India, and may reach foreign entities only through APIs that keep the data on Indian servers. **China**'s Surveying and Mapping Law (2017 revision) requires foreign surveying to be done with approved Chinese partners, prohibits export of high-accuracy data, and mandates the obfuscated GCJ-02 coordinate system (and Baidu's BD-09) for published maps, so a WGS 84 track plotted on a Chinese web map is offset by hundreds of metres; high-resolution DEMs of Chinese territory are state secrets. **Russia**, the Gulf states, and others require permits for surveys; **South Korea** prohibits export of detailed map data. The **Kyl–Bingaman Amendment** (US NDAA FY1997, §1064) ⟨H⟩ barred US commercial satellite operators from selling imagery of Israel finer than what non-US sources offered; NOAA set the limit at 2 m and in July 2020 lowered it to 0.4 m. Classified bathymetry and its tension with Seabed 2030 are discussed in [Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md).

## 68.9 Regulatory requirements for data use

Some uses of elevation data are regulated end to end. Aviation terrain and obstacle data must be processed by organizations working to **RTCA DO-200B / EUROCAE ED-76A**, with data-quality requirements traceable from originator to end user; the FAA issues Letters of Acceptance to compliant providers and Regulation (EU) 2017/373 requires equivalent assurance in Europe. ICAO Annex 15 eTOD ([Chapter 70](ch70-specifications-guided-tour.md)) assigns integrity levels (routine 10⁻³, essential 10⁻⁵, critical 10⁻⁸) to data elements — a legal requirement a DEM producer meets nowhere else. At sea, SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 requires ships on international voyages to carry up-to-date **official** charts; a privately produced bathymetric grid, however good, is not a chart for carriage purposes. Floodplain management under the NFIP requires communities to regulate to the BFE on FEMA's effective maps, changeable only by a LOMA/LOMR with certified elevations. Wetland delineation, dam safety, and mine reclamation impose their own elevation requirements ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)).

## 68.10 Indigenous and community data rights

Surveys over traditional lands and waters raise rights that are legal in some jurisdictions and ethical everywhere. In Canada the First Nations principles of **OCAP®** — ownership, control, access, possession — assert collective ownership of information about communities and control over its collection and use; agencies increasingly require OCAP-consistent data agreements for work on reserve or treaty lands. The **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics; Carroll et al. 2020) extend FAIR with a people-centred counterpart; UNDRIP (2007) supplies the consultation duty. Concretely: a lidar survey that will reveal archaeological features, sacred sites, or burials under canopy — which it does, routinely — should be planned with the community, should have an agreed release policy for sensitive-feature locations (redaction or generalization of specific cells; methods and artefacts in [Chapter 69](ch69-security-sovereignty-privacy-ethics.md)), and should return benefits to the community. In the US, NHPA §106 consultation applies to federal undertakings and ARPA allows site locations to be withheld from release.

## Then & now

Boundaries were first fixed by **monuments and witness trees** ⟨H⟩; the US Public Land Survey System (from 1785) made the monument the law and coordinates merely evidence of it, a priority rule that still holds. **Coordinates entered the law** with State Plane systems (1930s) and GNSS positions (1990s). **Tidal boundaries by datum** date from *Borax* (1935) and Shalowitz's *Shore and Sea Boundaries* (1962, 1964). **UNCLOS** (1982, in force 1994) made chart datums and the 2,500 m isobath instruments of sovereignty, and the CLCS process from 1997 made deep-water multibeam a legal evidentiary genre. **Open-data mandates** — INSPIRE (2007), the US OPEN Government Data Act (2018), EU Regulation 2023/138 — turned priced national DEMs into free ones, while **non-commercial research DEMs** and **ML licensing** questions (2020s) added new derivative-work uncertainty. Export control moved from instruments to software and cloud; data-sovereignty regimes (India 2021, China 2017) replaced blanket prohibition with thresholds and localization.

## Validation & uncertainty

Legal elevation questions are validation questions wearing robes; in each case below the methods of [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) decide the outcome and an undocumented uncertainty is itself a defect.

**Tidal datums at a site.** A boundary at MHW requires the MHW elevation at *that shore*, not at the nearest NOAA station. The standard procedure (NOAA 2003, NOS CO-OPS 2) installs a temporary gauge, observes simultaneously with a control station for at least a lunar month, and transfers the datum by the modified range-ratio method. Transfer uncertainty is roughly 0.03–0.05 m (1σ) for a 30-day series in similar tidal regimes (Swanson 1974), degrading to 0.1 m or worse in river-influenced estuaries or with short series. Report method, series length, control station, epoch, and σ; a boundary survey without them is an opinion.

**Flood elevations.** A certified lowest-floor elevation from levelling or RTK GNSS carries 1σ of 0.01–0.03 m; the BFE it is compared to carries hydraulic-model and terrain uncertainties of 0.1–0.5 m that the map does not print, which is why FEMA now requires QL2 lidar for new studies.

**Continental-shelf limits.** CLCS/11 requires accuracy estimates with the bathymetry; because the dominant errors are systematic, the right form is a sensitivity analysis that recomputes the 2,500 m isobath and FOS under alternative sound-speed profiles and smoothing scales.

**Volumes under contract.** For a dredging payment survey the controlling uncertainty is the water-level correction and datum transfer, not the multibeam's TVU; a 0.05 m bias over a 500 m × 100 m reach is 2,500 m³ of paid or unpaid material. EM 1110-2-1003 requires documented corrections and periodic performance tests; the survey report is the evidence.

**Evidentiary sufficiency.** To meet *Daubert*, be able to state the checkpoint design, the resulting RMSE$_z$, bias, and 95th percentile, the governing standard, the lineage, and the software version — recorded *at processing time*, not when the subpoena arrives.

## Software

**Open source:** GDAL/OGR and QGIS for baseline construction, buffer/envelope computations (zone limits are envelopes of geodesic arcs — use GeographicLib or PostGIS geography types, not projected buffers); GMT for isobath extraction (`grdcontour`) and FOS gradient analysis; PDAL/rasterio for acceptance-test scripts; SPDX tooling and `reuse` (FSFE) for licence identifiers in data bills of materials; NOAA's open tidal-analysis code (and the Python `utide`/`pytides` packages) for datum computation. **Free but closed:** NOAA VDatum for transforming between tidal, orthometric, and ellipsoidal datums in US waters; NGS tools (NCAT, GEOID18) for datum and unit conversions; FEMA's Elevation Certificate form and instructions. **Commercial:** CARIS LOTS (Law of the Sea) and Geocap Seafloor/Shelf for Article 76 workflows; Trimble Business Center and Carlson Survey for legal-description generation and plat preparation — caveat for all: a tool's "UNCLOS wizard" encodes one reading of the convention and your state's practice may differ.

## Pitfalls

- **Using MHW from a distant tide station as the boundary datum.** Tidal range and datum offsets vary along a coast and up estuaries by decimetres → install a site gauge, do a simultaneous comparison, report transfer uncertainty.
- **Reading a chart's low-water line as the current baseline, or a new survey as the legal one.** The legal line is the one on the officially recognized chart until the state updates and deposits it → check deposit status; keep the chart edition in the record.
- **Building a commercial product on NC-licensed inputs (FABDEM, MERIT, CoastalDEM free tier).** Licence summaries are read, licence texts are not → keep a licensed bill of materials with SPDX identifiers and segregate NC pipelines.
- **Assuming non-US government data are public domain.** Crown copyright, database rights, and national licences apply → read the licence; check whether the 2023/138 high-value-dataset obligation now applies.
- **Delivering a "topographic survey" in a state that reserves it to licensed surveyors.** Statutory definitions are broad; boards enforce → check the state's practice act and the contract's certification clause before bidding.
- **Filing FAA Form 7460-1 heights in the wrong datum or from a coarse DEM.** The form wants AMSL in feet with an accuracy code → derive site elevation from a surveyed value; state the accuracy code honestly.
- **Mixing US survey feet and international feet in State Plane coordinates.** 2 ppm is a metre or more of northing → confirm the unit on every coordinate list; metadata must say which foot.
- **Specifying "S-44 Order 1a" or "LBS" without edition or survey matrix.** Orders are chosen, not inherent → name edition, order/QL, checkpoint procedure, and acceptance statistic.
- **Having no record of when a known DEM error was discovered and disclosed.** Duty to warn attaches to knowledge → maintain errata and versioned releases with dates.
- **Collecting sub-metre data in India, China, or other restricted jurisdictions as a foreign entity without a local partner and in-country storage.** Thresholds are explicit → review the 2021 Guidelines / 2017 Law; structure the project accordingly.
- **Releasing lidar over Indigenous or archaeologically sensitive lands without a release policy.** Canopy-penetrating DTMs reveal sites → agree redaction and access rules with the community before acquisition.

## Key takeaways

- An elevation has legal meaning only together with its datum, epoch, unit, method, and uncertainty; a boundary, a BFE, or a 2,500 m isobath without them is incomplete evidence.
- UNCLOS turns chart datums and bathymetry into sovereignty: low-water lines on official charts, low-tide elevations, the 2,500 m isobath, and the foot of slope are all DEM products with legal force.
- *Borax* (1935) defined MHW as a 19-year statistical average; tidal boundaries are therefore datum transfers with quantifiable uncertainty, and states differ on which datum (MHW, MLW, or a physical line) bounds private land.
- Licences propagate: know whether each input is public domain, attribution-only, share-alike, or non-commercial, and keep a machine-readable bill of materials; EU Regulation 2023/138 has opened much national elevation data, but open is not the same as unrestricted.
- Licensure and certification are jurisdiction-specific; determine whether a deliverable is "authoritative" and who must seal it before the project starts.
- A specification incorporated into a contract is law between the parties; name editions, orders/quality levels, acceptance tests, remedies, data rights, and formats explicitly.
- Keep provenance as if it will be evidence: raw data, logs, versions, hashes, checkpoints, and the dates on which errors were found and disclosed.
- Export control covers instruments, software, cloud transfers, and conversations; national data-sovereignty rules set numeric accuracy thresholds — check both before equipment or data crosses a border.
- Indigenous data rights (OCAP, CARE) and consultation duties apply to surveys over traditional lands; plan release and redaction with the community, not after the fact.
