# Chapter 20 — Sonar: the many types and how they make bathymetry

> **Part V — Sensors and platforms.** The last of the ranging-sensor chapters takes the physics of [Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md) under water, where the medium is slow, bent, and lossy, and where the depth measurement that charts and seabed DEMs depend on is a chain of corrections rather than a single range.

**In this chapter.** Sonar produces nearly all bathymetry deeper than a few metres. You will be able to explain how sound travels in the sea — speed, refraction, absorption, beam patterns, the sonar equation — and how a bottom is detected by amplitude and by phase; place every member of the sonar family from the lead line and single-beam through multibeam, interferometric, side-scan, synthetic aperture, sub-bottom, parametric, imaging, and fisheries systems; derive the depth- and angle-dependent footprint and swath geometry that make sonar DEMs inherently variable-resolution; and work through the integration problem — motion, latency, lever arms, waterline and draft, squat, sound-speed casts, tides or ellipsoidal referencing — and the patch test that calibrates it. You will be able to compute an S-44 TVU/THU, understand the Hare–Godin–Mayer total propagated uncertainty and the CUBE/CHRT estimators that turn soundings into uncertainty-attributed surfaces, read backscatter and water-column data, and follow the processing chain from raw files to BAG/S-102 and the chart.

## 20.1 Underwater acoustics

**Sound speed.** Sound travels in seawater at about 1,500 m/s, varying with temperature, salinity, and pressure by roughly +4 m/s per °C (less at high temperature), +1.3 m/s per PSU, and +1.7 m/s per 100 m depth (Mackenzie 1981; Chen & Millero 1977; Del Grosso 1974 give the empirical equations, which agree to a few tenths of a metre per second). The range in nature — from about 1,440 m/s in cold, fresh, shallow water to 1,550 m/s in warm, saline, deep water — is 7 %, and the vertical **sound-speed profile (SVP)** is rarely uniform: a warm mixed layer over a thermocline produces gradients of −0.1 to −1 s⁻¹ in the top tens of metres, and below the thermocline the pressure term makes the speed rise slowly (+0.017 s⁻¹) to the bottom. Two consequences follow. The depth computed from a travel time scales with the *harmonic mean* sound speed along the path, so a 1 % speed error is a 1 % depth error; and oblique rays bend according to Snell's law, $\sin\theta(z)/c(z) = $ const, curving toward the horizontal where the speed increases downward and toward the vertical where it decreases, so that the outer beams of a swath land in different places and at different depths than a straight-ray computation would give (Section 20.4; Mathematics).

**Absorption and frequency.** Acoustic energy is lost to spherical spreading ($20\log_{10} r$ dB) and to absorption ($\alpha r$ dB), where the absorption coefficient $\alpha$ rises steeply with frequency through the boric-acid and magnesium-sulphate relaxations (Francois & Garrison 1982; Ainslie & McColm 1998): roughly 1 dB/km at 12 kHz, 30 dB/km at 100 kHz, 60–70 dB/km at 300 kHz, and 300 dB/km at 1 MHz in temperate seawater (approximate). Frequency therefore sets the reach: 12 kHz systems map full-ocean depth (22 km two-way path at 11 km depth, ~20 dB absorption), 200–400 kHz systems are limited to a few hundred metres, and 1 MHz imaging sonars to tens of metres. Frequency also sets the resolution for a given array size, since beamwidth $\approx \lambda/L$ ([Chapter 17](ch17-measurement-physics.md)): a 1° beam needs a 0.3 m array at 300 kHz and a 7 m array at 12 kHz, which is why deep-water multibeams are hull-length arrays and shallow-water ones are the size of a shoebox.

**Beam patterns and the sonar equation.** A transducer array of length $L$ produces a main lobe of width $\theta_{-3\,\text{dB}} \approx 0.88\lambda/L$ (unshaded) and sidelobes 13 dB down for a uniform array — lower with amplitude shading at the cost of a wider main lobe. Sidelobes matter because a strong nadir echo received through a sidelobe of an outer beam arrives before the outer beam's own bottom echo and can be detected in its place, producing the "tunnel" or "omega" artefacts of poorly adjusted systems. The **sonar equation** books the decibels: for a bottom echo,

$$\text{EL} = \text{SL} - 2\,\text{TL} + \text{BS} + 10\log_{10}A,\qquad \text{SNR} = \text{EL} - (\text{NL} - \text{DI}),$$

where SL is the source level (typically 200–240 dB re 1 µPa at 1 m), TL $= 20\log_{10} r + \alpha r$ the one-way transmission loss, BS the bottom backscattering strength per unit area (−10 to −40 dB depending on sediment and grazing angle), $A$ the ensonified area, NL the noise level (shipping, flow, weather, the vessel itself), and DI the receiver's directivity index. The equation predicts the depth at which a system loses the bottom and explains why outer beams, which have longer paths, grazing incidence (low BS), and larger ensonified areas smeared in time, are the first to fail.

**Bottom detection.** For each beam the system must choose one instant in the echo as "the bottom." Near nadir the echo is short and strong and the **amplitude** detector — the centre of gravity or the leading edge of the envelope — works well. At oblique angles the echo spreads over many samples as the beam's footprint sweeps across the bottom and the amplitude peak is ill-defined; the **phase** (split-aperture) detector divides the receive array in two, computes the phase difference of the echo between the halves, and finds the sample at which that difference passes through zero — the instant the bottom lies exactly on the beam axis (de Moustier 1988; Hughes Clarke 2018). Phase detection is the standard beyond about 20–30° and gives centimetre-scale precision where amplitude detection would give decimetres; it fails on the nadir beams (no phase slope) and on very rough or very steep bottoms. Modern systems choose per beam and report a detection-type flag and a quality factor (Lurton & Augustin 2010) that the TPU and the CUBE estimator consume.

## 20.2 The family tree

**Lead line and wire drag.** For four millennia depth was a weighted line; its soundings — one per cast, reduced by hand to a tide — still underlie many charts of the world's coasts. **Wire drag** (US Coast and Geodetic Survey, 1904–1990) swept a horizontal wire at a set depth between two launches to prove the *absence* of obstructions above that depth, a guarantee that no sounding method can give.

**Single-beam echo sounders (SBES)** transmit a single, usually wide (5–20°) beam straight down and record the two-way time of the first strong echo; with GNSS and heave compensation they give a profile along the track at 1–20 Hz. Their footprint (a 10° beam at 20 m is 3.5 m) averages the bottom and biases toward the shallowest point within it; their coverage is a line. **Dual-frequency** units (typically 33/200 kHz or 38/200 kHz) read two bottoms: the high frequency reflects from the top of fluid mud or vegetation, the low frequency penetrates to the consolidated bottom, and in dredged channels the two can differ by a metre — the operational definition of "nautical depth" in some ports is tied to the low-frequency return or to a density criterion rather than to either echo ([Chapter 4](ch04-names-and-definitions.md)).

**Multibeam echo sounders (MBES)** form many narrow beams across track in one ping. The classic **Mills cross** uses a transmit array along the keel (narrow along-track, wide across-track fan) and a receive array athwartships (narrow across-track beams formed by **beamforming** — delaying and summing the elements' signals for each steering angle); the intersection of the transmit fan and each receive beam is the footprint. Modern systems (Kongsberg EM 2040/712/124, Teledyne Reson SeaBat T-series, R2Sonic, Norbit) add **multi-sector** transmission (several sectors at slightly different frequencies to steer each against yaw and pitch and to achieve full along-track coverage), **dual-swath** (two fans per ping, fore and aft, doubling along-track density), **dual-head** installations (two receivers angled outward for wider swaths in shallow water), **FM chirp** pulses with pulse compression for range at depth, and recording of the full **water column** between transducer and bottom. Beams number 256–1,024 per swath over sectors of 120–170°, at ping rates from 1 Hz in the deep ocean to 50 Hz in a harbour.

**Interferometric (phase-measuring) bathymetric sonars** (Section 17.3; EdgeTech, Klein, GeoAcoustics/Kongsberg GeoSwath, Bathyswath) use side-scan-like arrays with two or more staves per side and compute the arrival angle of every time sample from the inter-stave phase, giving thousands of soundings per ping over swaths of 10–15 times water depth in very shallow water. Their weaknesses are the nadir gap, phase noise and multipath in the outer swath, and a lower sounding quality near nadir than MBES; their strength is coverage rate in 2–20 m depths.

**Side-scan sonar (SSS)** tows or mounts two fan beams looking sideways and records echo intensity against time, imaging the seabed's backscatter at centimetre to decimetre pixel size with no direct depth (except from shadow length, $h = L\sin\phi$ at grazing angle $\phi$); it detects objects — wrecks, boulders, pipelines, mines — far smaller than MBES soundings resolve, at the cost of a **nadir gap** below the fish and geometric distortion from towfish position uncertainty. Many hydrographic specifications require both SSS and MBES for object detection.

**Synthetic aperture sonar (SAS)** coherently combines successive pings along the track to synthesise an array hundreds of times longer than the physical one, giving along-track resolution independent of range (2–5 cm at 100–200 m ranges for systems such as Kongsberg HISAS and Kraken AquaPix) and, in **interferometric SAS**, bathymetry at the same resolution (Hayes & Gough 2009; Hansen 2011). SAS requires platform motion known to a fraction of a wavelength over the aperture — hence it flies on AUVs with DVL-aided navigation and micronavigation from the data itself — and it fails in strong currents or on rough seas.

**Sub-bottom profilers and seismic sources** map the *sub*-seafloor "DEMs": **chirp** systems (2–24 kHz swept pulses) resolve decimetres to tens of metres below the bed; **boomers** (0.5–5 kHz) and **sparkers** (50 Hz–4 kHz) penetrate tens to hundreds of metres at metre resolution. Their products — reflector horizons picked from the record and converted from two-way time with an assumed sediment velocity (1,500–1,800 m/s unconsolidated) — are surfaces with every validation need of a DEM plus a velocity-model uncertainty ([Chapter 65](ch65-mining-landfills-earthworks.md)). **Parametric** profilers (Innomar, Kongsberg TOPAS) exploit the nonlinear interaction of two ~100 kHz primaries to generate a 4–15 kHz difference frequency in a beam as narrow as the primaries', from a small transducer.

**Forward-looking and imaging sonars** (Sound Metrics ARIS, Blueprint Oculus; 0.7–3 MHz) produce video-rate acoustic images for ROV piloting, structure inspection, and fish counting in turbid water; they are not survey sensors but are increasingly registered into point clouds by SLAM-style processing ([Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)). **Scanning sonars** rotate a single beam mechanically for the same purpose. **Fisheries split-beam echo sounders** (Simrad EK80 and kin) measure the target strength and position of fish and plankton; they matter here because their **calibration spheres** — tungsten-carbide or copper spheres of known target strength (Demer et al. 2015) — are the best-controlled absolute calibration in underwater acoustics, and because their bottom detections are harvested as crowdsourced bathymetry ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md)). **ADCPs and DVLs** measure water and platform velocity by Doppler; the DVL's bottom track is the navigation backbone of every AUV ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)), but neither is a mapping instrument.

> **Definitions that bite.** "Swath width" is quoted as an angle (140°), as a multiple of depth (5.5 ×), or in metres at a stated depth, always for a flat bottom, calm sea, and favourable sediment. The *usable* swath — the sector within which soundings meet the project's TVU — is 20–40° narrower than the *achievable* swath on soft or sloping bottoms, and line spacing must be planned on the usable figure. A survey planned on the brochure swath has gaps.

## 20.3 Multibeam geometry

On a flat bottom at depth $d$, a beam of width $\theta$ steered to angle $\phi$ from nadir has a slant range $d/\cos\phi$, an across-track footprint of about $d\,\theta/\cos^2\phi$, and an along-track footprint of $d\,\theta_T/\cos\phi$ for transmit beamwidth $\theta_T$. At nadir the footprint is $2d\tan(\theta/2) \approx d\theta$: 0.35 m for a 1° beam in 20 m, 70 m in 4,000 m. At 60° the across-track footprint is four times the nadir value and the slant range twice, so the outer beams have longer, weaker, more smeared echoes, larger ensonified areas, lower grazing angles, and far greater sensitivity to refraction and roll errors — the **outer-beam degradation** that limits the usable swath. The swath width is $2d\tan\phi_{\max}$: 5.5 × depth at $\phi_{\max} = 70°$, 3.5 × depth at 60°, 2 × depth at 45°, and in deep water the usable sector shrinks to 45–60° so that deep systems cover 2–3 × depth where shallow ones cover 4–7 ×.

**Sounding density and grid resolution.** Across track, the sounding spacing is set by the beam spacing (equi-angle or equi-distant modes); along track, by the ping rate and the vessel speed: at 8 kn (4.1 m/s) and a ping rate limited by the two-way time to the outer beam (in 100 m of water, about 0.4 s for a 70° swath), consecutive swaths are about 1.6 m apart at nadir, and in 4,000 m (ping interval ~10 s) 40 m apart. A grid cell should contain several soundings and should not be finer than the footprint: the common practice of gridding at 1 % of depth (or at a resolution chosen by depth band — the variable-resolution logic behind CHRT and the S-102 tiling schemes) follows from footprint and density together, and a 0.5 m grid of 50 m deep water from a 1° system is oversampled by a factor of two in footprint terms and under-populated at the swath edges ([Chapter 44](ch44-resolution-and-sampling.md), [Chapter 46](ch46-data-models.md)). Side-scan's nadir gap and MBES's outer-beam loss are complementary, which is why combined systems and 100–200 % coverage plans exist.

> **Rule of thumb.** Plan line spacing at 3–4 × depth for shallow-water MBES and 2–2.5 × depth in deep water; grid no finer than the nadir footprint — for a single uniform grid, the footprint of the *deepest* water in the area — or use a variable-resolution surface. The rule fails over soft, sloping, or very rough bottoms and in strong stratification (narrow the usable swath), and for feature-detection surveys, which need 200 % coverage or side-scan regardless of depth.

## 20.4 Integration: motion, latency, offsets, water level, and the patch test

A sounding is the vector sum of the transducer's position and orientation and the beam's range and angle, exactly as in the lidar equation ([Chapter 18](ch18-topographic-lidar.md)), with the water level added at the end. The pieces:

**Motion.** Roll, pitch, and heading from the inertial system rotate every beam; a roll error $\delta r$ moves the outer sounding vertically by $d\tan\phi\,\delta r$ — 0.1° at 60° in 50 m is 15 cm — a pitch error displaces the swath along track by $d\,\delta p$, a heading error rotates it. **Heave** — vertical motion at wave periods — comes from doubly integrated, high-pass-filtered vertical acceleration, good to about 5 cm or 5 % of amplitude; it fails on long-period swell beyond the filter cutoff, in turns (the "heave hook"), and when the IMU is far from the transducer, so that roll and pitch produce **induced heave** at the transducer that the lever arms must correct. GNSS-derived heights (RTK/PPK/PPP) now replace heave in ellipsoidally referenced surveys ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md)).

**Latency.** Each sensor's data arrive with a delay; a position latency $\delta t$ shifts every sounding along track by $v\,\delta t$ (1 s at 8 kn is 4 m), and an attitude latency applies roll from the wrong instant, producing a roll artefact whose sign depends on the roll rate. Precise time-stamping at the source (PPS-disciplined clocks, [Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)) has reduced the problem but not the need to test for it.

**Lever arms, waterline, draft, squat.** The offsets between the GNSS antenna, the IMU, and the transducer are measured by dimensional survey to about 1 cm and entered — in the right sign convention — in the acquisition and processing software; an error in the vertical lever arm is a constant depth bias. The **waterline** (static draft of the transducer below the water surface) is measured at rest and changes with loading and fuel burn; **squat and settlement** — the vessel's sinkage and trim change under way, typically 2–20 cm for launches at survey speed and more for ships — must be measured (by RTK GNSS at several speeds, or by the classic reference-pile method) and applied as a speed-dependent correction. In an ellipsoidally referenced survey, in which the GNSS height of the antenna is carried to the transducer through the lever arms and attitude, draft and squat drop out of the depth computation; in a tide-reduced survey they are a direct bias.

**Sound speed.** The **surface sound speed** at the transducer face is needed for beamforming — flat arrays steer beams by phase delays computed with the local speed, and an error $\delta c_s$ mis-steers a beam at $\phi$ by $\delta\phi = \tan\phi\,\delta c_s / c_s$ — so a sound-speed probe at the face sampling every second is standard; the **profile** through the water column is needed for ray tracing and is measured by CTD, by an expendable or underway profiler (MVP, XBT with salinity assumed), or by a cast on a line, as often as the water changes: every few hours in stable conditions, every 20 minutes or continuously in estuaries, fronts, and river plumes. The characteristic refraction artefacts are the **smile** (outer beams curling up) or **frown** (curling down) across an otherwise flat swath, which appear when the profile used is too slow or too fast relative to the water, and their magnitude at the swath edge is of the order of the depth times the relative speed error times $\tan^2\phi$ (Mathematics). Beaudoin, Hughes Clarke & Bartlett (2004) showed how surface-speed errors and profile errors interact in multi-sector systems and how to correct them in post-processing; Dinn, Loncarevic & Costello (1995) quantified the depth errors from profile errors across the swath.

**Water level.** Finally, a depth below the instantaneous surface becomes a depth below chart datum by subtracting the water level — from a tide gauge (with a zoning model for the spatial variation between gauge and survey area, [Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)), from a hydrodynamic model, or, in an **ellipsoidally referenced survey (ERS)**, from the GNSS-derived ellipsoidal height of the vessel and a separation model (ellipsoid to chart datum, such as VDatum or a national equivalent). ERS converts the tide, draft, squat, and heave problems into a GNSS-height problem and a separation-model problem, both of which are better behaved and better documented than a tide gauge 30 km from the survey ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md)).

**The patch test.** Before any survey, and after any change to the installation, the angular offsets between the IMU and the transducer and the navigation latency are calibrated at sea with a standardised set of lines (Godin 1998):

| Parameter | Lines | Bottom | What is compared | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Latency | Same line, same direction, two speeds | Slope or distinct feature | Along-track position of the feature between passes | Shift proportional to speed difference |
| Pitch | Same line, reciprocal, same speed | Slope or feature (nadir) | Along-track position of the feature | Shift $2d\,\delta p$ between passes |
| Roll | Same line, reciprocal, same speed | Flat | Across-track depth profiles | Opposite tilts, difference $2d\tan\phi\,\delta r$ at the edges |
| Yaw (heading) | Adjacent parallel lines, same direction, feature in outer beams | Feature off nadir | Along-track position of the feature between lines | Shift proportional to across-track distance |

The order matters (latency, then pitch, roll, yaw, since each contaminates the next), the vessel must be moving at survey speed with the same sensors and settings, and the residual after calibration should be checked with a fresh pair of lines. Typical results are 0.05–0.2° for pitch and yaw and 0.02–0.1° for roll; roll is the one that matters most because it multiplies $\tan\phi$. Software fits these by eye on profiles or by automated least squares; both should be reported with the lines used. The patch test does not calibrate lever arms, waterline, sound speed, or the GNSS–IMU alignment, which have their own procedures ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md)).

## 20.5 Uncertainty: TPU, S-44, CUBE, crosslines, reference surfaces

The **total propagated uncertainty (TPU)** of a sounding is the first-order propagation of every term above through the sounding geometry (Hare, Godin & Mayer 1995; Hare 1995, 2001): the horizontal part (THU) from positioning, heading × range, and the across-track lever of the beam; the vertical part (TVU) from range (sound speed, detection), angle (roll, refraction, beam steering), heave or GNSS height, lever arms, draft/squat, and water level/separation. The model is depth- and angle-dependent by construction: the roll and refraction terms scale with $d\tan\phi$ and $d\tan^2\phi$, so the outer beams of a deep swath carry the largest TVU, and a per-sounding uncertainty is the natural product. **IHO S-44** (Edition 6.1.0, 2022) sets the allowances by survey order as

$$\text{TVU}_{\max}(d) = \sqrt{a^2 + (b\,d)^2}$$

at 95 % confidence, with $(a, b)$ = (0.15 m, 0.0075) for Exclusive Order, (0.25 m, 0.0075) Special Order, (0.5 m, 0.013) Orders 1a and 1b, and (1.0 m, 0.023) Order 2; and THU allowances of 1 m, 2 m, 5 m + 5 % of depth, and 20 m + 10 % of depth respectively, plus feature-detection and coverage requirements (Special Order: 100 % feature search for 1 m cubes; Order 1a: 2 m cubes to 40 m, 10 % of depth beyond). A sounding's TVU must be below the allowance *and* the survey must demonstrate it; the demonstration is the subject of the Validation section.

**CUBE** (Combined Uncertainty and Bathymetry Estimator; Calder & Mayer 2003) turned per-sounding TPU into surfaces. At each grid node, soundings within a capture radius contribute to one or more depth **hypotheses**, each a running weighted mean with weights from the propagated vertical uncertainty inflated for horizontal uncertainty and distance from the node; a sounding that disagrees beyond a tolerance starts a new hypothesis, and when several coexist (a wreck and the seabed; noise and the bottom) a **disambiguation** rule — by sounding count, by agreement with a neighbourhood surface, or both — chooses, with the alternatives retained for review. The output is depth, uncertainty, hypothesis count, and hypothesis strength per node; the hypothesis-count layer is a QC map pointing to where a hydrographer must look. **CHRT** (Calder & Rice 2017) lets resolution vary with local depth and density, honouring the footprint logic of Section 20.3. Both are *estimators*, not shoal-biased selections: the navigation product must still be shoal-biased by **designated soundings** on features, and averaging surfaces must not be charted without that step.

**Crosslines and reference surfaces** are the external checks. Crosslines — lines run perpendicular to the main lines, typically totalling 4–5 % of the mainscheme length, under different conditions (another day, another tide, another cast) — are compared sounding-to-surface where they cross, by beam angle, depth, and line; the statistics (mean, standard deviation, 95 % bounds) should lie within the order's TVU, and systematic patterns by angle reveal roll or refraction problems. A **reference surface** — a small, flat, stable area surveyed densely and repeatedly, often with a different vessel or at the start of each season — provides the absolute check for a system's calibration and the long-term record of its stability ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md), [Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)).

## 20.6 Backscatter, multispectral MBES, and water column

Every MBES also records the **backscatter** intensity of each beam's bottom echo, and side-scan records nothing else. Converted to backscattering strength by removing source level, transmission loss, ensonified area, and beam patterns, and corrected for grazing-angle dependence, backscatter measures seabed roughness and hardness at the acoustic wavelength — a proxy for sediment type and the input to seabed classification and habitat mapping (Lurton & Lamarche 2015). Its radiometric calibration is poor compared with its geometry: absolute levels differ between systems by tens of decibels and within a system by sector, firmware, and the settings a surveyor changes mid-line; relative mosaics are achievable, absolute ones need calibration against a reference. **Multispectral MBES** — one system operated at several frequencies on successive pings (e.g. 100/200/400 kHz) — exploits frequency-dependent penetration and scattering to separate sediments that one frequency confuses.

**Water-column imaging** records the full echo from transducer to bottom for every beam, revealing gas seeps (bubble flares), fish schools, suspended sediment plumes, the masts of wrecks and the tops of mooring lines that bottom detection misses, kelp, and the acoustic footprint of the vessel's own bubbles (Colbo et al. 2014). For bathymetry its value is twofold: the **least depth** over a wreck or a mast is found in the water column when the bottom detector, biased toward the strong seabed echo, has missed it; and the sidelobe and bubble-sweep contamination that corrupts bottom detection is visible and diagnosable. Water-column data are large (gigabytes per hour) and are usually recorded selectively; hydrographic specifications now require them over features and for least-depth determination.

## 20.7 Platforms

The physics of Section 20.3 makes the platform the resolution control. A **ship** with a hull-mounted 12–30 kHz system covers the deep ocean at 2–3 × depth swaths and 50–150 m footprints; a **launch** with a 200–400 kHz system covers 0–200 m depths at decimetre footprints; a **USV** does the same with a smaller hull, no crew, and more motion per unit sea state ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md)). An **AUV** flying 30–80 m above a 3,000 m seabed with a 200–400 kHz MBES recovers the launch's footprint at abyssal depth over a 100–300 m swath — resolution by proximity — at the cost of acoustic positioning ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)) that limits absolute horizontal accuracy to metres or tens of metres while the relative bathymetry is decimetre-quality; an **ROV** adds station-keeping for millimetre-scale structured-light and laser-line systems. Vessel noise, bubble sweep-down in a head sea, and propeller wash degrade every sonar; gondolas, drop keels, and pole mounts forward of the bow wave are the fixes.

## 20.8 Processing chain and deliverables

Raw data arrive in manufacturer formats — Kongsberg `.all` and `.kmall`, Teledyne Reson/Norbit `.s7k`, EdgeTech `.jsf`, the extended Triton `.xtf` common for side-scan and interferometric systems, HYPACK `.hsx`, and the vendor-neutral **Generic Sensor Format (GSF)** used for exchange and archive since 1998 ⟨H⟩ — each carrying ranges and angles, attitude, navigation, sound speed, and settings in time-stamped datagrams ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)). Processing proceeds: import and vessel configuration → post-processed navigation and attitude (SBET) → sound-speed application and re-ray-tracing → water-level or ERS reduction → TPU → **cleaning** (filters, CUBE hypothesis review, and manual editing of remaining fliers — the step where over-cleaning removes real shoals and under-cleaning leaves noise) → surface generation (CUBE/CHRT at depth-appropriate resolution) → designated soundings and feature verification → export as **BAG** (with uncertainty layer) and increasingly **S-102** tiles, plus sounding selection, side-scan contacts, and backscatter mosaic → the **Descriptive Report** (NOAA) or Report of Survey describing vessel, sensors, calibrations, sound speed, water levels, crosslines, and anomalies ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md), [Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)). Chart compilation — generalisation, shoal-biased selection, symbolisation — is a separate craft ([Chapter 58](ch58-making-maps.md)).

## Then & now

Leonardo da Vinci described listening for ships through a tube in the water around 1490 ⟨H⟩; practical echo ranging waited for the Titanic (1912), Fessenden's oscillator (1914), and Alexander Behm's 1913 echo-sounder patent ⟨H⟩, with Atlas Werke (Bremen, founded 1902 ⟨H⟩) among the firms that made echo sounders routine between the wars, and Simrad (Simonsen Radio, Oslo, 1947; the gis-history timeline gives 1931 ⟨H⟩, which conflicts with company histories) joining after the Second World War. The USC&GS Hydrographic Manual (Hawley 1928 ⟨H⟩; Umbach 1976 ⟨H⟩) codified single-beam practice, tide reduction, and wire drag. Multibeam began with the US Navy's SASS in the 1960s (a multibeam patent dates from 1962 ⟨H⟩), became commercial with **SeaBeam** in 1977, and expanded with Hydrosweep (1989 ⟨H⟩), the Simrad EM series, and the shallow-water imaging multibeams of Hughes Clarke, Mayer & Wells (1996). Open processing arrived with **MB-System** (1993; Caress & Chayes 1996) and pfmabe (1994); GSF (1998 ⟨H⟩) gave an exchange format; NOAA's HSSD (2000 ⟨H⟩, updated annually) set contract specifications; **CUBE** (2003) and **BAG** (2006) made uncertainty a deliverable, and S-102 is making it a navigational product. The 2020s are the decade of USVs and AUVs and of Seabed 2030's push to map the three quarters of the ocean floor never ensonified at modern resolution.

## Mathematics

**Sonar equation, numerically.** A 300 kHz system (SL = 220 dB re 1 µPa at 1 m, $\alpha$ = 0.065 dB/m) over sand (BS ≈ −25 dB at 45° grazing) at 100 m slant range with a 2 m² ensonified area: TL $= 20\log 100 + 6.5 = 46.5$ dB; EL $= 220 - 93 - 25 + 3 = 105$ dB; with NL = 60 dB and DI = 25 dB, SNR = 70 dB. At 400 m on mud (BS ≈ −35 dB): TL = 78 dB, EL $= 220 - 156 - 35 + 6 = 35$ dB, SNR ≈ 0 dB — the bottom is lost. Absorption is what kills the outer beams at high frequency.

**Ray tracing through a layered SVP.** Divide the profile into layers $i$ with constant gradient $g_i = (c_{i+1} - c_i)/(z_{i+1} - z_i)$. With Snell's constant $p = \sin\theta_0 / c_0$ fixed by the launch angle $\theta_0$ (after beam-steering with the surface speed $c_0$), the ray in layer $i$ is a circular arc of radius $R_i = 1/(p\,g_i)$; the horizontal distance and travel time across the layer are

$$\Delta x_i = \frac{\cos\theta_i - \cos\theta_{i+1}}{p\,g_i},\qquad \Delta t_i = \frac{1}{g_i}\ln\!\left[\frac{c_{i+1}}{c_i}\cdot\frac{1 + \cos\theta_i}{1 + \cos\theta_{i+1}}\right],\qquad \sin\theta_{i+1} = p\,c_{i+1},$$

(for $g_i \to 0$ use the straight-line limits $\Delta x = \Delta z\tan\theta$, $\Delta t = \Delta z/(c\cos\theta)$). The algorithm accumulates $\Delta t_i$ layer by layer until the one-way travel time $\tau/2$ is exhausted, interpolating within the final layer, and returns $(x, z)$ of the bottom point. **Refraction-error magnitude:** for a uniform speed error $\delta c$ applied to the whole column, the depth error at beam angle $\phi$ is approximately $\delta z \approx z\,\frac{\delta c}{c}\,(1 - \tan^2\phi)$ when the surface speed is also wrong by $\delta c$ (steering error partly cancels the range error), and $\delta z \approx -z\,\frac{\delta c}{c}\tan^2\phi$ for the steering term alone; the $\tan^2\phi$ dependence is the smile/frown. With $z = 50$ m, $\delta c/c = 0.2$ % (3 m/s), $\phi = 60°$: $\tan^2\phi = 3$, so the outer-beam error is of the order of 50 × 0.002 × 3 ≈ 0.3 m — above the Special Order allowance of 0.45 m at 95 % only in combination with other terms, but a clear curl visible in every swath.

**Footprint.** Nadir footprint $2d\tan(\theta/2)$; across-track footprint at $\phi$: $d\,\theta/\cos^2\phi$; along-track: $d\,\theta_T/\cos\phi$. For $\theta = \theta_T = 1°$ = 0.01745 rad and $d = 1{,}000$ m: nadir 17.5 m; at 60°, 70 m across × 35 m along.

**TVU/THU.** For a sounding at depth $d$ and beam angle $\phi$ with roll uncertainty $\sigma_r$ (rad), heave/GNSS-height uncertainty $\sigma_h$, sound-speed relative uncertainty $\sigma_c/c$, detection range uncertainty $\sigma_\rho$, draft/lever uncertainty $\sigma_o$, water-level uncertainty $\sigma_w$:

$$\sigma_z^2 \approx (d\tan\phi\,\sigma_r)^2 + \sigma_h^2 + \left(d\,\frac{\sigma_c}{c}(1 + \tan^2\phi)\right)^2 + (\cos\phi\,\sigma_\rho)^2 + \sigma_o^2 + \sigma_w^2,$$

and $\text{TVU} = 1.96\,\sigma_z$. With $d = 30$ m, $\phi = 60°$, $\sigma_r = 0.02° = 3.5\times10^{-4}$ rad, $\sigma_h = 0.05$ m, $\sigma_c/c = 0.001$, $\sigma_\rho = 0.05$ m, $\sigma_o = 0.03$ m, $\sigma_w = 0.08$ m: terms 0.018, 0.05, 0.12, 0.025, 0.03, 0.08 → $\sigma_z = 0.16$ m, TVU = 0.31 m against Order 1a's 0.63 m and Special Order's 0.34 m; the sound-speed term dominates at the swath edge. The THU combines the positioning uncertainty with heading × across-track distance and the horizontal part of the refraction error: $\sigma_{xy}^2 \approx \sigma_{\text{pos}}^2 + (d\tan\phi\,\sigma_\psi)^2 + \ldots$

**CUBE weighting.** A sounding with depth $z_i$, vertical variance $\sigma_{v,i}^2$, horizontal variance $\sigma_{h,i}^2$, at distance $r_i$ from a node, is given an effective variance $\sigma_i^2 = \sigma_{v,i}^2 + \left(\frac{r_i + \sigma_{h,i}}{\Delta}\right)^{\alpha}\sigma_{v,i}^2$ (distance-inflated, with node spacing $\Delta$ and exponent $\alpha \approx 2$), and the hypothesis estimate updates as $\hat z \leftarrow \frac{\hat z/\hat\sigma^2 + z_i/\sigma_i^2}{1/\hat\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_i^2}$, $\hat\sigma^2 \leftarrow (1/\hat\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_i^2)^{-1}$; a sounding is admitted to a hypothesis if $|z_i - \hat z| < k\,\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2 + \sigma_i^2}$ (with $k$ set by the chosen confidence), otherwise it seeds a new one (Calder & Mayer 2003).

**Patch-test geometry.** On reciprocal lines over a flat bottom a roll offset $\delta r$ produces a depth difference between passes of $\Delta z(\phi) = 2d\tan\phi\,\delta r$ (hence $\delta r = \Delta z/(2d\tan\phi)$ from the outer-beam difference); on reciprocal lines over a slope, a pitch offset $\delta p$ displaces a feature along track by $\Delta x = 2d\,\delta p$; a latency $\delta t$ between passes at speeds $v_1 \neq v_2$ in the same direction displaces it by $\Delta x = (v_1 - v_2)\,\delta t$; a yaw offset $\delta\psi$ displaces a feature at across-track distance $y$ by $\Delta x = 2y\,\delta\psi$ between adjacent same-direction lines with the feature on opposite sides.

## Validation & uncertainty

**Calibrate, then prove.** The patch test, the dimensional offset survey, the waterline and squat measurements, and the sound-speed regime are the *inputs* to the TPU model; validation shows that the model's prediction is met. The evidence hierarchy, from cheapest to most independent:

1. **Swath-internal checks.** Flat-bottom swaths viewed across track should be flat to within the detection noise; curl means refraction, tilt means roll, ribbing along track means heave/latency/induced heave. These are inspected during acquisition and quantified afterwards by fitting planes to flat patches and reporting residuals by beam angle.
2. **Overlap statistics.** Adjacent lines' overlaps, compared surface-to-surface by beam-angle bin, reveal sector-to-sector offsets, roll residuals, and day-to-day water-level or draft changes. Report the mean and standard deviation of the differences per line pair.
3. **Crosslines.** Compared sounding-to-mainscheme surface, binned by beam angle and depth, with the statistics tested against the order's TVU: the standard expectation is that the 95 % bound of the differences is within the allowance, after allowing for the fact that both surfaces carry uncertainty (their difference has variance equal to the sum). Crosslines run under different tide, cast, and loading are the only check on the time-varying terms.
4. **Reference surface.** Repeat survey of the reference area at the start and end of the project, compared with the archived surface; this is the absolute vertical check and the drift monitor.
5. **Independent control.** Where available: a lidar-surveyed intertidal flat, RTK-surveyed points on hard bottoms at low water, or an ALB overlap ([Chapter 19](ch19-bathymetric-lidar.md)); a GNSS-at-tide-gauge comparison for ERS; a known object (wreck, placed target) for feature detection and least depth.

> **Worked example.** Crossline analysis for a 20–45 m Special Order harbour-approach survey, 300 kHz dual-head MBES, ERS. 18,400 crossline soundings compared with the CUBE mainscheme surface. Overall: mean −0.02 m, standard deviation 0.11 m, 95 % of |differences| < 0.22 m — within Special Order's 0.30–0.42 m over this depth range. By beam angle: the 50–65° bins show a mean of −0.09 m on the port head and +0.08 m on starboard, the inner bins ±0.01 m. The antisymmetric outer-beam pattern with sign reversal between heads is a residual roll offset on each head of about $\delta r = 0.09/(2\times30\times\tan 58°) \approx 0.94$ mrad = 0.054°, below the 0.1° the patch test was accepted at, but costing a quarter of the allowance at the swath edges. The crossline statistics pass; the report still recommends a refined roll value from a least-squares patch-test re-fit, because the next survey in 60 m will not pass with it.

**What to report.** Vessel configuration with a signed offset diagram; patch-test lines, method, values, and residuals; sound-speed casts (where, when, profiles) and the surface-speed record; water-level source and zoning, or the ERS separation model and its uncertainty; TPU parameters; crossline statistics by angle and depth; reference-surface comparison; CUBE parameters and hypothesis-count map; cleaning log; designated soundings and features; unresolved anomalies. This is the Descriptive Report's content, and it is what makes a survey auditable when a vessel grounds on the chart made from it ([Chapter 56](ch56-case-files.md)).

## Pitfalls

- **Grid resolution finer than the depth-dependent footprint** → a 0.5 m grid in 100 m of water from a 1° system → detect by computing $d\theta$; use depth-banded or variable resolution.
- **Sound-speed casts too infrequent in stratified water** → smiles/frowns and depth-proportional errors that crosslines reveal too late → detect from swath curl in real time; cast on a schedule set by the water, not the clock, and log surface speed continuously.
- **Mis-set waterline or draft** → a constant vertical bias in every tide-reduced sounding → detect with the reference surface or a crossline from another day/loading; use ERS where possible.
- **Squat ignored at survey speed** → 5–20 cm bias that changes with speed → measure squat by RTK at several speeds; apply or hold speed constant.
- **Over-cleaning that removes real shoals** → aggressive filters or CUBE disambiguation erase a rock pinnacle or a mast → review hypothesis-count maps and water-column data over features; never delete without a reason recorded.
- **Averaging surfaces used for charting** → the CUBE mean is not the least depth → designate soundings on features and shoal-bias the chart selection.
- **Bubble sweep-down on the transducer** → dropouts and noisy outer beams in head seas → watch the water column; slow down, change heading, or re-site the transducer.
- **Patch test done in the wrong order or on the wrong bottom** → pitch contaminated by latency, roll on a sloping bottom → follow latency → pitch → roll → yaw on the prescribed bottoms, at survey speed, and verify with fresh lines.
- **Lever-arm sign conventions mixed between acquisition and processing software** → double-applied or reversed offsets → maintain one signed configuration diagram; check with a flat-bottom reciprocal pair.
- **Tide gauge far from the survey without zoning** → spatially varying water-level error of decimetres → model the zoning or use ERS with a separation model.

## Key takeaways

- Sonar resolution degrades with depth by physics — footprint $\approx d\theta$ grows linearly with depth and as $1/\cos^2\phi$ across the swath — so bathymetric products are inherently variable-resolution and must be gridded accordingly.
- The vertical budget is dominated by water level (or GNSS height and separation model), sound speed, and vessel geometry (roll, lever arms, draft, squat); the acoustic range itself is a minor term in shallow water.
- Refraction and roll errors scale with $\tan\phi$ and $\tan^2\phi$: the outer beams are where the errors live, where the patch test and crosslines must be examined, and where the usable swath ends.
- The patch test calibrates angular offsets and latency in a fixed order; it does not calibrate lever arms, waterline, sound speed, or alignment, which need their own procedures.
- TPU per sounding, S-44 TVU/THU by order, and CUBE/CHRT estimation with uncertainty are one chain: calibrate, propagate, estimate, then prove with crosslines and a reference surface.
- Navigation surfaces must be shoal-biased with designated soundings and must carry uncertainty; averaging surfaces are not charts.
- Backscatter and water-column data are part of the deliverable: they classify the seabed, find the least depth over features, and diagnose the artefacts that corrupt bottom detection.
