# Chapter 11 — A history of positioning: from plumb bobs to PPP

> **Part IV — Positioning and orientation.** This chapter opens the part on positioning by tracing how humans learned to say *where* with increasing confidence, so that the GNSS, inertial, acoustic, and SLAM chapters that follow ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md) through [Chapter 15](ch15-slam.md)) can be read as the latest entries in a very long ledger rather than as settled truth.

**In this chapter.** Every elevation dataset is pinned to the ground by a positioning technology, and every positioning technology was, in its day, "accurate." This chapter follows the ledger from the Egyptian plumb bob and the gnomon through triangulation, chronometers, radio hyperbolae, inertial platforms, and satellites to today's precise point positioning, keeping the gis-history timeline tags (⟨H⟩) where the entries come from that source. The aim is practical, not antiquarian: after reading it you should be able to date a dataset by the positioning method embedded in it, estimate the error envelope that method implies (tens of metres for LORAN-C, metres for Transit, decimetres for early differential GPS, centimetres for RTK), recognize the legacy positions that still live on modern charts and in modern DEMs, and read historical accuracy claims with the vocabulary of [Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md) rather than at face value. The recurring lesson is that each generation's "accurate" became the next generation's "biased," and the merged products you build inherit the vintage of their weakest input.

## 11.1 Antiquity to the Renaissance

Positioning began as the problem of the vertical, not the horizontal. The **plumb bob** — a weight on a string that finds the local gravity vector — appears in Egyptian building and surveying practice in the third millennium BC ⟨H⟩, and with it the concepts that still underlie every levelling instrument and every orthometric height in this book: a physical "down" defined by gravity, and a horizontal defined as perpendicular to it. The merkhet (a plumb line sighted against a slotted palm rib) let Egyptian surveyors align monuments to the meridian from star transits; the A-frame level with a hanging plumb line let them carry a horizontal across a construction site. Nothing in modern geodesy departs from this definition of horizontal; we have merely learned that the plumb line is deflected by local mass, by amounts of a few arc seconds to tens of arc seconds in mountains ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

The **gnomon** — a vertical stick whose shadow is measured — added latitude. The ratio of shadow length to gnomon height at local noon on a known date gives the solar altitude and hence latitude to roughly a degree with care. Eratosthenes of Cyrene (around 240 BC) turned this into geodesy: comparing the noon shadow at Alexandria with the reported vertical sun at Syene on the summer solstice and the distance between them, he estimated the Earth's circumference. The result is conventionally quoted as 250 000 stadia; because the length of his stadion is uncertain the modern-unit equivalent ranges from about 2 % to 16 % from the true value, and the honest statement is that the method was sound and the inputs (the Alexandria–Syene distance, the assumption that both lie on a meridian) were rough. It is the first documented case of a positioning result whose accuracy claim depends entirely on units and assumptions that later readers could not fully recover — a pattern this chapter returns to.

Hipparchus of Nicaea (about 140 BC ⟨H⟩) compiled the first known trigonometric table (a table of chords), proposed a systematic grid of latitude and longitude, and realized that longitude could be fixed by timing a lunar eclipse at two places — the first time-is-position idea, two millennia before the chronometer made it routine. His star catalogue, measured against a coordinate frame, also let him discover precession by comparison with older observations: the first geodetic change detection, and the first demonstration that reference frames themselves drift ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

The **magnetic compass** was known in China by the Han dynasty (lodestone spoons are described around 206 BC) and used for maritime navigation by the eleventh century ⟨H⟩; it reached Mediterranean navigators by the late twelfth century. A compass provides a heading, not a position, but combined with a log (speed) and a sandglass (time) it enabled **dead reckoning**, the integration of velocity to estimate position — the same operation an inertial navigator performs today, with the same error law: error grows with time since the last fix. The **Antikythera mechanism** ⟨H⟩, a geared astronomical calculator recovered from a first-century-BC shipwreck, is a reminder that the computational side of positioning — predicting where celestial bodies would be, so that observing them could tell you where you were — is equally ancient.

Medieval **portolan charts** (late thirteenth century onward) drew coastlines from compass bearings and estimated distances between ports, criss-crossed with rhumb lines from compass roses. They are strikingly accurate in shape over the Mediterranean (a few percent in scale) yet have no graticule and are rotated by the magnetic declination of their era, roughly 8–10° counterclockwise relative to true north — an entire map class systematically biased by a quantity (declination) nobody yet modelled. Gerardus Mercator's 1569 world map ⟨H⟩ then introduced the projection that makes rhumb lines straight, solving the navigator's problem at the price of area distortion that still misleads map readers ([Chapter 10](ch10-projections-and-resampling.md)).

## 11.2 Instruments

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries supplied the measuring tools that the national surveys of §11.4 would use for two hundred years. The **plane table** (described by Abel Foullon in 1551) let a surveyor draw a map directly in the field by sighting with an alidade and intersecting rays from two stations; it is the ancestor of every graphical triangulation. The **theodolite** — the word first appears in Leonard Digges's *Pantometria* (1571, published by his son Thomas in 1576) for an instrument that measured horizontal angles with a graduated circle — became, with the addition of a telescope and a vertical circle, the universal instrument of geodesy. Angle precision improved from degrees to arc minutes (Ramsden's great theodolite of 1787, 3 ft circle, read to about 1″ with micrometers) and eventually to tenths of an arc second in twentieth-century Wild and Kern instruments.

Distance was the weaker measurement for most of this period. **Gunter's chain** (1620) — 66 ft in 100 links, so that 10 square chains make an acre and 80 chains make a statute mile — was the standard for property and cadastral measurement in the English-speaking world until steel tapes; its precision was a few parts per thousand in ordinary use, and chained baselines for geodetic triangulation required glass rods, bimetallic bars, or invar wires in catenary to reach parts per million. Because distance was so hard, triangulation networks measured only a handful of baselines and carried scale by angles; a baseline error propagated as a scale error across an entire country, and when later surveys remeasured old networks with electronic distance measurement the scale discrepancies were typically a few parts per million but occasionally much worse ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md) on datum distortions).

At sea, the **octant** (Hadley and Godfrey independently, 1731, following Newton's unpublished 1699 design) and the **sextant** (Campbell, 1757, extending the arc to measure angles up to 120°) made celestial altitude observations possible from a moving deck to about 1′ — the equivalent of 1 nmi (1.85 km) in latitude. The **Brunton pocket transit** (1894) gave geologists a hand compass with clinometer, good to about half a degree, still the instrument of structural geology. The real break came with electronics: the **tellurometer** (Trevor Wadley, South Africa, 1957 ⟨H⟩) measured distance by the phase of a modulated microwave carrier to a few centimetres plus a few parts per million over tens of kilometres, and the geodimeter (Bergstrand, 1947–1953) did the same with modulated light. Electronic distance measurement inverted the geometry of surveying: trilateration became as easy as triangulation, and in the 1970s the combination of an electronic theodolite, an EDM, and a microprocessor produced the **total station**, which measures a 3D vector to a reflector in one operation. A modern total station resolves angles to 0.5–1″ and distances to about 1 mm + 1–2 ppm; it remains the most accurate general-purpose positioning instrument for ranges under a kilometre and the reference against which GNSS checkpoints are often verified ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md), §14.5).

> **Definitions that bite.** "Accuracy" in pre-twentieth-century survey reports usually means *closure* — the misfit when a traverse or triangle is checked against itself — not the deviation from a truth that did not exist. A triangulation with 1″ triangle closures can still be rotated and scaled relative to the modern frame by tens of metres over a few hundred kilometres. When you read "accurate to one part in 50 000," ask: relative to what?

## 11.3 Longitude and time

Latitude was easy — measure the altitude of the pole star or the noon sun. Longitude was a clock problem: one hour of time difference equals 15° of longitude, so knowing the local time (from the sun) and the time at a reference meridian gives longitude. The difficulty was carrying the reference time. Christiaan Huygens's **pendulum clock** (1656) made land-based astronomy precise enough to measure longitude differences by lunar eclipses and Jupiter's satellites but failed at sea. Ole Rømer's 1676 measurement of the finite speed of light came from exactly this work: the eclipses of Io ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter, which spoiled them as a longitude clock but founded a branch of physics.

John Harrison's marine chronometers culminated in **H4** (tested on the 1761–1762 Jamaica voyage, losing about 5 s over 81 days, or roughly 1.25′ of longitude), and the Board of Longitude's protracted dispute over whether it met the 1714 Longitude Act's half-degree standard is well told by Sobel (1995). The practical consequence for mapping was slower than the story suggests: chronometers remained expensive for decades, so coastlines surveyed before the mid-nineteenth century are often several arc minutes in error in longitude even when their latitudes are good. Islands "discovered" twice, reefs charted in two positions, and whole coastlines shifted east or west by kilometres persist in some chart lineages into the GPS era (§11.10).

The reference meridian itself was not agreed until the **International Meridian Conference** of 1884 ⟨H⟩ adopted Greenwich. Before that, French charts used Paris (2° 20′ 14″ east of Greenwich), Spanish charts Cádiz, and many national topographic series had their own prime meridians; Ferro (El Hierro) was used in central Europe, defined as exactly 20° west of Paris. The conference also recommended a universal day beginning at Greenwich midnight, the ancestor of **Universal Time** (the name adopted by the IAU in 1928 ⟨H⟩) and of **Coordinated Universal Time**, introduced in 1960 and placed on its present atomic basis with leap seconds in 1972 ⟨H⟩. [Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md) treats the time scales; the point here is that longitude is time, and the history of positioning is in large part the history of clocks — a lineage that runs unbroken from Harrison to the atomic clocks aboard GNSS satellites, where 1 ns of clock error is 30 cm of range.

## 11.4 National surveys

By the eighteenth century, states wanted maps of themselves, and the method was triangulation: measure one baseline, observe angles in a chain of triangles, and propagate coordinates. Four generations of the Cassini family carried the triangulation of France from the Paris meridian arc (1683–1718) to the complete *Carte de Cassini* (surveyed 1756–1789, 182 sheets at 1:86 400) — the first topographic map of an entire country based on geodetic control. The Cassini arc measurements also fed the eighteenth-century dispute over the Earth's flattening, resolved by the Lapland and Peru expeditions of the 1730s in favour of Newton's oblate spheroid ([Chapter 7](ch07-shape-of-the-earth.md)).

The **Principal Triangulation of Great Britain** (1791–1853 ⟨H⟩), begun by the Board of Ordnance under William Roy's Hounslow Heath baseline of 1784 and completed under Clarke, defined the Airy spheroid and the first national coordinate system; its retriangulation in 1936–1962 produced OSGB36, whose residual distortions of up to several metres are now modelled by the OSTN15 grid ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)). The **Survey of the Coast** was established in the United States in 1807 ⟨H⟩ under Ferdinand Hassler, becoming the Coast Survey, then the Coast and Geodetic Survey, and today NOAA's National Geodetic Survey and Office of Coast Survey; Theberge's history on NOAA's site documents how the early baselines, the 1833 Buttermilk Channel marks ⟨H⟩ (among the oldest surviving survey monuments of the agency, set during Hassler's New York Harbor triangulation), and the later transcontinental arc of 1871–1897 built the control that the North American Datum of 1927 would adjust. The **Great Trigonometrical Survey of India** (1802–1871), under Lambton and then George Everest, carried the Great Arc from Cape Comorin to the Himalaya, measured the heights of the peaks by vertical angles over 150 km distances (Peak XV, 1852–1856, 29 002 ft), and in doing so confronted both atmospheric refraction and the deflection of the vertical — Pratt's analysis of why the plumb line was not pulled toward the Himalaya as much as expected produced isostasy.

The mathematics of these surveys is the mathematics of this book. Carl Friedrich Gauss, triangulating the Kingdom of Hanover from 1818, used **least squares** (which he had developed for orbit determination; Legendre published it first in 1805) to adjust redundant angle observations, invented the heliotrope to make distant stations visible, and worked out the conformal projection and the geodesy of curved surfaces that the practical problem required. Least-squares adjustment with redundancy and residual analysis — the spine of [Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md) and [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) — is a nineteenth-century survey technology.

The **US Geological Survey** was created in 1879 ⟨H⟩ to map the public lands; its topographic quadrangles, contoured from plane-table and later photogrammetric surveys on the horizontal datums of 1927 and vertical datum of 1929, are the source of the first generation of US DEMs (the 1:24 000 DEMs digitized from contours in the 1970s–1990s), whose characteristic stair-step and "contour ghost" artefacts are inherited positioning and interpolation history ([Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)).

> **Case file.** When NAD27 was readjusted as NAD83 in 1986, positions in the conterminous United States shifted by up to about 100 m in the west and about 10–40 m in the east, and the heights of first-order triangulation stations were found to carry systematic errors of metres where trigonometric levelling had been used. The shifts were not random — they were the accumulated scale and orientation error of a triangulation network that had been adjusted piecemeal with 1927-era computing. Any map, DEM, or sounding sheet referenced to NAD27 carries those shifts. NGS's NADCON grid models them to roughly 0.15 m (1σ) in CONUS where control is dense, but the original data were never that good; the transformation is more precise than the positions it transforms (Snay & Soler 2008; [Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md)).

## 11.5 Radio navigation

The Second World War produced positioning by radio. **Gee** (UK, operational 1942, developed from 1940) measured the time difference of arrival of pulses from a master and slave stations, placing the receiver on a hyperbola; two pairs gave a fix, accurate to a few hundred metres near the stations and a few kilometres at 500 km. **LORAN** (Loran-A, US, 1942) applied the same hyperbolic principle at lower frequency (1.85–1.95 MHz) for ranges of about 1 000 km by day over sea with accuracies of a few kilometres. **Decca** (UK, 1942, commercial from 1946) used continuous-wave phase comparison at 70–130 kHz, giving repeatable fixes of tens of metres within a chain's coverage — good enough that Decca lanes were printed as lattices on fishing and survey charts, and a generation of North Sea and coastal surveys were positioned in "Decca coordinates" whose conversion to geographic coordinates depended on the chain's propagation model. The Soviet **CHAYKA** system (operational from 1969) and **LORAN-C** (100 kHz, US Coast Guard, opened to civil use in 1974 ⟨H⟩) extended coverage to continental scale with absolute accuracies of about 0.25 nmi (460 m) and repeatable accuracies of 20–90 m. **Omega** (eight VLF stations, 10–14 kHz, 1971–1997) gave worldwide coverage at 2–4 nmi accuracy. These numbers matter because many pre-1990 bathymetric surveys in national archives — the ones that still underlie charts and the "legacy" layers of compiled bathymetry such as GEBCO ([Chapter 55](ch55-public-products.md)) — were positioned by exactly these systems, and their horizontal error must be carried into any vertical comparison on a sloping seafloor.

The hyperbolic systems were switched off as GPS matured: Omega in 1997, Decca in 2000, US LORAN-C in 2010. The dependence that followed, and the demonstrated ease of jamming GNSS ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.8), has driven the **eLoran** revival — an enhanced LORAN-C with data channel, differential corrections, and time-of-transmission control giving 8–20 m accuracy in tests — maintained by South Korea, under renewed study in the US and UK, and treated in [Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md).

## 11.6 Inertial navigation

The **V-2** rocket's guidance (1942 ⟨H⟩) is generally counted as the first inertial navigation system: gyroscopes to hold a reference orientation and accelerometers integrated to velocity and position, with no external signal. Post-war development produced gimballed platforms good enough for submarines (the SINS systems of the 1950s), the Apollo guidance computer, and the airline inertial navigators of the 1970s, drifting about 1 nmi per hour. Three advances made inertial sensing the companion of every modern mapping sensor. First, Rudolf Kálmán's 1960 recursive filter ⟨H⟩ provided the mathematics to blend inertial dead reckoning with intermittent external fixes in a statistically optimal way — the GNSS/INS integration of [Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md) is a Kalman filter with specific state and measurement models. Second, **strapdown** mechanization replaced the physical gimballed platform with computation: with sensors bolted to the vehicle, the attitude is integrated numerically, which became practical as computers and ring-laser and fibre-optic gyroscopes matured in the 1970s–1980s. Third, **MEMS** gyroscopes and accelerometers (1990s onward) brought inertial sensing to a few dollars and a few millimetres, enabling drones, phones, and handheld scanners — at the price of drift rates thousands of times larger than navigation-grade units, which is why consumer-grade inertial positioning is useless without continuous aiding ([Chapter 13](ch13-imu-ins.md), §13.1).

Inertial navigation's contribution to elevation data is less about position than about **orientation**: a scanning lidar or multibeam sonar needs to know where it is pointing to a few thousandths of a degree, and no satellite system can tell it that. The history of airborne lidar is in large part the history of making IMUs small, cheap, and well-characterized enough to ride on aircraft and then drones.

## 11.7 Satellites

**Sputnik 1** (October 1957 ⟨H⟩) was positioned from the ground by the Doppler shift of its 20 MHz beacon; within days, researchers at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory realized the problem could be inverted — a satellite in a known orbit could position a receiver on the ground. The result was **Transit** (the Navy Navigation Satellite System, operational 1964, released for civil use in 1967). A Transit fix required integrating the Doppler curve over a 10–15 minute pass and knowing the receiver's velocity; passes came every hour or two; a single-pass fix was good to roughly 100–200 m for a moving ship and a multi-day static occupation could reach a few metres. Transit's static mode — "Doppler surveying" with receivers such as the Magnavox Geoceiver — is historically important for geodesy because it produced the first global, satellite-based datum points (the WGS 72 and Doppler-derived NAD83 (1986) control) and the first direct measurements of the geoid-to-ellipsoid separation over the oceans. Transit was switched off in 1996.

The **Global Positioning System** launched its first Block I satellite in 1978 ⟨H⟩, reached full operational capability in 1995, and defined the modern template: a constellation broadcasting ranging codes and precise time on L-band carriers, so that a receiver solving for four unknowns (three coordinates and a clock offset) from four or more pseudoranges gets an instantaneous fix. The Soviet **GLONASS** followed with its first launch in 1982 ⟨H⟩, reached a full constellation in 1995, decayed in the late 1990s, and was restored by 2011. Civil GPS was deliberately degraded by **Selective Availability**, which dithered the clock to limit horizontal accuracy to about 100 m (95 %); SA was switched off on 2 May 2000 ⟨H⟩, overnight improving standalone civil accuracy to about 10–15 m. In the interim, differential techniques had made SA irrelevant for surveying: the US Coast Guard's maritime **DGPS** beacon network reached initial operating capability in 1996 ⟨H⟩ broadcasting pseudorange corrections at 285–325 kHz for 1–3 m accuracy, and the FAA's **WAAS** was commissioned for aviation in 2003 ⟨H⟩ (EGNOS in Europe followed in 2009), providing satellite-broadcast corrections that give 1–2 m horizontal and 2–4 m vertical accuracy to any receiver with the right firmware.

Centimetre positioning came from the carrier phase. Geodesists had tracked the phase of the GPS carrier since the early 1980s (the Macrometer, 1982, needed no codes at all) and resolved its integer ambiguities over static baselines to obtain millimetre-level relative positions — which is how plate motion was first measured directly ([Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)). **Real-time kinematic** positioning — resolving ambiguities on the fly with a radio link to a base — became commercial in the early-to-mid 1990s (Trimble's 4000SSi RTK, 1994, is often cited; the on-the-fly ambiguity-resolution literature dates from 1989–1993, and the LAMBDA method from Teunissen 1995). **Precise point positioning** (Zumberge et al. 1997) dispensed with the base station entirely by using precise satellite orbits and clocks from a global network, reaching a few centimetres after tens of minutes of convergence; PPP with ambiguity resolution (PPP-AR, from about 2008) and PPP-RTK bring convergence toward minutes.

None of this works without infrastructure and formats. The **NMEA 0183** interface standard (1984 ⟨H⟩) gave receivers a common sentence format — still the lingua franca of position output in survey launches and drones, with its 1980s-era limitations on precision and timestamping ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)). **RINEX** (Receiver Independent Exchange format, Gurtner and Mader, 1989 ⟨H⟩) made raw observations portable between receivers and software and remains the archive format of GNSS geodesy. The **International GPS Service** (now the International GNSS Service, IGS) began routine operations on 1 January 1994 and the US **CORS** network was formally established the same year; together they supply the precise orbits, clocks, station coordinates, and antenna calibrations that every post-processed survey depends on. China's **BeiDou** launched its first experimental satellite in 2000 ⟨H⟩ and reached global coverage with BDS-3 in 2020; Europe's **Galileo** launched its first two operational satellites in 2011 ⟨H⟩ and declared Initial Services in December 2016 ⟨H⟩. Japan's QZSS and India's NavIC add regional augmentation. The multi-constellation sky of the 2020s — more than 120 navigation satellites, typically 25–40 above the horizon at mid-latitudes — is the single largest reason that survey-grade positioning under partial sky obstruction became routine ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.1).

> **Worked example.** Dating a dataset by its positioning. A hydrographic survey sheet of a US east-coast estuary states "positions by Raydist" and a survey date of 1968. Raydist was a medium-frequency phase-comparison (range–range) system with repeatable accuracy of a few metres near shore but absolute accuracy limited by the surveyed positions of the shore stations and the propagation model, typically 10–30 m. The soundings are lead-line and single-beam echo sounder, reduced to the tidal datum of the time. Combining this with a modern multibeam survey positioned by RTK (2 cm) on a channel slope of 1:20 (2.9°), a 20 m horizontal uncertainty in the old data maps to $20 \times \tan 2.9° \approx 1.0$ m of apparent depth difference — larger than the real channel change you were hoping to detect. The 1968 sheet is not wrong; it is being asked a question it cannot answer ([Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md)).

## 11.8 Underwater

Beneath the surface, radio does not propagate and satellites cannot help; underwater positioning recapitulates the whole history in miniature. The **lead line** — a weight on a marked rope, with tallow in a cup to sample the bottom — gave depth and, through the sample, a crude position-by-sediment for centuries; its soundings positioned by sextant angles to shore marks (the three-point fix with a station pointer) underlie the oldest layer of every chart. The echo sounder (1920s) solved depth but not position; surface positioning by Decca, LORAN, or Raydist (§11.5) positioned the vessel, and the sounder's position was assumed equal to it.

For instruments *below* the surface — towed sonars, ROVs, AUVs, drill strings — acoustics replaced radio. **Long-baseline (LBL)** positioning with seafloor transponders, developed in the 1960s–1970s for offshore oil and for deep-submergence vehicles such as *Alvin*, measures ranges from the vehicle to transponders at surveyed positions, giving repeatability of a metre or better within the array but absolute accuracy limited by how well the array was surveyed from the surface. **Short-baseline (SBL)** and **ultra-short-baseline (USBL)** systems mount the array on the ship and measure range and bearing to a single transponder on the vehicle, trading array deployment for an accuracy that degrades with range (typically 0.1–0.5 % of slant range) and depends on the ship's own attitude and position. The modern deep-water solution is an **INS aided by a Doppler velocity log** (DVL, which measures velocity over the seafloor acoustically) and periodically corrected by USBL or LBL fixes — the underwater equivalent of GNSS/INS, with drift of roughly 0.1–0.5 % of distance travelled between fixes for good systems. [Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md) gives the mechanics; the historical point is that deep-sea bathymetry collected before the 1990s has horizontal uncertainties of hundreds of metres to kilometres, and that AUV-collected bathymetry today is positioned by a dead-reckoning chain whose error you must budget explicitly.

## 11.9 The mobile age

Two developments since 2000 moved positioning from the survey crew to the population. The first was **positioning from signals of opportunity**: the practice of **wardriving** (the term dates from about 2000 ⟨H⟩, when Peter Shipley's drives around Berkeley mapping WiFi access points were publicized) created databases of access-point locations that Skyhook, Google, and Apple turned into WiFi positioning for phones, accurate to tens of metres where access points are dense; cell-tower positioning (hundreds of metres to kilometres) and, since about 2010, Bluetooth beacons (metres, indoors) fill the gaps. Fused with GNSS and the phone's barometer and IMU, this is the "location services" that every citizen now carries, and it is the positioning behind crowdsourced elevation and bathymetry contributions ([Chapter 16](ch16-platforms.md), §16.10) — whose accuracy is heterogeneous and whose metadata rarely says which source produced any given fix.

The second was the arrival of raw GNSS measurements and dual-frequency chips in phones (Android exposed raw measurements in 2016; the first dual-frequency phone, the Xiaomi Mi 8 with a Broadcom BCM47755, shipped in 2018). Phones now produce carrier-phase observations of a quality that permits decimetre positioning under good conditions ([Chapter 12](ch12-gnss.md), §12.9). At scale they also become a sensor network: Smith et al. (2024) ⟨H⟩ used ionospheric delay measurements from millions of Android phones to map total electron content with coverage comparable to or exceeding the fixed monitoring networks over much of the globe — an inversion of the historical relationship in which a few hundred geodetic stations served everyone, and an illustration that error sources (here the ionosphere) become observables when enough receivers look at them.

## 11.10 What history teaches about error

Reading the ledger end to end yields a few durable lessons.

**Each generation's "accurate" became the next generation's "biased."** Portolans were accurate until declination was understood; triangulations were accurate until EDM revealed their scale errors; NAD27 was accurate until Doppler and VLBI showed it was 100 m off in the west; Transit was accurate until GPS; GPS code positioning was accurate until carrier phase; ITRF2008 was accurate until ITRF2014 shifted its scale by about 1 ppb (roughly 6 mm at the Earth's surface). The error that each era could not see was a systematic one — a quantity that was constant or slowly varying across the whole dataset and therefore invisible to internal checks. Internal precision (closures, repeatability) has never been a guarantee of external accuracy, and the vocabulary of [Chapter 5](ch05-error-and-uncertainty.md) — bias versus dispersion, precision versus accuracy, relative versus absolute — is the tool for keeping the two apart.

**Positions outlive the technology that produced them.** Charted rocks fixed by sextant in 1850, Decca-positioned soundings from 1965, and NAD27 benchmarks from 1930 are still in use, transformed (or not) into modern frames by grid interpolation that is smoother than the original errors. The IHO's **Zones of Confidence** (CATZOC) scheme exists precisely because chart compilers must tell navigators that the position accuracy of a sounding may be ±500 m if it came from a pre-1970 survey ([Chapter 62](ch62-navigation-and-charting.md)). A compiled DEM or bathymetric grid that blends sources of different vintages inherits the worst positioning in each cell, and its metadata should say so ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md), [Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

**Improvement has been roughly tenfold per generation, and each step changed what the datum *is*.** Hyperbolic radio (hundreds of metres) → Transit (tens of metres) → GPS code (metres) → differential (decimetres) → carrier phase (centimetres) → modern geodetic frames (millimetres per year). Each step also moved the definition of "where" from a physical network of monuments to a computed frame of satellite orbits and station velocities, so that modern coordinates come with an epoch and a velocity ([Chapter 8](ch08-horizontal-datums.md), [Chapter 38](ch38-plate-motion-and-vlm.md)). An old dataset cannot simply be "put on" a modern frame; it can only be transformed with the uncertainty that the transformation and its own positioning imply.

**Time has always been the limiting measurement.** Hipparchus's eclipses, Harrison's chronometer, LORAN's pulse timing, GNSS's atomic clocks, and the timestamp that aligns a lidar pulse with an IMU sample at 200 Hz are the same problem at different scales ([Chapter 6](ch06-time-as-coordinate.md)). When a positioning system fails in the field today it is more often a timing failure — a dropped PPS, an unsynchronized logger, a leap-second mishandled — than a geometric one.

**Dependence invites fragility.** The world turned off LORAN, Decca, and Omega within fifteen years of GPS reaching full capability, and the result is a single point of failure that a $20 jammer can exploit; the eLoran revival and the rediscovery of celestial navigation in naval training are historical corrections ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)).

> **Rule of thumb.** For an archival survey of unknown positioning method, assume the horizontal uncertainty appropriate to the best *civil* technology of its decade: pre-1940 visual fixes, 50–500 m offshore; 1940s–1960s hyperbolic radio, 100 m–2 km; 1970s–1980s Transit/LORAN-C, 50–500 m; 1990–2000 code DGPS, 1–5 m; post-2000 standalone GPS, 3–10 m; post-2005 RTK/PPK where documented, 0.02–0.1 m. These are order-of-magnitude priors to be replaced by the survey's own metadata whenever it exists, and they say nothing about the vertical, which depends on the datum reductions of the time ([Chapter 9](ch09-vertical-datums.md)).

## Validation & uncertainty

The historical record is itself a source of uncertainty information, and reading it correctly is a validation skill.

**Treat the positioning method as metadata of first importance.** For any dataset older than about 2005, determine how it was positioned before you determine anything else. Survey sheets, descriptive reports, and chart source diagrams usually state the system (sextant fixes, Decca chain and lane numbers, LORAN-C rates, Transit, DGPS, RTK). Where they do not, the date and the agency are proxies: the Rule of thumb above gives order-of-magnitude priors. Record the inferred horizontal uncertainty $\sigma_{xy}$ in the dataset's lineage ([Chapter 49](ch49-metadata.md)).

**Propagate horizontal error into the vertical.** On a surface with slope $s$ (rise over run), a horizontal uncertainty $\sigma_{xy}$ produces an apparent vertical uncertainty $\sigma_z \approx s\,\sigma_{xy}$ when the dataset is compared to another at the same nominal coordinates. For LORAN-C-positioned soundings ($\sigma_{xy} \approx 100$ m) on a continental slope of 3° ($s = 0.052$), $\sigma_z \approx 5$ m — larger than the depth-measurement error of the echo sounder. [Chapter 53](ch53-accuracy-assessment.md) formalizes this as the slope-dependent component of vertical error; [Chapter 41](ch41-change-detection.md) uses it to set the minimum detectable change.

**Distinguish repeatable from absolute.** Hyperbolic radio systems and early differential GPS had repeatable accuracies several times better than their absolute accuracies. A 1980 survey whose lines tie together to 20 m may be displaced as a whole by 200 m. Internal consistency of a legacy survey is not evidence of absolute position; only an independent tie — a charted feature, a re-surveyed pipeline, a seamount summit — tests the absolute component.

**Check datum and frame explicitly.** Legacy positions may be on a local datum (NAD27, ED50, Tokyo, a Decca chain's own spheroid), transformed once with a 3-parameter shift, transformed again with a 7-parameter shift, or never transformed. Ambiguity about which happened is common in compiled bathymetry. A feature that appears twice in a compilation, offset by 100–200 m, is usually the same feature on two datums ([Chapter 48](ch48-compositing.md)).

**Use historical control as validation targets with their own uncertainty.** Old benchmarks and triangulation stations are superb checkpoints for modern DEMs *if* their coordinates have been re-observed in the modern frame; NGS datasheets, for example, distinguish adjusted positions from scaled (map-derived) ones, and the latter can be 10 m or more in error horizontally — useless for a 1 m DEM. Read the datasheet's source code before trusting a mark ([Chapter 25](ch25-calibration-infrastructure.md), [Chapter 52](ch52-ground-truth.md)).

> **Uncertainty budget.** Order-of-magnitude horizontal positioning uncertainty (1σ, absolute) by era for coastal and offshore surveys, with the apparent vertical error it induces on a 2° seafloor slope ($s = 0.035$):
>
> | Method (era) | $\sigma_{xy}$ | Induced $\sigma_z$ on 2° slope |
> |---|---|---|
> | Sextant three-point fix, visible shore (pre-1940) | 20–100 m | 0.7–3.5 m |
> | Decca / Raydist (1950s–1980s), within chain | 10–50 m | 0.35–1.75 m |
> | LORAN-C (1974–2010), absolute | 100–460 m | 3.5–16 m |
> | Transit, ship underway (1967–1996) | 100–200 m | 3.5–7 m |
> | GPS with SA (1990–2000) | 30–50 m | 1–1.75 m |
> | Code DGPS (1996–) | 1–3 m | 0.035–0.1 m |
> | RTK/PPK (2000s–) | 0.02–0.05 m | < 0.002 m |
>
> The vertical column is what you would misattribute to "depth change" if you compared surveys at nominal coordinates without accounting for horizontal error. Values are typical ranges from the sources cited in this chapter, not guarantees for a specific survey.

**What to report.** For any product that merges data of different vintages: the positioning method and estimated $\sigma_{xy}$ of each source; the datum/frame and transformation applied, with its uncertainty; and a per-cell or per-source quality layer so that users can exclude legacy positions where the slope makes them harmful. This is exactly the content of the IHO's CATZOC attribute and of the uncertainty layer in BAG files ([Chapter 47](ch47-file-formats.md)); the history in this chapter is the reason those fields exist.

## Software

**Open source:** PROJ (datum and frame transformations, including NADCON/NTv2 grids for legacy datums); the `pyproj` and GDAL wrappers; GMT (historical chart digitizing and comparison); RTKLIB (reprocessing archived RINEX observations from 1990s surveys with modern precise products, which can improve a decade-old baseline from metres to centimetres if the raw data were kept); the gis-history repository itself (https://github.com/schwehr/gis-history) as a sourced timeline. **Free but closed:** NGS NADCON/VERTCON and HTDP (frame and epoch transformations for US legacy control); NOAA's historical chart and hydrographic survey archives (survey sheets with positioning method stated in the descriptive report). **Commercial:** CARIS HIPS/BASE Editor and QPS Qimera carry source-survey metadata and CATZOC through compilation; Trimble Business Center and Leica Infinity can reprocess archived raw GNSS data. One caveat: no software can recover absolute accuracy that was never in the data — reprocessing improves results only when raw observations, not just final positions, were archived.

## Standards & guides

- IHO S-44, *Standards for Hydrographic Surveys*, Edition 6.1.0 (2022): defines the positional and depth uncertainty orders against which legacy surveys are retrospectively classified.
- IHO S-57 / S-101 CATZOC (Category of Zone of Confidence): the attribute by which chart compilers encode source-survey positioning and depth accuracy for the mariner.
- IHO C-13, *Manual on Hydrography* (2005, with updates): chapter on positioning includes historical and legacy systems.
- NGS, *NADCON 5.0* and *HTDP* documentation: transformations from legacy US horizontal datums (NAD27, old Hawaiian, Puerto Rico, Alaska) and between NAD83 realizations, with stated uncertainties.
- NOAA Office of Coast Survey, *Hydrographic Surveys Specifications and Deliverables* (HSSD, annual): requires the descriptive report to state positioning method and uncertainty — the modern successor of the practice that lets us read old sheets.
- ISO 19115-1:2014 (Geographic information — Metadata): lineage and positional-accuracy elements in which positioning vintage should be recorded.

> **Try it.** Reprocess a legacy GNSS checkpoint to see how much positioning vintage matters. Take a RINEX 2 file from a 1990s static occupation (many national archives and the IGS/CDDIS hold them) and process it against a nearby CORS station with RTKLIB using modern IGS final orbits, then compare to the originally published coordinate.
>
> ```bash
> # Fetch IGS final products for the observation day (example: GPS week 0940, day 3 → 1998-01-14)
> # (verify the current CDDIS path and authentication requirements before running)
> wget https://cddis.nasa.gov/archive/gnss/products/0940/igs09403.sp3.Z
> uncompress igs09403.sp3.Z
>
> # Static baseline: rover = legacy file, base = CORS RINEX for the same day
> rnx2rtkp -p 3 -m 15 -f 2 -t -e -o legacy_static.pos \
>   -k static.conf legacy0140.98o cors0140.98o cors0140.98n igs09403.sp3
>
> # Compare final solution with the published 1998 coordinate (metres, local ENU)
> tail -1 legacy_static.pos
> ```
>
> Expected outcome: with several hours of dual-frequency data, a fixed solution agrees with the published coordinate to a few centimetres *after* you transform the published value from its original frame and epoch (for a 1998 US point, NAD83(1986) or an early HARN realization) to the frame of the CORS coordinates. If you skip the frame transformation, the discrepancy of 1–2 m you see is the datum, not the receiver — the lesson of §11.10 in one command.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating a legacy chart or sounding position as if it were GNSS-accurate** → chart compilation preserves old soundings in modern symbology, and nothing in the rendered chart says "sextant fix, 1887" → read the source diagram and CATZOC; assume hundreds of metres for pre-1970 offshore positions until proven otherwise.
- **Reading historical accuracy claims with today's definitions** → "accurate to 1:50 000" or "closure 2″" describe internal consistency, not deviation from a modern frame → separate relative from absolute accuracy; look for independent ties to the modern frame.
- **Assuming a datum transformation restores accuracy** → NADCON/NTv2 grids are smooth models of the average shift; original surveys had local distortions larger than the grid's residual → treat transformed legacy positions as having the original positioning uncertainty plus the transformation uncertainty.
- **Ignoring magnetic declination in compass-derived legacy data** → old bearings were magnetic; declination has changed by many degrees since (and differs by site) → correct with the historical geomagnetic model (gufm1/IGRF) appropriate to the survey date.
- **Confusing a prime meridian** → pre-1884 (and some later) national maps use Paris, Ferro, Cádiz, Pulkovo, Washington → check the sheet margin; a Ferro offset is 17° 40′ east of Greenwich, impossible to miss, but a Paris offset (2° 20′) can masquerade as a datum problem.
- **Assuming "Decca coordinates" or "LORAN rates" were converted to geographic correctly** → conversions used the chain's propagation model and spheroid; later re-conversions sometimes used a different model → where lane numbers or time differences are preserved, keep them in the archive so the conversion can be redone.
- **Merging surveys of different vintages without a slope-dependent vertical allowance** → horizontal error on a slope masquerades as depth change → compute $\sigma_z \approx s\,\sigma_{xy}$ per source and include it in the compilation's uncertainty layer.
- **Trusting "scaled" coordinates on old benchmark descriptions** → positions scaled from a 1:24 000 map are 10 m or worse → use only adjusted coordinates as checkpoints, and verify the vertical is from levelling, not from a map contour.
- **Believing that discontinuing an old system was free** → LORAN/Decca shutdown left no backup to GNSS and discarded repeatable-position infrastructure → plan for GNSS denial ([Chapter 14](ch14-positioning-beyond-gnss.md)) rather than assuming availability.
- **Discarding raw observations because the final positions were "good enough"** → final positions cannot be improved; raw observations can be reprocessed with better orbits, antenna models, and frames decades later → archive RINEX, raw IMU, and raw sonar/lidar ([Chapter 50](ch50-archiving-and-provenance.md)).

## Key takeaways

- Positioning accuracy has improved roughly tenfold per technological generation, from kilometres (hyperbolic radio) through metres (Transit, code GPS) to centimetres (RTK, PPP-AR) — and each step revealed a systematic error the previous generation could not see.
- Every DEM, chart, and bathymetric compilation inherits the positioning technology of its sources; know the vintage of what you merge and carry its horizontal uncertainty into your vertical budget.
- Internal precision (closure, repeatability) has never guaranteed absolute accuracy; only independent ties to the modern frame test the systematic component.
- Longitude is time: the chain from Harrison's chronometer to GNSS atomic clocks to the PPS line on your lidar is one problem, and timing failures remain the commonest field failure.
- Legacy positions outlive their technology; CATZOC, BAG uncertainty layers, and lineage metadata exist so that the mariner or modeller can tell a 1887 sextant fix from a 2023 RTK fix.
- Horizontal error becomes vertical error on slopes: $\sigma_z \approx s\,\sigma_{xy}$ is the first calculation to make when comparing surveys of different eras.
- The world's dependence on GNSS after switching off LORAN, Decca, and Omega is a historical fragility that the following chapters address; plan for denial.
- Archive raw observations: a 1995 RINEX file can be reprocessed to centimetres today; a 1995 coordinate list cannot.
