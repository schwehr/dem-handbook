### 32.5.1 Relativistic Clock Corrections in Satellite Positioning

A GNSS satellite clock is subject to continuous gravitational blueshift and special relativistic time dilation. While the constant component is canceled by the factory frequency preset ($10.22999999543\text{ MHz}$), periodic variations arise from orbital eccentricity.

#### 1. Exact Derivation of the Eccentricity Relativistic Correction ($\Delta t_{\text{rel}}$)
In an elliptical Keplerian orbit around Earth ($GM_\oplus = 3.986004418 \times 10^{14}\text{ m}^3\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$), satellite speed $v$ and geocentric radius $r$ oscillate between perigee and apogee according to the vis-viva and eccentric-anomaly relations:
$$r(E) = a (1 - e \cos E), \qquad v^2 = G M_\oplus \left( \frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a} \right)$$
where $a$ is the orbital semi-major axis, $e$ is eccentricity, and $E$ is the eccentric anomaly.

In an Earth-centered inertial metric (Ashby, 2003), the proper time $\tau$ ticked by the satellite's onboard atomic clock relative to geocentric coordinate time $t$ (referenced to the rotating geoid potential $\Phi_0 = -W_0 \approx -6.2637 \times 10^7\text{ m}^2\cdot\text{s}^{-2} < 0$, where $\Phi_0 / c_0^2 \approx -6.9693 \times 10^{-10}$ and orbital gravitational potential $\Phi(r) = -\frac{G M_\oplus}{r} < 0$) satisfies:
$$\frac{d\tau - dt}{dt} = \frac{\Phi(r) - \Phi_0}{c_0^2} - \frac{v^2}{2 c_0^2} = -\frac{G M_\oplus}{c_0^2 r} - \frac{G M_\oplus}{2 c_0^2}\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) - \frac{\Phi_0}{c_0^2}$$

Grouping the $1/r$ and $1/a$ terms separates the constant secular rate offset from the periodic orbital eccentricity term:
$$\frac{d\tau - dt}{dt} = \underbrace{\left( -\frac{3 G M_\oplus}{2 a c_0^2} - \frac{\Phi_0}{c_0^2} \right)}_{\text{Secular drift } (+4.4647\times 10^{-10}, \text{ factory-compensated})} + \underbrace{\frac{2 G M_\oplus}{c_0^2}\left( \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{r} \right)}_{\text{Periodic eccentricity perturbation}}$$

Differentiating Kepler's equation $M = E - e \sin E = \sqrt{G M_\oplus / a^3}\,(t - t_p)$ gives $dt = \frac{r}{\sqrt{G M_\oplus / a}}\,dE$. Integrating the periodic perturbation over $E$ yields the exact proper-time clock offset $\Delta t_{\text{rel}}$:
$$\Delta t_{\text{rel}} = \int \frac{2 G M_\oplus}{c_0^2}\left( \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{r} \right) \frac{r}{\sqrt{G M_\oplus / a}}\,dE = \frac{2\sqrt{G M_\oplus a}}{c_0^2} \int \left(\frac{r}{a} - 1\right) dE$$

Substituting $\frac{r}{a} - 1 = -e\cos E$ and integrating from perigee ($E = 0$):
$$\Delta t_{\text{rel}} = \frac{2\sqrt{G M_\oplus a}}{c_0^2} \int (-e\cos E)\,dE = -\frac{2 \sqrt{G M_\oplus a}}{c_0^2}\, e \sin E$$

Using the Keplerian radial-velocity identity $\mathbf{X}^s \cdot \mathbf{V}^s = r \dot{r} = \sqrt{G M_\oplus a}\,e\sin E$, this takes the universal coordinate-free vector form ([notation-and-symbols.md](../00-front-matter/notation-and-symbols.md), Section 4.3):
$$\Delta t_{\text{rel}} = -\frac{2 (\mathbf{X}^s \cdot \mathbf{V}^s)}{c_0^2}, \qquad \Delta\rho_{\text{rel}} = c_0\,\Delta t_{\text{rel}} = -\frac{2 (\mathbf{X}^s \cdot \mathbf{V}^s)}{c_0}$$
* **Magnitude:** For a GPS orbit ($a \approx 26,560\text{ km}$) with $e = 0.01\text{–}0.02$, $\Delta t_{\text{rel}}$ oscillates sinusoidally with an amplitude of **$\pm 23\text{ to }\pm 46\text{ nanoseconds}$** ($\Delta\rho_{\text{rel}} \approx \pm 6.8\text{ to }\pm 13.8\text{ meters}$), augmented at the millimeter-to-centimeter level by the general-relativistic **Shapiro signal propagation delay** $\Delta\rho_{\text{Shapiro}} = \frac{2 G M_\oplus}{c_0^2}\ln\left(\frac{r^s + r_r + \rho_r^s}{r^s + r_r - \rho_r^s}\right) \le 1.9\text{ cm}$.

#### 2. Derivation of the Sagnac Range Correction
Because GNSS coordinates are expressed in the rotating Earth-Centered, Earth-Fixed (ECEF) frame ($\boldsymbol{\omega}_\oplus = [0, 0, \omega_\oplus]^\top$, $\omega_\oplus = 7.292115 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$), Earth rotation during the $\tau \approx 67\text{–}86\text{ ms}$ signal flight time displaces the receiver in inertial space by $\boldsymbol{\omega}_\oplus \times \mathbf{X}_r\,\tau$. Evaluating the Lorentz-invariant light-cone condition in rotating ECEF coordinates yields the **Sagnac range correction** (`TOC.md` Section 32.5):
$$\Delta \rho_{\text{Sagnac}} = \frac{2\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_\oplus \cdot \mathbf{A}_{\text{eq}}}{c_0} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}_\oplus \cdot (\mathbf{X}^s \times \mathbf{X}_r)}{c_0} = \frac{\omega_\oplus}{c_0}\big( X^s Y_r - Y^s X_r \big)$$
where $\mathbf{A}_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}^s \times \mathbf{X}_r)$ is the directed area swept in the equatorial projection by the satellite-to-receiver vector, reaching **$\pm 20\text{ to }\pm 30\text{ meters}$** for low-elevation eastern and western satellites.

### 32.5.2 Atmospheric Group Refractivity Delay (Ciddor, Mendes–Pavlis, and Saastamoinen)

In laser altimetry and LiDAR ($532\text{ nm}$, $1064\text{ nm}$, or $1550\text{ nm}$), short optical pulses travel at the **group velocity** $v_g = c_0 / n_g$, whereas microwave GNSS signals ($1.2\text{–}1.6\text{ GHz}$) propagate through a non-dispersive neutral troposphere.

#### 1. Ciddor (1996) Optical Phase and Group Refractivity
Let $\sigma = 1/\lambda$ denote vacuum wavenumber in $\mu\text{m}^{-1}$. Under the IAG-standard **Ciddor (1996)** dispersion equation for dry standard air ($15^\circ\text{C}$, $1013.25\text{ hPa}$, $450\text{ ppm }\text{CO}_2$), phase refractivity $N_{\text{phase,std}}(\sigma) = (n_{\text{as}} - 1) \times 10^6$ (in parts per million) is:

$$N_{\text{phase,std}}(\sigma) = 10^{-2}\left( \frac{k_1}{k_0 - \sigma^2} + \frac{k_3}{k_2 - \sigma^2} \right)$$

with exact constants $k_0 = 238.0185\,\mu\text{m}^{-2}$, $k_1 = 5,792,105\,\mu\text{m}^{-2}$, $k_2 = 57.362\,\mu\text{m}^{-2}$, and $k_3 = 167,917\,\mu\text{m}^{-2}$ *(note the $10^{-2}$ conversion from Ciddor's $10^8$ scaling to parts-per-million $10^6$)*. Applying Rayleigh's group-dispersion operator $N_g(\sigma) = N_{\text{phase}}(\sigma) + \sigma \frac{d N_{\text{phase}}(\sigma)}{d\sigma}$ yields the exact **standard dry-air group refractivity**:

$$N_{g,\text{dry,std}}(\sigma) = 10^{-2}\left[ \frac{k_1(k_0 + \sigma^2)}{(k_0 - \sigma^2)^2} + \frac{k_3(k_2 + \sigma^2)}{(k_2 - \sigma^2)^2} \right]$$

Evaluating this formula reveals substantial chromatic dispersion across common LiDAR wavelengths: $N_{g,\text{dry,std}}(1064\text{ nm}) = \mathbf{276.73\text{ ppm}}$, whereas $N_{g,\text{dry,std}}(532\text{ nm}) = \mathbf{289.76\text{ ppm}}$ ($4.7\%$ higher!). Scaling to ambient dry-air density $\rho_d(P, T, e)$ and water-vapor density $\rho_w(T, e)$ via compressibility $Z(P, T, e)$ gives local group refractivity $N_g(z) = \frac{\rho_d(z)}{\rho_{d,\text{std}}}N_{g,\text{dry,std}}(\sigma) + \frac{\rho_w(z)}{\rho_{w,\text{std}}}N_{g,\text{ws}}(\sigma)$.

#### 2. Closed-Form Zenith and Slant Delay: Optical Laser vs. Microwave GNSS
Integrating $N_g(z)$ along the ray path from target orthometric height $z_0$ (pressure $P_0$, water-vapor partial pressure $e_0$, latitude $\varphi$) to sensor height $H$ (ambient pressure $P(H)$):

$$\Delta R_{\text{atm}} = 10^{-6} \int_{z_0}^H N_g(z) \sec\theta(z) \, dz$$

1. **Optical Spaceborne Laser Altimetry — Mendes & Pavlis (2004) (`FCULzd`):**
   Adopted by IERS Conventions (2010) and NASA ICESat-2 (`ATL03`; Neumann et al., 2019) for spaceborne ranging ($P(H) = 0$), the total zenith optical delay is $d_{\text{atm}}^z = d_h^z + d_{nh}^z$:
   $$d_h^z = 0.002416579\, f_h(\lambda)\, f_s(\varphi, z_0)^{-1}\, P_0, \qquad d_{nh}^z = 10^{-4}\big[5.316 f_{nh}(\lambda) - 3.759 f_h(\lambda)\big]\, f_s(\varphi, z_0)^{-1}\, e_0$$
   where gravity-variation factor $f_s(\varphi, z_0) = 1 - 0.00266\cos(2\varphi) - 2.8\times 10^{-7} z_0$ (with $z_0$ in $\text{m}$), $f_h(\lambda)$ is the normalized Ciddor hydrostatic dispersion factor ($f_h \approx 1.00004$ at $532\text{ nm}$; $f_h \approx 0.95512$ at $1064\text{ nm}$), and $f_{nh}(\lambda) \approx 0.99996$ at $532\text{ nm}$. Notice that at optical frequencies, the non-hydrostatic wet correction $d_{nh}^z$ is **two orders of magnitude smaller than in microwave GNSS** ($\approx +1.6\text{ mm}$ per $10\text{ hPa}$ of $e_0$ at $532\text{ nm}$).
2. **Airborne LiDAR Partial-Column Delay ($P(H) > 0$):**
   For an airborne scanner at altitude $H$ within the atmosphere, hydrostatic integration stops at aircraft static pressure $P(H)$, replacing $P_0$ with the differential column pressure drop $\Delta P = P_0 - P(H)$:
   $$\Delta R_{\text{airborne}}(\theta) \approx 0.0024166\, f_h(\lambda)\, f_s(\varphi, \bar{z})^{-1}\,\big(P_0 - P(H)\big)\,\sec\theta$$
3. **Microwave GNSS & Radar Altimetry — Saastamoinen (1972) / Davis et al. (1985):**
   For GNSS trajectories ($1.2\text{–}1.6\text{ GHz}$) and Ku/Ka-band radar altimeters, permanent $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ dipole rotation adds a large positive wet zenith delay ($\text{ZWD}_{\text{radio}} \approx 5\text{–}35\text{ cm}$) to the hydrostatic delay ($\text{ZHD}_{\text{radio}} \approx 2.31\text{ m}$ at sea level):
   $$T_r^s = m_h(\theta_{\text{el}})\frac{0.0022768\, P_0}{f_s(\varphi, z_0)} + m_w(\theta_{\text{el}})\cdot 0.002277\left(\frac{1255}{T_0} + 0.05\right)e_0$$

#### 3. Worked Numerical Verification Example: Spaceborne ICESat-2 vs. Airborne Topo LiDAR
Suppose a coastal wetland benchmark at $\varphi = 45^\circ\text{ N}$, $z_0 = 0\text{ m}$ ($f_s = 1.000$), $P_0 = 1013.25\text{ hPa}$, $T_0 = 293.15\text{ K}$, $e_0 = 20.0\text{ hPa}$ is surveyed simultaneously by:
* **ICESat-2 ATLAS ($532\text{ nm}$, spaceborne $H = 496\text{ km}$, $P(H) = 0$, off-nadir $\theta = 2^\circ$):**
  $d_h^z = 0.002416579 \times 1.00004 \times 1013.25 = 2.4487\text{ m}$, $d_{nh}^z \approx +0.0031\text{ m}$, and $\Delta R_{\text{ICESat-2}} = 2.4518 \times \sec(2^\circ) = \mathbf{2.453\text{ m}}$.
* **Airborne $1064\text{ nm}$ LiDAR ($H = 1,500\text{ m}$ AGL, aircraft static pressure $P(H) = 845.56\text{ hPa}$, scan edge $\theta = 25^\circ$):**
  Pressure column $\Delta P = 1013.25 - 845.56 = 167.69\text{ hPa}$ gives nadir delay $\Delta R_0 = 0.002416579 \times 0.95512 \times 167.69 = \mathbf{0.387\text{ m}}$, increasing at the $\theta = 25^\circ$ swath edge to $0.387 \times \sec(25^\circ) = \mathbf{0.427\text{ m}}$! Subtracting $\Delta R_{\text{atm}}$ from $R_{\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{2}c_0\Delta t_{\text{measured}}$ removes both the $38.7\text{ cm}$ vertical datum bias and the $4.0\text{ cm}$ cross-track "smile" curvature.

### 32.5.3 Separating Time Latency ($\delta t_{\text{lat}}$) from Pitch Boresight ($\delta\theta$) in Field Calibration

In both hydrographic multibeam patch tests (NOAA Field Procedures Manual) and airborne LiDAR strip adjustment (Glennie, 2007), an uncalibrated timestamp latency $\delta t_{\text{lat}}$ between the GNSS/INS navigation stream and the ranging sensor produces an along-track horizontal shift $\Delta x_{\text{along}} = v\,\delta t_{\text{lat}}$ that mimics a static pitch mounting misalignment $\delta\theta$ (which shifts the nadir footprint by $\Delta x_{\text{pitch}} \approx d\,\delta\theta$ in water depth $d$, or $H_{\text{agl}}\,\delta\theta$ in flight). On a seafloor or hillside of along-track slope $\tan\alpha$, running reciprocal lines ($+v$ vs. $-v$) reveals a combined apparent vertical discrepancy $\Delta z_{\text{recip}}$:

$$\Delta z_{\text{recip}}(v, d) = 2\tan\alpha \Big( v\,\delta t_{\text{lat}} + d\,\delta\theta \Big)$$

Because $v\,\delta t_{\text{lat}}$ scales linearly with vessel speed $v$ whereas $d\,\delta\theta$ scales linearly with depth $d$ (and is independent of speed!), surveyors decouple the $2\times 2$ observability rank deficiency by running two reciprocal passes over the same sloped feature at **fast speed $v_{\text{fast}}$** (e.g., $10\text{ knots}$) and **slow speed $v_{\text{slow}}$** (e.g., $4\text{ knots}$) at constant depth $d$:

$$\delta t_{\text{lat}} = \frac{\Delta z_{\text{recip}}(v_{\text{fast}}, d) - \Delta z_{\text{recip}}(v_{\text{slow}}, d)}{2\tan\alpha\,(v_{\text{fast}} - v_{\text{slow}})}$$

With IEEE 1588v2 PTP or hardware TTL event markers properly wired, $\delta t_{\text{lat}}$ solves identically to $0.000 \pm 0.001\text{ s}$; any non-zero estimate ($>5\text{ ms}$) immediately flags a broken PPS trigger, serial `$GPZDA` baud-rate lag, or software buffer bug!
