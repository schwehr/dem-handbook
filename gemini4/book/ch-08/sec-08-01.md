# Chapter 8: Photogrammetry, Stereo Cameras, and Structure from Motion (SfM)

*Reconstructing 3D surface geometry from overlapping 2D optical projections—from historical glass plates to multi-view satellite stereo and underwater photogrammetry.*

---

## 8.1 Classical Photogrammetry and Stereo Cameras

Photogrammetry is the science and mathematical art of recovering reliable three-dimensional (3D) metric coordinates of physical surfaces from overlapping two-dimensional (2D) passive optical images. Unlike active ranging systems such as LiDAR (Chapter 9), radar interferometry (Chapter 10), or multibeam echosounders (Chapter 11), which directly measure the round-trip time of flight or phase of an emitted electromagnetic or acoustic wave, passive optical cameras collapse the 3D scene along perspective rays onto a 2D detector array. Recovering the lost range dimension requires observing the same surface point from at least two spatially separated viewpoints—a geometric configuration known as *stereopsis*—and intersecting the conjugate optical rays in 3D space.

Over the past two centuries, photogrammetry has evolved from analog opto-mechanical stereoplotters aligning glass-plate negatives (anchored by historical milestones from Aimé Laussedat's terrestrial perspective experiments and Gaspard-Félix Tournachon "Nadar"'s 1858 balloon photography to the founding of the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing in 1934 and JPL's VICAR digital image processing system in 1966) to fully automated digital pipelines capable of reconstructing continental-scale Digital Surface Models (DSMs) from sub-meter satellite constellations and millimeter-scale benthic bathymetry from Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV) stereo rigs. Whether the target surface is an alpine glacier (where elevation $z$ is measured positive-up relative to an ellipsoid or geoid, in meters) or a coral reef mapped by a downward-looking stereo AUV (where depth $d = -z$ is measured positive-down relative to the sea surface or chart datum, in meters), the governing projective physics, lens aberration models, epipolar geometry, and disparity error propagation laws remain identical.

### 8.1.1 Camera Projection Geometry, Interior Orientation, and Lens Distortion

#### Central Perspective (Pinhole) Projection and Interior Orientation

The foundational mathematical abstraction of an optical frame camera is the *central perspective* (or *pinhole*) model. Consider a 3D point $\mathbf{X} = [X, Y, Z]^{\mathsf{T}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ expressed in a right-handed terrestrial or marine mapping frame $\mathcal{F}_m$ (e.g., Earth-Centered Earth-Fixed [ECEF] or a local East-North-Up [ENU] tangent frame, in meters). Let camera exposure station $j$ have its optical perspective center at $\mathbf{C}_j = [X_{0j}, Y_{0j}, Z_{0j}]^{\mathsf{T}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and its 3D orientation specified by a proper orthogonal rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_j \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ ($\mathbf{R}_j^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_j = \mathbf{I}_3$, $\det \mathbf{R}_j = +1$) that rotates vectors from the mapping frame $\mathcal{F}_m$ into the local camera coordinate frame $\mathcal{F}_{c,j}$.

Two coordinate conventions coexist in operational practice, and confusing them is a frequent source of sign errors when bridging photogrammetric and computer vision software:
1. **Classical Photogrammetric Convention (e.g., ASPRS, BAE SOCET GXP, Trimble Inpho):** The camera frame has $+X_c$ pointing right along detector rows (increasing column/sample index $u$), $+Y_c$ pointing up along detector columns (opposite to row/line index $v$), and $+Z_c$ pointing *away* from the scene (upward toward the aircraft/satellite zenith), so that the optical axis looks along $-Z_c$ and points in front of the lens satisfy $Z_c < 0$.
2. **Computer Vision Convention (e.g., OpenCV, COLMAP, Hartley & Zisserman, 2004):** The camera frame has $+X_c$ pointing right along detector rows (increasing column/sample index $u$), $+Y_c$ pointing down along detector columns (increasing row/line index $v$), and $+Z_c$ pointing *outward* along the optical axis into the scene, so that visible points satisfy $Z_c > 0$.

Adopting the computer vision forward-projection convention first to establish the matrix-projective form, a 3D point $\mathbf{X}$ transforms into the camera frame $\mathcal{F}_{c,j}$ as:

$$\mathbf{X}_{c,j} = \begin{bmatrix} X_c \\ Y_c \\ Z_c \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{R}_j \left(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{C}_j\right) = \mathbf{R}_j \mathbf{X} + \mathbf{t}_j \quad (\text{m}) \tag{8.1}$$

where $\mathbf{t}_j = -\mathbf{R}_j \mathbf{C}_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the translation vector of the mapping origin expressed in the camera frame. Perspective division projects $\mathbf{X}_{c,j}$ onto the normalized (dimensionless) undistorted image plane at $Z_c = 1$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{x} \\ \tilde{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} X_c / Z_c \\ Y_c / Z_c \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{dimensionless rad/rad}) \tag{8.2}$$

In physical focal-plane coordinates $(x, y)$ (measured in millimeters from the fiducial or detector-array origin), $\tilde{x} = (x - x_0)/f$ and $\tilde{y} = (y - y_0)/f$, where $f$ is the calibrated focal length (mm) and $(x_0, y_0)$ is the principal point offset (mm). For an ideal, distortion-free digital detector array with horizontal and vertical pixel pitches $p_x, p_y$ ($\mu\text{m}$ or $\text{mm}\cdot\text{px}^{-1}$), the mapping from normalized image coordinates $[\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}, 1]^{\mathsf{T}}$ to homogeneous pixel coordinates $\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = [u, v, 1]^{\mathsf{T}}$ (where $u$ is the column/sample index and $v$ is the row/line index, in pixels) is governed by the upper-triangular *intrinsic calibration matrix* $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{K} \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{x} \\ \tilde{y} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{x} \\ \tilde{y} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \tag{8.3}$$

The five *interior orientation* (IO) parameters in $\mathbf{K}$ have direct physical meanings:
* $f_x = f / p_x$ and $f_y = f / p_y$ are the effective focal lengths expressed in horizontal and vertical pixel units ($\text{px}$). For modern Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) and Charge-Coupled Device (CCD, invented at Bell Labs by Boyle and Smith in 1969) sensors with square detectors ($p_x = p_y$), $f_x = f_y$.
* $(c_x, c_y)$ is the *principal point* in pixel coordinates ($\text{px}$)—physically defined as the foot of the perpendicular dropped from the interior perspective center onto the detector plane (the *principal point of autocollimation*) or, in calibration practice, the center of symmetry of radial lens distortion (the *principal point of best symmetry*).
* $s = -f_x \cot\theta_{\text{axes}}$ is the *pixel skew* parameter ($\text{px}$), quantifying the deviation of the detector row and column axes from strict orthogonality ($\theta_{\text{axes}} = 90^\circ \implies s = 0$). In modern photolithographic solid-state arrays, $s$ is zero to within $10^{-5}\text{ px}$, though it remains nonzero in scanned historical film photography when the scanner stage rails are slightly non-orthogonal.

#### The Brown–Conrady Lens Distortion Model

Real multi-element compound lenses cannot simultaneously achieve a wide field of view, a large aperture (low $f$-number), and strictly rectilinear pinhole projection across the entire focal plane. As chief rays pass through curved refractive lens surfaces and aperture stops, they experience spatially varying angular deviations known as *monochromatic geometrical aberrations*. Following the seminal formulations of Conrady (1919) and Duane Brown (1966, 1971), photogrammetric interior orientation models decompose this displacement into two physical components evaluated at the normalized radial distance $r = \sqrt{\tilde{x}^2 + \tilde{y}^2}$ from the principal point:

1. **Symmetric Radial Distortion ($\Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{rad}}, \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{rad}}$):** Caused by axial variations in refractive magnification across spherical or aspherical lens elements. Because a centered optical system is rotationally symmetric about the optical axis, the radial displacement $\Delta r(r)$ is an odd function of $r$, yielding even powers of $r$ when multiplied by $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$:
   $$\begin{aligned}
   \Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{rad}} &= \tilde{x}\left(k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6\right) \\
   \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{rad}} &= \tilde{y}\left(k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6\right)
   \end{aligned} \tag{8.4}$$
   where $k_1, k_2, k_3$ are dimensionless radial distortion coefficients (when applied to normalized coordinates $\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}$; note that in traditional metric camera calibration certificates where $r$ is measured in $\text{mm}$, $k_n$ carries units of $\text{mm}^{-2n}$). When $k_1 < 0$, magnification decreases with off-axis angle, pulling outer image corners inward (*barrel distortion*, typical of wide-angle consumer and UAV lenses); when $k_1 > 0$, magnification increases outward (*pincushion distortion*, typical of telephoto lenses).
2. **Decentering (Tangential and Asymmetric Radial) Distortion ($\Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{tan}}, \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{tan}}$):** During mechanical lens assembly, the optical centers of individual glass elements are never perfectly collinear. Conrady (1919) and Brown (1966) demonstrated that small decentering tilts and lateral shifts of lens elements behave to first order like a thin prism superimposed on the lens, introducing both radial and tangential displacements parameterized by two dimensionless coefficients $(p_1, p_2)$ (written here in the OpenCV/COLMAP ordering, where $p_1$ and $p_2$ swap indices relative to Brown's original $P_1, P_2$, i.e., $P_1^{\text{Brown}} = p_2$ and $P_2^{\text{Brown}} = p_1$):
   $$\begin{aligned}
   \Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{tan}} &= 2 p_1 \tilde{x}\tilde{y} + p_2\left(r^2 + 2\tilde{x}^2\right) \\
   \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{tan}} &= p_1\left(r^2 + 2\tilde{y}^2\right) + 2 p_2 \tilde{x}\tilde{y}
   \end{aligned} \tag{8.5}$$

Combining Equations (8.3)–(8.5), the distorted normalized coordinates $(\tilde{x}_d, \tilde{y}_d)$ and final observed pixel coordinates $(u, v)$ are given by:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \tilde{x}_d \\ \tilde{y}_d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{x} + \Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{rad}} + \Delta\tilde{x}_{\text{tan}} \\ \tilde{y} + \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{rad}} + \Delta\tilde{y}_{\text{tan}} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x \tilde{x}_d + s \tilde{y}_d + c_x \\ f_y \tilde{y}_d + c_y \end{bmatrix} \tag{8.6}$$

Notice that the Brown–Conrady model (Equation 8.6) is formulated in the *forward* direction (mapping an ideal 3D ray $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$ to distorted pixel coordinates $(u, v)$). When *undistorting* an observed pixel $(u, v)$ back to an ideal ray $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$ for triangulation or epipolar rectification, one first inverts $\mathbf{K}$ to obtain $(\tilde{x}_d, \tilde{y}_d)$ and then solves the non-linear system $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_d = \mathbf{g}_{\text{dist}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ via fixed-point iteration or 3–5 steps of $2 \times 2$ Newton–Raphson iteration initialized at $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)} = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_d$.

#### Exterior Orientation and the Classical Collinearity Equations

In the classical photogrammetric convention (where the camera looks along $-Z_c$, with rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_j = [r_{mn}]_{3\times 3} = \mathbf{R}_\kappa \mathbf{R}_\phi \mathbf{R}_\omega$ parameterized by roll $\omega$, pitch $\phi$, and yaw/crab $\kappa$), a 3D object point $\mathbf{X}_i = [X_i, Y_i, Z_i]^{\mathsf{T}}$, the camera perspective center $\mathbf{C}_j = [X_{0j}, Y_{0j}, Z_{0j}]^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the undistorted image point $[x_{ij} - x_0 - \Delta x_{\text{dist}}, \, y_{ij} - y_0 - \Delta y_{\text{dist}}, \, -f]^{\mathsf{T}}$ (where subtracting the forward distortion $\Delta x_{\text{dist}}, \Delta y_{\text{dist}}$ defined in Equations 8.4–8.6 restores the ideal rectilinear projection; note that classical calibration certificates sometimes tabulate additive corrections $\Delta x_{\text{corr}} = -\Delta x_{\text{dist}}$) lie along a single straight line in 3D space. Enforcing this *collinearity condition* yields the classical **Collinearity Equations**:

$$\begin{aligned}
x_{ij} - x_0 - \Delta x_{\text{dist}}(x_{ij}, y_{ij}) &= -f \, \frac{r_{11}(X_i - X_{0j}) + r_{12}(Y_i - Y_{0j}) + r_{13}(Z_i - Z_{0j})}{r_{31}(X_i - X_{0j}) + r_{32}(Y_i - Y_{0j}) + r_{33}(Z_i - Z_{0j})} \\[6pt]
y_{ij} - y_0 - \Delta y_{\text{dist}}(x_{ij}, y_{ij}) &= -f \, \frac{r_{21}(X_i - X_{0j}) + r_{22}(Y_i - Y_{0j}) + r_{23}(Z_i - Z_{0j})}{r_{31}(X_i - X_{0j}) + r_{32}(Y_i - Y_{0j}) + r_{33}(Z_i - Z_{0j})}
\end{aligned} \tag{8.7}$$

Equation (8.7) is the workhorse of analytical and digital photogrammetry:
* **Space Resection / Bundle Adjustment (Section 8.2 & 8.4):** Given image observations $(x_{ij}, y_{ij})$ of tie points and Ground Control Points (GCPs), solve for the 6-DOF *exterior orientation* $(\mathbf{C}_j, \mathbf{R}_j)$ of each camera and, optionally, the interior orientation parameters $(f, x_0, y_0, k_1, k_2, k_3, p_1, p_2)$ via non-linear least squares.
* **Space Intersection (Triangulation):** Given calibrated interior and exterior orientations for $M \ge 2$ overlapping cameras and conjugate image coordinates $(x_{ij}, y_{ij})$ for $j = 1, \dots, M$, Equation (8.7) provides $2M$ equations in the 3 unknown coordinates $(X_i, Y_i, Z_i)$ of the surface point, yielding an overdetermined system ($2M \ge 4 > 3$) whose least-squares residual directly measures 3D ray-intersection consistency.

### 8.1.2 Frame Cameras vs. Linear Pushbroom Scanners and Rational Polynomial Coefficients (RPCs)

#### Metric Frame Cameras vs. Linear Pushbroom Scanners

Optical imaging sensors used for topographic and bathymetric DEM generation fall into two fundamentally distinct geometric architectures:

1. **2D Frame Cameras (Central Perspective Across Both Axes):** A 2D detector array (or historical film frame) is exposed simultaneously across all rows and columns at a single instant $t_j$ using a mechanical leaf shutter or a global electronic CMOS shutter. Consequently, every pixel $(u, v)$ in image $j$ shares a single, rigid 6-DOF exterior orientation $(\mathbf{C}(t_j), \mathbf{R}(t_j))$. Large-format airborne metric cameras (e.g., Vexcel UltraCam Eagle/Osprey, Leica DMC-III) and benthic AUV stereo cameras use rigidly calibrated multi-head or monolithic global-shutter frame arrays to preserve strict rigid perspective geometry. (By contrast, low-cost consumer UAV cameras often use *electronic rolling shutters*, exposing each row sequentially over $15\text{–}30\text{ ms}$, which introduces velocity- and vibration-induced shear distortion unless explicitly modeled.)
2. **1D Linear Pushbroom Scanners (Perspective Across-Track, Orthographic/Kinematic Along-Track):** Building a monolithic 2D focal plane with $30{,}000 \times 30{,}000$ pixels and a diffraction-limited meter-class telescope for a Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite is prohibitively massive and expensive. Instead, virtually all high-resolution optical Earth observation satellites—as well as airborne three-line scanners such as the Leica ADS40/80/100—employ **linear pushbroom arrays** (frequently utilizing Time Delay and Integration [TDI] charge-coupled stages to boost signal-to-noise ratio at short line-dwell times). A 1D linear array of $N_s \sim 10{,}000\text{–}50{,}000$ detectors is oriented perpendicular to the flight/orbit direction (across-track), while the forward orbital or aircraft motion sweeps the field of view along-track.

```
   (a) 2D Metric Frame Camera                (b) 1D Linear Pushbroom Scanner
   ==========================                ===============================
         Single Epoch t_j                      Continuous Line Epochs t(l)
              [C_j, R_j]                       [C(t_1),R(t_1)] ... [C(t_L),R(t_L)]
                  /\                                 /\       /\       /\
                 /  \                               /  \     /  \     /  \
                /    \                             /    \   /    \   /    \
         +------------------+                     +------+ +------+ +------+
         |  All rows (u,v)  |                     |Line 1| |Line l| |Line L|
         |  share 1 pose    |                     +------+ +------+ +------+
         +------------------+                        ---- Flight / Orbit --->
```

Because each image row (scanline $l \in [0, L_{\max}]$) of a pushbroom image is acquired at a distinct epoch

$$t(l) = t_0 + l \cdot \Delta t_{\text{line}} \tag{8.8}$$

(where $\Delta t_{\text{line}} = \mathrm{GSD}_{\text{along}} / v_g \approx 70\text{–}300\,\mu\text{s}$ for sub-meter LEO satellites moving at ground velocity $v_g \approx 7{,}000\,\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$), **a pushbroom image does not have a single perspective center**. Instead, each scanline $l$ is a 1D central-perspective projection along the across-track detector coordinate, governed by a continuous, time-varying trajectory and attitude function $(\mathbf{C}(t(l)), \mathbf{R}(t(l)))$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_c(s) \\ y_c(s) \\ -f \end{bmatrix} = \lambda_{i,l} \, \mathbf{R}_{\text{body}\to\text{cam}} \, \mathbf{R}_{\text{ECEF}\to\text{body}}\!\left(t(l)\right) \left( \mathbf{X}_i - \mathbf{C}_{\text{ECEF}}\!\left(t(l)\right) \right) \tag{8.9}$$

where $\lambda_{i,l} > 0$ is the perspective scale factor for object point $i$ at scanline $l$, $\mathbf{R}_{\text{body}\to\text{cam}} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the calibrated mounting (boresight) matrix between the spacecraft/aircraft attitude reference frame and the camera optical frame, and $(x_c(s), y_c(s))$ are the calibrated focal-plane coordinates (mm) of detector sample $s$ along the linear array (with $y_c(s) \approx 0$ for an ideal straight array oriented along $+X_c$, plus small non-zero along-track offsets accounting for TDI sub-array staggering and optical distortion).

To acquire stereo imagery within a single orbital pass (eliminating multi-date cloud cover, seasonal snow melt, and solar illumination changes between passes), pushbroom satellites employ two designs:
* **Three-Line Scanners (Simultaneous Multi-Angle Arrays):** Three separate linear arrays (or three telescopes) mounted on the same optical bench view Forward ($+20^\circ\text{ to }+27^\circ$), Nadir ($0^\circ$), and Backward ($-20^\circ\text{ to }-27^\circ$) simultaneously as the platform advances. Examples include the Mars Express HRSC, Japan's ALOS PRISM ($2.5\text{ m}$ GSD, $\pm 23.8^\circ$ forward/backward), Cartosat-1, ZY-3, and the airborne Leica ADS100 (which places forward, nadir, and backward panchromatic/multispectral lines on a single focal plane).
* **Agile Single-Line Body-Pointing Scanners:** A single ultra-high-resolution telescope ($0.30\text{–}0.50\text{ m}$ GSD) slews its entire spacecraft bus rapidly along-track using high-torque Control Moment Gyroscopes (CMGs) or reaction wheels to scan the target area forward, pitches back through nadir, and scans it again backward (bi-stereo or tri-stereo) within $30\text{–}90\text{ s}$. Examples include Maxar WorldView-1/2/3/Legion and Airbus Pléiades-1A/1B and Pléiades Neo.

> [!WARNING]
> **Spacecraft Attitude Jitter and Cross-Track DEM Ripples:** Because pushbroom collinearity (Equation 8.9) depends directly on the instantaneous attitude $\mathbf{R}(t(l))$ at every scanline, high-frequency mechanical micro-vibrations ($1\text{–}50\text{ Hz}$) excited by spinning reaction wheels, CMGs, cryocoolers, or solar-array stepping motors directly perturb the line-of-sight pointing vector. While spacecraft star trackers and gyroscopes sample attitude at $10\text{–}100\text{ Hz}$, vibrations near or above the attitude telemetry Nyquist frequency—or regularized out by coarse polynomial/spline attitude fitting—remain uncompensated in the camera model. At an orbital altitude $H = 500\text{ km}$, an unmodeled pitch/roll jitter amplitude of just $\delta\theta = 0.5\,\mu\text{rad}$ ($0.10\text{ arcsec}$) displaces the ground intersection by $H\,\delta\theta = 0.25\text{ m}$ ($0.5\text{ px}$ at $0.5\text{ m}$ GSD). Through the stereo base-to-height amplification factor $H/B$, this creates periodic **cross-track washboard ripples (undulations)** of $0.3\text{–}1.5\text{ m}$ amplitude across the DEM (Ayoub et al., 2008; Girod et al., 2017; Beyer et al., 2018). Detecting and removing attitude jitter requires either high-rate focal-plane parallax analysis across staggered CCD sub-arrays or solver-level jitter modeling (`jitter_solve` in NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline).

#### Rational Polynomial Coefficients (RPCs / RFM, NITF RPC00B)

Evaluating the physical pushbroom sensor model (Equation 8.9) requires access to time-tagged spacecraft ephemeris polynomials $\mathbf{C}(t)$, attitude quaternions $\mathbf{q}(t)$, TDI line-rate clocks, velocity aberration corrections, atmospheric refraction tables, and confidential optical look-angle polynomials. Commercial and defense satellite operators rarely release raw physical telemetry (to protect proprietary and national-security spacecraft characteristics), and maintaining dozens of bespoke physical sensor models across photogrammetric software packages is brittle.

Instead, the geospatial community standardized on the **Rational Function Model (RFM)**, specified by the US National Imagery Transmission Format **NITF `RPC00B`** standard (and distributed in GeoTIFF `_RPC.TXT` or `.RPB` metadata sidecars). An RFM approximates the exact physical ground-to-image projection over the volume bounding a satellite scene using ratios of 20-term cubic polynomials—known as **Rational Polynomial Coefficients (RPCs)**—fitted by the vendor on a dense 3D virtual grid generated from the rigorous physical sensor model (to within $< 0.01\text{ px}$ approximation error).

First, geodetic coordinates—latitude $\phi$ (decimal degrees, WGS84), longitude $\lambda$ (decimal degrees, WGS84), and ellipsoidal height $h$ (meters above the WGS84 reference ellipsoid)—are shifted and scaled into dimensionless normalized coordinates $(U, V, W) \in [-1, +1]^3$ to prevent floating-point ill-conditioning when evaluating cubic powers:

$$U = \frac{\phi - \phi_{\text{off}}}{\phi_{\text{scale}}}, \qquad V = \frac{\lambda - \lambda_{\text{off}}}{\lambda_{\text{scale}}}, \qquad W = \frac{h - h_{\text{off}}}{h_{\text{scale}}} \tag{8.10}$$

Similarly, the image coordinates—Line $L$ (row index, pixels) and Sample $S$ (column index, pixels)—are related to normalized image coordinates $(L_n, S_n) \in [-1, +1]^2$ via:

$$L_n = \frac{L - L_{\text{off}}}{L_{\text{scale}}}, \qquad S_n = \frac{S - S_{\text{off}}}{S_{\text{scale}}} \tag{8.11}$$

The RPC forward projection maps $(U, V, W) \mapsto (L_n, S_n)$ as the ratio of four cubic polynomials:

$$L_n = \frac{P_1(U, V, W)}{P_2(U, V, W)}, \qquad S_n = \frac{P_3(U, V, W)}{P_4(U, V, W)} \tag{8.12}$$

In the `RPC00B` ordering convention (which superseded the legacy `RPC00A` tag ordering by placing normalized longitude $V$ before normalized latitude $U$ in the linear terms $c_2^{(k)} V + c_3^{(k)} U$ and reordering the quadratic/cubic cross-terms), each polynomial $P_k(U, V, W)$ ($k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$) contains 20 monomials of total degree $i + j + m \le 3$:

$$\begin{aligned}
P_k(U, V, W) =\; & c_1^{(k)} + c_2^{(k)} V + c_3^{(k)} U + c_4^{(k)} W + c_5^{(k)} V U + c_6^{(k)} V W + c_7^{(k)} U W + c_8^{(k)} V^2 + c_9^{(k)} U^2 + c_{10}^{(k)} W^2 \\
&+ c_{11}^{(k)} U V W + c_{12}^{(k)} V^3 + c_{13}^{(k)} V U^2 + c_{14}^{(k)} V W^2 + c_{15}^{(k)} V^2 U + c_{16}^{(k)} U^3 + c_{17}^{(k)} U W^2 \\
&+ c_{18}^{(k)} V^2 W + c_{19}^{(k)} U^2 W + c_{20}^{(k)} W^3
\end{aligned} \tag{8.13}$$

where $c_1^{(2)} = c_1^{(4)} = 1.0$ are fixed to remove projective scale ambiguity, leaving $78$ independent polynomial coefficients plus $10$ normalization offset/scale constants. Physically, the numerators $P_1, P_3$ capture the primary optical projection, platform motion, and Earth curvature, while the denominators $P_2, P_4$ capture perspective foreshortening along the optical ray (analogous to the $Z_c$ denominator in Equation 8.7).

**Bias-Compensated RPC Bundle Adjustment (Grodecki & Dial, 2003):** Although the internal relative geometry of an RPC model is accurate to sub-pixel precision, absolute spacecraft star-tracker biases ($1\text{–}5\text{ arcsec}$) and ephemeris errors translate the raw RPC ground footprint by $1\text{–}10\text{ m}$. Because the field of view of a satellite telescope is very narrow ($< 1.5^\circ$), these physical orientation biases project almost purely as a 2D translation—or, with small drift rates over a $15\text{ km}$ strip, a 6-parameter 2D affine transformation—directly in image space $(L, S)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{obs}} &= L_{\text{off}} + L_{\text{scale}} \frac{P_1(U, V, W)}{P_2(U, V, W)} + \Delta L_0 + a_L L + a_S S \\[4pt]
S_{\text{obs}} &= S_{\text{off}} + S_{\text{scale}} \frac{P_3(U, V, W)}{P_4(U, V, W)} + \Delta S_0 + b_L L + b_S S
\end{aligned} \tag{8.14}$$

Estimating just the two shift parameters $(\Delta L_0, \Delta S_0)$ per image using tie points or a single accurate GCP (or co-registering the output DSM to ICESat-2 laser altimetry, Chapter 12) removes meter-scale horizontal and vertical datum offsets without ever needing to touch the 80 internal RPC coefficients.

### 8.1.3 Base-to-Height Ratio ($B/H$) Trade-Offs, Epipolar Rectification, and Dense Stereo Matching

#### Vertical Precision Derivation and the Fundamental $B/H$ Trade-Off

To understand how image-space disparity errors propagate into vertical DEM uncertainty $\sigma_z$, consider the *normal case* of stereo photogrammetry: two identical downward-looking (nadir) cameras with calibrated focal length $f$ and pixel pitch $p_{\text{px}}$ separated by a horizontal baseline $B$ (m) at flying/orbital altitude $H$ (m) above a reference datum ($z = 0$). A ground point at elevation $z$ (so that its vertical distance below the camera baseline is $h_c = H - z$) projects to image coordinates $x_1 = f \frac{X}{H - z}$ in the left camera (at $X_{01} = 0$) and $x_2 = f \frac{X - B}{H - z}$ in the right camera (at $X_{02} = B$).

The *x-parallax* (or metric disparity) $p$ is the difference between the two image coordinates:

$$p = x_1 - x_2 = \frac{f B}{H - z} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad H - z = \frac{f B}{p} \tag{8.15}$$

Differentiating Equation (8.15) with respect to $z$ around $H - z \approx H$ gives the sensitivity of parallax to elevation:

$$\frac{dp}{dz} = \frac{f B}{(H - z)^2} \approx \frac{f B}{H^2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |dz| = \frac{H}{B} \cdot \frac{H}{f} \, |dp| \tag{8.16}$$

Applying linear variance propagation ($\sigma_z^2 = (\partial z / \partial p)^2 \sigma_p^2$) and substituting the Ground Sample Distance $\mathrm{GSD} = \frac{H}{f} p_{\text{px}}$ (m/px) alongside the dimensionless pixel-level disparity precision $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} = \sigma_p / p_{\text{px}}$ (px) yields the **fundamental photogrammetric vertical precision equation**:

$$\sigma_z = \frac{H}{B} \cdot \frac{H}{f} \, \sigma_p = \left(\frac{B}{H}\right)^{-1} \cdot \mathrm{GSD} \cdot \sigma_{p,\text{px}} \quad (\text{m}) \tag{8.17}$$

Equation (8.17) reveals a profound geometric tension governed by the **base-to-height ratio ($B/H$)**, which is directly related to the stereo convergence angle $\theta_{\text{conv}} = 2 \arctan\!\left(\frac{B}{2H}\right)$:
* **Geometric Leverage ($B/H$ in the denominator):** For a fixed sub-pixel matching precision $\sigma_{p,\text{px}}$, widening the baseline $B/H$ increases the ray intersection angle $\theta_{\text{conv}}$, shrinking the vertical uncertainty ellipse and reducing $\sigma_z$ proportionally to $(B/H)^{-1}$.
* **Radiometric Appearance and Occlusion Degradation ($\sigma_{p,\text{px}}$ in the numerator):** Equation (8.17) assumes $\sigma_{p,\text{px}}$ is constant and independent of $B/H$. In reality, as $B/H$ increases beyond $0.4\text{–}0.6$, three physical effects degrade or destroy stereo matching:
  1. **Geometric Occlusions (Dead Zones):** Consider an urban street canyon, alpine gorge, or submarine reef trench of width $w_s$ bounded by vertical walls of height $h_b$. For a symmetric stereo pair with base-to-height ratio $B/H$, each camera looks inward at an off-nadir incidence angle $\alpha = \arctan(B / (2H))$. The left camera is occluded over a horizontal shadow zone of width $w_{\text{occ},1} = h_b \tan\alpha = h_b \frac{B}{2H}$ adjacent to the left wall, and the right camera is occluded over $w_{\text{occ},2} = h_b \frac{B}{2H}$ adjacent to the right wall. Subtracting both monocular shadow zones from $w_s$ leaves a *mutually visible stereo width* of $w_{\text{stereo}} = \max\!\left(0, \, w_s - h_b \frac{B}{H}\right)$, meaning the fraction of the canyon floor observable in stereo is $f_{\text{stereo}} = \max\!\left(0, \, 1 - \frac{h_b}{w_s}\frac{B}{H}\right)$. The entire canyon floor becomes *100% mutually unobservable* in stereo ($w_{\text{stereo}} = 0$) whenever the canyon height-to-width aspect ratio exceeds:
     $$\frac{h_b}{w_s} \ge \left(\frac{B}{H}\right)^{-1} \tag{8.18}$$
  2. **Perspective Foreshortening:** A terrain slope of inclination $\beta$ facing away from one camera and toward the other is compressed on one focal plane by $\cos(\alpha + \beta)$ and stretched on the other by $\cos(\alpha - \beta)$, violating the local scale-invariance assumptions of correlation windows.
  3. **Non-Lambertian BRDF Differences:** Specular glints on water or snow, tree canopy bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) hotspots, and deep shadows shift rapidly across wide convergence angles, causing $\sigma_{p,\text{px}}$ to explode or creating catastrophic blunders.

#### Worked Numerical Example: Wide-Baseline vs. Narrow-Baseline Satellite and Benthic AUV Stereo

1. **Orbital Pushbroom Stereo ($H = 500\text{ km}$, $\mathrm{GSD} = 0.50\text{ m}$):** Compare two acquisition geometries over (A) a flat, well-textured alluvial fan and (B) a steep alpine canyon or urban street with aspect ratio $h_b / w_s = 2.0$ (e.g., $h_b = 30\text{ m}$ tall buildings bordering a $w_s = 15\text{ m}$ wide street):
   * **Case 1: Wide Baseline ($B = 400\text{ km} \implies B/H = 0.80$, $\theta_{\text{conv}} = 43.6^\circ$).** Over the open alluvial fan, dense matching achieves $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} = 0.15\text{ px}$. By Equation (8.17), the theoretical vertical random error is:
     $$\sigma_z = \frac{1}{0.80} \times 0.50\,\text{m} \times 0.15 = 0.094\,\text{m}$$
     However, in the urban canyon ($h_b / w_s = 2.0 > (0.80)^{-1} = 1.25$), each wall casts a monocular occlusion shadow of $h_b \frac{B}{2H} = 30\text{ m} \times 0.40 = 12\text{ m}$ across the $15\text{ m}$ street ($w_{\text{stereo}} = \max(0, 15 - 24) = 0\text{ m}$). By Equation (8.18), the street floor is **100% occluded** in stereo—matching completely fails!
   * **Case 2: Narrow Baseline ($B = 100\text{ km} \implies B/H = 0.20$, $\theta_{\text{conv}} = 11.4^\circ$).** Because the two views are nearly identical in perspective and illumination, correlation improves slightly to $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} = 0.12\text{ px}$. On open terrain, reduced geometric leverage yields a larger vertical random error:
     $$\sigma_z = \frac{1}{0.20} \times 0.50\,\text{m} \times 0.12 = 0.300\,\text{m}$$
     Yet in the urban/alpine canyon, the occlusion threshold is $(B/H)^{-1} = 5.0 > 2.0$: the cameras look down at only $\alpha = 5.7^\circ$ off-nadir, so each wall occludes only $h_b \frac{B}{2H} = 30\text{ m} \times 0.10 = 3.0\text{ m}$ ($20\%$) in a single view. While $80\%$ of the floor is visible to each individual camera, the central strip *mutually visible in both stereo images* has width $w_{\text{stereo}} = 15\text{ m} - 2(3.0\text{ m}) = 9.0\text{ m}$ ($f_{\text{stereo}} = 1 - 2.0 \times 0.20 = 60\%$), producing a continuous, void-free street-level DEM!

2. **Benthic AUV Stereo Photogrammetry ($H = 2.5\text{ m}$ altitude above a coral reef, $B = 0.80\text{ m} \implies B/H = 0.32$, $\mathrm{GSD} = 0.60\text{ mm}$):** With dome-port refractive calibration (Section 8.2.3) and $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} = 0.15\text{ px}$, Equation (8.17) yields a relative vertical depth precision of $\sigma_d = (0.32)^{-1} \times 0.60\text{ mm} \times 0.15 = 0.28\text{ mm}$. When integrated with the AUV's INS/DVL/Paroscientific pressure-depth vertical datum uncertainty ($\sigma_{d,\text{nav}} \approx 0.05\text{ m}$ at $d = 20\text{ m}$ depth), the Total Vertical Uncertainty at 95% confidence is $\mathrm{TVU}_{95\%} = 1.96 \sqrt{\sigma_d^2 + \sigma_{d,\text{nav}}^2} \approx 0.098\text{ m}$, easily satisfying **IHO S-44 (Edition 6.1.0, 2022) Exclusive Order** ($\mathrm{TVU}_{\max}(d) = \sqrt{a^2 + (b \cdot d)^2} = \sqrt{0.15^2 + (0.0075 \times 20)^2} = 0.212\text{ m}$) and **NOAA HSSD** shallow-water object-detection specifications. On land, under the **ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data (2014; Edition 2, 2023)**, an airborne stereo survey with $\mathrm{GSD} = 0.10\text{ m}$, $B/H = 0.30$, $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} = 0.15\text{ px}$ ($\sigma_{z,\text{match}} = 0.05\text{ m}$), and bundle-adjusted GNSS/INS trajectory error $\sigma_{z,\text{pose}} = 0.03\text{ m}$ achieves $\mathrm{RMSE}_z = \sqrt{0.05^2 + 0.03^2} = 0.058\text{ m}$ and a **Non-Vegetated Vertical Accuracy** of $\mathrm{NVA}_{95\%} = 1.960 \times \mathrm{RMSE}_z = 0.114\text{ m}$ (meeting the ASPRS $10\text{ cm}$ Vertical Accuracy Class and USGS 3DEP QL1/QL2 bare-earth targets where vegetation permits). Because stereo matching blunders in shadows or steep canyons produce heavy non-Gaussian tails, practitioners should always report both parametric $\mathrm{RMSE}_z$ and the robust **Normalized Median Absolute Deviation** ($\mathrm{NMAD} = 1.4826 \times \mathrm{median}(|\Delta z - \mathrm{median}(\Delta z)|)$, Chapter 6).

#### Epipolar Geometry and Quasi-Epipolar Rectification

Searching for the conjugate match of pixel $\mathbf{u}_1 = [u_1, v_1, 1]^{\mathsf{T}}$ across the entire 2D domain of image 2 would require $\mathcal{O}(W^2)$ evaluations per pixel and suffer severe ambiguity. **Epipolar geometry** reduces this 2D search to a 1D search along a single line.

For two pinhole frame cameras with calibrated intrinsics $\mathbf{K}_1, \mathbf{K}_2$ and relative pose $(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t})$ mapping camera 1 to camera 2, the 3D optical ray from $\mathbf{C}_1$ through $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1 = \mathbf{K}_1^{-1}\mathbf{u}_1$, the baseline vector $\mathbf{t}$, and the conjugate ray through $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_2 = \mathbf{K}_2^{-1}\mathbf{u}_2$ must be coplanar. This coplanarity condition $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_2^{\mathsf{T}} (\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1) = 0$ defines the **Essential Matrix** $\mathbf{E} = [\mathbf{t}]_{\times}\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ (where $[\mathbf{t}]_{\times} = -[\mathbf{t}]_{\times}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix of $\mathbf{t}$) in normalized coordinates, and the **Fundamental Matrix** $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{K}_2^{-\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{E} \mathbf{K}_1^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ (of rank 2, with 7 degrees of freedom) in pixel coordinates:

$$\mathbf{u}_2^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{F} \mathbf{u}_1 = 0 \tag{8.19}$$

For any pixel $\mathbf{u}_1$ in image 1, the vector $\mathbf{l}_2 = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{u}_1 = [a, b, c]^{\mathsf{T}}$ defines the *epipolar line* $a u_2 + b v_2 + c = 0$ in image 2 along which the conjugate point must lie (parameterized by elevation $z$). **Epipolar rectification** applies a pair of $3\times 3$ projective homographies $(\mathbf{H}_1, \mathbf{H}_2)$ to warp both undistorted frame images onto a common virtual image plane parallel to the baseline $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{C}_2 - \mathbf{C}_1$, transforming the fundamental matrix into the canonical form

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{rect}} = [\mathbf{e}_1]_{\times} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad {\mathbf{u}_2'}^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{F}_{\text{rect}} \mathbf{u}_1' = v_1' - v_2' = 0$$

so that conjugate epipolar lines become strictly horizontal and aligned to identical image rows ($v_1' = v_2'$). The 3D reconstruction problem then reduces to estimating the 1D horizontal **disparity** $d(u', v') = u_1'(u', v') - u_2'(u', v')$.

**Quasi-Epipolar Rectification for Pushbroom Sensors:** For linear pushbroom scanners (Equation 8.9) and RPC cameras (Equation 8.12), the perspective center moves continuously with scanline $L$. Consequently, projecting a straight 3D optical ray from image 1 into image 2 traces a **non-linear hyperbola-like curve** rather than a straight line, and *no exact global epipolar homography exists* (Gupta & Hartley, 1997). Operational satellite photogrammetry pipelines solve this via two strategies:
1. **Piecewise Homography / Epipolar Resampling (De Franchis et al., 2014; MicMac / S2P):** Partition the scene into small tiles ($500 \times 500\text{ px}$) over which the pushbroom trajectory is locally linear, and compute local rectifying homographies.
2. **Map-Projection ("Ortho-Ready") Quasi-Epipolar Alignment (NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline, Beyer et al., 2018):** Project both raw images through their RPC/pushbroom models onto a smooth reference elevation surface (e.g., a coarse global DEM such as Copernicus GLO-30 filtered with a Gaussian kernel, or the mean scene ellipsoid height $h_{\text{off}}$), or apply a global affine epipolar alignment. This removes $> 95\%$ of topographic parallax and reduces cross-epipolar vertical disparity $\Delta v'$ to $< 1\text{–}2\text{ px}$, allowing 1D or narrow-band 2D stereo matchers to recover the residual disparity relative to the seed surface with minimal foreshortening distortion.

    A["Raw Stereo Pair (Frame or Pushbroom/RPC)"] --> B["Tie-Point Extraction and Bundle Adjustment (BA)"]
    B --> C["Epipolar Homography or Map-Projected Quasi-Epipolar Warp"]
    C --> D["Local Cost Volume C(u,v,d) (Census, NCC, or Deep Features)"]

#### Dense Stereo Matching: From Local Cost Volumes to SGM, MGM, and Deep Stereo

Given a rectified stereo pair $(I_1, I_2)$, dense stereo matching constructs a 3D **cost volume** $C(u, v, d)$ measuring the dissimilarity between a patch around pixel $\mathbf{p} = (u, v)$ in $I_1$ and its shifted counterpart $(u - d, v)$ in $I_2$ across candidate disparities $d \in [d_{\min}, d_{\max}]$.

1. **Matching Cost Functions:**
   * *Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) and Zero-Mean Sum of Absolute Differences (ZSAD):* Measure linear intensity similarity over a $W \times W$ window (typically $5\times 5$ to $9\times 9\text{ px}$). While effective on textured surfaces, large windows blur across depth discontinuities (fattening building edges and cliff faces).
   * *The Census Transform (Zabih & Woodfill, 1994):* Encodes each pixel $\mathbf{p}$ as a bit-string $\xi(\mathbf{p})$ of length $W^2 - 1$ by comparing the intensity $I(\mathbf{p})$ against every neighbor $\mathbf{q}$ in its window ($\text{bit} = 1$ if $I(\mathbf{q}) < I(\mathbf{p})$, else $0$). The matching cost $C(\mathbf{p}, d)$ is the **Hamming distance** (bitwise `XOR` popcount) between $\xi_1(\mathbf{p})$ and $\xi_2(\mathbf{p} - [d, 0]^{\mathsf{T}})$. Because the Census transform depends only on relative intensity *ordering*, it is invariant to any monotonic radiometric gain, bias, or gamma change between stereo views—making it the default local cost in modern satellite and airborne SGM pipelines.

2. **Semi-Global Matching (SGM; Hirschmüller, 2008):** Purely local "winner-take-all" matching ($d^* = \arg\min_d C(\mathbf{p}, d)$) is noisy in low-texture areas. Conversely, minimizing a full 2D Markov Random Field (MRF) smoothness energy across an entire image is NP-hard. Hirschmüller (2008) bridged this gap by formulating a global 2D energy functional $E(D)$ over the disparity image $D$:

   $$E(D) = \sum_{\mathbf{p}} \left( C(\mathbf{p}, D_{\mathbf{p}}) + \sum_{\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{p}}} P_1 \, \mathbb{I}\!\left[|D_{\mathbf{p}} - D_{\mathbf{q}}| = 1\right] + \sum_{\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{p}}} P_2 \, \mathbb{I}\!\left[|D_{\mathbf{p}} - D_{\mathbf{q}}| > 1\right] \right) \tag{8.20}$$

   where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, $P_1$ is a small penalty for a $1\text{-px}$ disparity change between adjacent pixels (permitting smooth, slanted terrain slopes without heavy penalty), and $P_2 > P_1$ is a larger penalty for disparity discontinuities $|D_{\mathbf{p}} - D_{\mathbf{q}}| > 1$ (preserving sharp vertical jumps at building walls, cliffs, and tree crowns). Typically, $P_2$ is adapted inversely to the local image intensity gradient, $P_2(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}) = \max\!\left(P_1, \, \frac{P_{2,0}}{|I_1(\mathbf{p}) - I_1(\mathbf{q})|}\right)$, since true depth discontinuities usually coincide with radiometric edges.

   SGM approximates the 2D minimization of Equation (8.20) by aggregating 1D dynamic programming path costs $L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}, d)$ independently along $N_r = 8$ or $16$ cardinal directions $\mathbf{r}$ radiating toward pixel $\mathbf{p}$:

   $$L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}, d) = C(\mathbf{p}, d) + \min\!\begin{cases} L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{r}, d) \\ L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{r}, d-1) + P_1 \\ L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{r}, d+1) + P_1 \\ \min_{k} L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{r}, k) + P_2 \end{cases} - \min_{k} L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{r}, k) \tag{8.21}$$

   The last subtracted term prevents numerical overflow along long scanlines without altering the argmin. The aggregated cost volume is $S(\mathbf{p}, d) = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} L_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{p}, d)$, from which the integer disparity is selected via $d_{\text{int}}^*(\mathbf{p}) = \arg\min_d S(\mathbf{p}, d)$, followed by a Left–Right consistency check ($|D_1(\mathbf{p}) - D_2(\mathbf{p} - [D_1(\mathbf{p}), 0]^{\mathsf{T}})| \le 1\text{ px}$) to mask occluded pixels.

3. **More Global Matching (MGM; Facciolo et al., 2015) and PatchMatch Stereo:** Because SGM's 1D rays $\mathbf{r}$ do not communicate laterally with adjacent rays until the final summation $S(\mathbf{p}, d)$, low-texture areas (snowfields, sand dunes, calm water, paved roads) often suffer from radial "streaking" or "star" artifacts propagating outward from high-contrast features. **More Global Matching (MGM)** eliminates streaking without increasing computational complexity ($\mathcal{O}(W H D)$) by averaging the recursive transition term in Equation (8.21) across *both* the predecessor pixel along direction $\mathbf{r}$ ($\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}$) and the predecessor perpendicular to $\mathbf{r}$ ($\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}^\perp$), turning each 1D ray pass into a true 2D quadrant sweep. Alternatively, **PatchMatch Stereo** (Bleyer et al., 2011) assigns each pixel a continuous 3D tangent plane $d(\mathbf{u}) = a_{\mathbf{p}} u + b_{\mathbf{p}} v + c_{\mathbf{p}}$ and alternates between randomized plane refinement and spatial propagation across neighbors, naturally modeling arbitrarily slanted terrain without integer disparity discretization.

4. **Deep Stereo Networks (`PSMNet`, `RAFT-Stereo`):** End-to-end deep stereo architectures replace hand-crafted Census/NCC descriptors with learned convolutional feature pyramids. Pyramid Stereo Matching Network (`PSMNet`; Chang & Chen, 2018) builds a 4D feature volume ($H \times W \times D \times 2F$) and regularizes it using stacked 3D hourglass convolutional networks, learning contextual priors to bridge specular or textureless roofs. Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms for Stereo (`RAFT-Stereo`; Lipson et al., 2021) avoids memory-heavy 3D convolutions by constructing lightweight multi-scale 1D correlation pyramids and iteratively updating a high-resolution disparity field via ConvGRU recurrent units. While deep stereo networks achieve state-of-the-art boundary sharpness on urban airborne and satellite benchmarks, practitioners must guard against *domain shift*: networks trained solely on terrestrial autonomous-driving datasets (e.g., KITTI, SceneFlow) can hallucinate planar structures when applied directly to nadir alpine, glacial, or benthic underwater imagery without domain-specific fine-tuning.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Integer Disparity Quantization and "Terracing / Stair-Stepping" Artifacts:** Evaluated at integer pixel shifts $d \in \mathbb{Z}$, a discrete cost volume quantizes terrain elevation into discrete vertical steps of height $\Delta z_{\text{step}} = \left(\frac{B}{H}\right)^{-1} \mathrm{GSD}$ (e.g., $1.25\text{ m}$ steps for $\mathrm{GSD} = 0.5\text{ m}$ and $B/H = 0.4$). On gentle slopes, integer disparities produce severe artificial contour **terracing (stair-stepping)** that completely corrupts first- and second-order terrain derivatives (slope $|\nabla z|$ and curvature $\nabla^2 z$, Chapter 17), causing false contour lines where $\text{slope} = 0$ on flats and spikes at step risers. Fitting a simple 3-point parabola through $S(\mathbf{p}, d^* - 1), S(\mathbf{p}, d^*), S(\mathbf{p}, d^* + 1)$ reduces quantization but still suffers from **pixel-locking bias** (which systematically pulls fractional disparities toward integer or half-integer values). High-precision scientific pipelines (such as NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline's `stereo_rfne` stage) follow integer SGM/MGM initialization with continuous 2D affine Lucas–Kanade (`--subpixel-mode 3`) or affine-adaptive Bayes Expectation-Maximization (EM, `--subpixel-mode 2`) sub-pixel image registration, achieving bias-free disparity precision down to $\sigma_{p,\text{px}} \approx 0.05\text{–}0.10\text{ px}$.

In operational satellite photogrammetry using the open-source NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP; originated at NASA Ames in 1996 and built atop the VisionWorkbench library introduced in 2006; Beyer et al., 2018), bias-compensated RPC bundle adjustment, seed-DEM quasi-epipolar orthorectification, MGM census matching, and affine-adaptive sub-pixel refinement are chained together as follows:

```bash
# 1. Bias-compensated RPC bundle adjustment in image space
bundle_adjust left.ntf right.ntf left.xml right.xml -o ba/run

# 2. Map-project both images onto a smooth seed DEM (quasi-epipolar alignment)
mapproject --tr 0.50 seed_glo30_smooth.tif left.ntf  left.xml  left_map.tif  --bundle-adjust-prefix ba/run
mapproject --tr 0.50 seed_glo30_smooth.tif right.ntf right.xml right_map.tif --bundle-adjust-prefix ba/run

# 3. Dense stereo with MGM (--stereo-algorithm asp_mgm) and Bayes EM sub-pixel refinement (--subpixel-mode 2)
parallel_stereo --stereo-algorithm asp_mgm --subpixel-mode 2 \
  --bundle-adjust-prefix ba/run \
  left_map.tif right_map.tif left.xml right.xml stereo/out seed_glo30_smooth.tif

# 4. Convert triangulated 3D point cloud (PC.tif) to a gridded GeoTIFF DSM and ray-intersection error map
point2dem --tr 1.0 --errorimage stereo/out-PC.tif -o stereo/out
```

> [!TIP]
> **Pre-Rectifying with a Seed DEM (`mapproject`):** When processing high-relief satellite stereo (e.g., WorldView or Pléiades over steep mountains) in NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP), never run SGM/MGM directly on raw unprojected images. Instead, first orthorectify both the left and right images onto a smooth coarse reference DEM (e.g., Copernicus GLO-30 smoothed with a $100\text{ m}$ Gaussian filter) using `mapproject`. This removes the vast majority of terrain-induced perspective foreshortening and non-linear pushbroom epipolar curvature before correlation, narrowing the required disparity search range $[d_{\min}, d_{\max}]$ by an order of magnitude and dramatically reducing blunders and voids on steep slopes.
